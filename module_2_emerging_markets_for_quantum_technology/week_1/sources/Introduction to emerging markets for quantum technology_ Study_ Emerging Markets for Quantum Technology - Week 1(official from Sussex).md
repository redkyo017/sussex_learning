1.1 Introduction to emerging markets for quantum technology

BACK TO TOP

This week’s interactive material provides a foundation for the rest of the Emerging Markets for Quantum Technology module. In this week, we will define key terms of quantum innovation and show how the various parts of the module build on one another to provide a comprehensive overview of emerging markets for quantum technologies. In this module, we are going to focus on patterns of innovation – understood in terms of successful commercialisation – and how technical, market and managerial contingencies influence success. Before you start exploring the material, take a minute to think about the different kinds of quantum technologies that exist, whether they have been successfully commercialised, and the different barriers to commercial success they face.

What are quantum technologies?

Quantum technology is a field of science and engineering that seeks to understand and utilise the principles of quantum mechanics (the branch of physics that describes the behaviour of matter and energy on sub-microscopic scales). It encompasses a wide range of applications, including quantum computing, quantum communication, quantum

sensors, quantum cryptography, quantum imaging and quantum simulation \[1\].

Quantum technologies attempt to use quantum phenomena such as entanglement to generate new properties or phenomena. These are used to deliver new technical functions that harness these properties. Quantum technologies are used to process and transmit information more efficiently and securely than classical technologies. For example, quantum computers use qubits to perform calculations faster than classical computers. Quantum cryptography provides new secure communication channels, and

quantum sensors can detect and measure incredibly small signals \[1\].

The development of quantum technologies can be broken down into two distinct ‘waves’:

Quantum technology wave 1.0: Technologies are reliant on quantum effects such

as spin, Q-tunnelling or quantised energy. Quantum 1.0 technologies include lasers, transistors, semiconductor devices and MRI scanners.

Quantum technology wave 2.0: Technologies exploit, create, manipulate and read

out quantum states of matter and phenomena such as superposition and correlation.

(gravity, magnetic fields), communication and many more \[2\].

As we go through this module, we will look at many of these different quantum technologies in detail. Our focus will be on the quantum technology wave 2.0.

In this module, we primarily explore the following quantum technologies in more detail:

Quantum computing: Has the potential to solve complex problems that are currently

beyond the reasonable scope of classical computers. Potential industry users include finance, logistics and drug discovery (where the technology could be used to improve optimisation, simulations, and decision making).

Quantum communication: Has the potential to provide improved secure

communication channels. Potential customers include the military, government agencies, and the financial services sector.

Quantum sensors: Can be used to detect and measure incredibly weak signals.

Potential customers can be found in the oil and gas industries, as well as around environmental monitoring and medical diagnostics.

Quantum cryptography: Enables secure encryption that is potentially impossible to

crack. Potential customers might be found in the finance and healthcare sectors, as well as the military and other government agencies.

Quantum materials: Explores unique properties to create new materials with novel

properties, such as superconductivity, that have a wide range of potential industrial applications, including energy, electronics and transport.

1.2 Understanding quantum innovation

These areas of technology are very diverse and are at different stages of commercialisation. As such, this module adopts a ‘contingency approach’ to understanding innovation, commercialisation and market dynamics. This ‘contingency approach’ involves understanding and analysing patterns of innovation, commercialisation and diffusion using the same generic process, but adapting that process to the specific features of different technologies and markets. The managerial challenge to drive commercial success, therefore, involves the configuration of that same generic process.

Important definitions for innovation

To have a shared language when we are discussing the commercialisation of quantum innovations, we need some initial shared definitions. These will help ensure we understand each other and provide important signals about what to be concerned about. Some key terms we will be using include:

Invention: The first time someone comes up with an idea or a prototype for a new or

improved product, process, service or function.

Innovation: The first successful commercialisation of an invention or new idea. Diffusion: The subsequent further adoption and adaptation of an innovation \[4\].

These definitions have important consequences

Invention is defined as an event. It happens at a discrete point in time. It is typically an individual activity, although numerous people may be involved in generating a prototype.

Innovation is defined in terms of successful commercialisation. It is a process, rather than an event. The focus on successful commercialisation means we must consider not just technical success, but also success in the marketplace. This typically involves a firm making a commercial profit from the innovation. This, in turn, typically involves the innovation generating value for customers that they will be willing to pay for. However, there are examples of non-commercial innovation – for example, in non-profit settings such as hospitals. We will explore these later in the module.

Diffusion is also a process. It involves the adoption of the innovation by a growing range of customers and users. They will often adapt the innovation to their own settings. It is therefore very misleading to think that innovation is an event that stops when the product enters the market. Typically, we see extensive improvements brought about through incremental innovations and modifications by users.

1.3 Key features of innovation

Features of innovation

#### Innovations can have several features; they can be:

new to the world new to a country new to a firm.

Some cultures have a view of innovation that unless an innovation is new to the world, it’s not really an innovation. Other cultures regard importing technologies from around the world as key, and many nations have this as a vital part of their innovation policies.

Incremental and radical innovations

Innovations differ by degree from incremental to radical. Radical innovation refers to the development of new products, services or technologies that fundamentally disrupt and transform existing markets or industries. They typically involve a significant departure from existing products, services or technologies. As a result, they will typically have a much higher degree of uncertainty, reflecting their connection to new breakthrough discoveries, new business models, or new, innovative applications.

Incremental innovation refers to improvement to existing products, services or technologies through small, gradual changes. It typically involves making existing products, services or business models better, faster, cheaper or more convenient. As it builds on existing innovations, it is typically less risky and less costly than radical innovation. The impact of incremental innovation over many years, or decades, can have a very large, long-term impact.

Radical and incremental innovation is a continuum. They should not be seen as distinct. Radical changes are often very primitive when they are first introduced and require extensive incremental modifications, over many years, to improve performance and bring

down costs \[5\].

Change necessary for innovation

Innovation is not just technological; it also includes organisational, business model and market change. Organisational innovation involves new organisational practices,

employees are managed, and how products and services are developed and delivered. This is important to firms as it improves efficiency, reduces costs, increases productivity, enhances customer satisfaction, and creates new opportunities for growth and profitability. Just as there is radical and incremental technical innovation, organisational innovation can involve both radical changes and continuous, incremental improvements.

Types of innovation

Innovation can also differ by type: product innovation, process innovation, position innovation and paradigm innovation.

Product innovation

This is about changes in the things and services that an organisation offers that better meet the needs of its customers. In quantum technologies, product innovation might involve the development of new quantum computers, sensors or communication systems. For example, IBM’s quantum computer is a product innovation that is designed to perform complex calculations using quantum mechanics.

Process innovation

This refers to changes in how the products and services firms provide are created and delivered, such as improving how quantum computers are manufactured or programmed. For example, Rigetti Computing has developed a process innovation called hybrid quantum computing, which combines classical and quantum computing to improve performance and uses a cloud platform

called Forrest to help programmers write quantum algorithms \[6\].

Position innovation

This involves changes in the company’s positioning in the market, such as targeting new markets or developing new partnerships. Quantum computing companies might partner with traditional computing companies to develop hybrid systems that exploit both quantum and classical computing environments to address new problems and solve problems in new markets.

A quantum computing company might go from providing products, to providing services, to moving upstream or downstream to exploit that capability and make profits in entirely new markets.

Key features of innovation

The process of innovation is not easy, as it typically requires matching a technology to a market need. Given the focus on ‘successful commercialisation’, this matching needs to be undertaken in a way that generates value for customers that they will be willing to pay the focal firm for, so that they generate sufficient revenue to cover their costs and make a profit.

Given this difficulty, the process of innovation is inherently uncertain and can take many years (sometimes decades). Innovations are often very primitive when they are first developed and have unclear potential. Only after an extensive period of experimentation and incremental improvement are the problems solved and their performance improved enough to be launched successfully.

Innovations sometimes require complementary innovations. These may come from outside the innovating organisation. Natural gas, for example, was a waste product until the discovery of high-pressure piping. This dependence on complementary innovations, and especially on complementary innovations from other organisations (which may be in entirely different industries), makes predicting the eventual use and value of innovations even more difficult.

Innovation is not only research and development (R&D) based. Many people who do not understand innovation assume it comes mainly from R&D. In fact, in most developed nations, R&D is less important as a source of innovation than innovations that come from suppliers and customers. R&D is vital in some sectors, such as chemicals, pharmaceuticals and electronics – and, as we shall see, quantum – but is less important in other sectors. High-tech manufacturing only makes up about 3% of the UK economy. It is therefore important to consider the non-R&D intensive forms of innovation in the other 97% of the economy. Innovation can be:

science-based and draw on internal R&D and links to universities and research (for example, drugs) scale-intensive and draw heavily on internal production engineering to produce standardised products at scale (for example, steel) design-intensive and draw on creative solutions, designed for specific clients’ needs project-based and involve the co-ordination and integration of systems for clients software/digital and be based on the production of software systems.

sectors. Yoghurt production, for example, used to be very low-tech, but now draws much

It is important to note that individual organisations may draw on many different types of innovation. Pharmaceutical firms, for example, do R&D, but also draw on production engineering to produce drugs and invest heavily in projects (for example, to build new

1.4 Complementary assets

What are ‘complementary assets’?

A very important idea, which will be a big part of this module, emerges from the definition of innovation. Innovation is defined in terms of successful commercialisation. This, in turn, involves not just creating value, but also having a way of capturing that value. People who misunderstand innovation, by reducing it to R&D or invention (or new product development), often fail to appreciate the importance of having an ability to capture value. The things that enable firms to capture value are called ‘complementary assets’. They are called complementary because they complement the assets that firms use to create value. If firms can only create value, but can’t capture some of the profits, they will rapidly exit the market. Hence, complementary assets are an essential part of our intellectual toolbox, which we will use to understand emerging quantum technology markets.

How to achieve successful commercialisation

Successful commercialisation involves both creating and capturing value. Most management training focuses on the first of these and overlooks the second, but capturing value is vital to success.

To help think strategically about value capture, we draw on a framework developed by

David Teece \[9\]. He noted in the 1980s that often the original innovators do not benefit

from their work. For example, Electric and Music Industries (EMI) invented the CT

scanner, but its competitors eventually took over its markets \[10\]. EMI lacked the

essential assets needed to succeed in the market. A key lesson from Teece’s work is that being a good inventor isn’t enough to be a good innovator. You need to capture value

differences between firms that profit from their innovations and firms that innovate and

then leave the market \[11\]. Teece suggests that firms need assets to create value and

‘complementary’ assets that allow them to capture value and prevent their competitors profiting.

Types of complementary assets

Complementary assets can be broken down into eight categories.

Select each heading to learn more.

Intellectual property rights

These can involve patents, trademarks and copyright that provide a framework for assigning ownership to an innovation. They can protect a firm’s core technology or product from imitation and infringement by competitors. For example, IBM has 1000+ patents related to quantum technologies, which

provide a legal monopoly on these technologies for a limited period \[12\].

Brands, reputations and relationships with customers

These also help firms capture value. They signal to customers the quality of a firm’s technologies and products and reduce the costs of working with customers.

Tacit knowledge

This knowledge is hard to copy and write down and is typically transferred through face-to-face interactions. It can be very difficult to transfer and this makes it very difficult for firms to copy technology quickly.

Trade secrets or technical complexity

If the knowledge behind a technology can be kept secret, and/or is so complex that it is distributed among many people and is hard to access, then it can be difficult for firms to copy a technology. For example, with many quantum technologies, the production processes are very complex and can be extremely difficult and expensive to replicate. The process technology used in the production of quantum technology is not accessed when the products are bought. So, competitor firms will struggle to replicate the technology.

Lead time advantages

Even if it is possible to replicate a technology, firms can capture more value if they can speed up their innovation and production processes. Therefore, they get new products to market before competitors can copy their current products.

Global marketing networks

These networks are vital for firms to sell internationally and firms that lack them can struggle to fully commercialise their products. Small quantum technology firms, for example, may struggle to sell in international markets.

Co-specialised assets

These involve things like providing after-sales support, training, maintenance and finance for customers. For example, a firm selling advanced quantum computers that can provide maintenance and after-sales support may be preferred compared to a firm offering a superior product that was more difficult to maintain.

The economic systems

In a capitalist system, a lot of innovative activity happens within firms, and is co-ordinated by markets. We will not be covering much economic theory in this module, but it is useful to know a little about the frameworks that economists use.

Opportunity cost

Economists often think their most important contribution is the idea of opportunity cost. This is the idea that the true cost of something is the next-best alternative you give up to get it.

Most people, for example, just think in terms of costs. So, when they go to university to do an undergraduate degree, they would only consider the price they pay for their fees as the degree’s costs. However, economists also include the costs of foregone income, as you must give up work to study. So, a degree that costs £12,000 might cost you much more once you add in the lost income. Importantly, economists wouldn’t include the costs of accommodation. This is a ‘sunk cost’, as you would have to pay it no matter where you lived. When you compare the consequences of your choices with the next best choice, you can see that you just ignore sunk costs – you can’t change them, so you will get them anyway.

Cost benefit principle

This involves looking at all the costs and all the benefits of your options and only pursuing options where benefits exceed costs. You should evaluate based on your willingness to pay. In theory, both sellers and buyers will benefit if they follow this principle.

Marginal costs and benefits

Economists look at the costs and benefits of incremental changes, such as employing one more worker, eating one more ice-cream or making one more computer. They suggest that you should select one more if the benefits exceed the costs and then repeat the process. However, each extra selection is likely to have a decreasing benefit. Supply and demand curves

We would expect that as the price of goods goes up, more suppliers would be willing to sell. Similarly, as the price increases, the number of people willing to buy will decline. We can use these ideas to draw supply and demand curves that link prices with quantities.

A demand curve depicts the amount of a product that people want at different prices. Meanwhile, a supply curve depicts the amount of a product that sellers will supply at different prices. Given the focus on marginal benefits, we would expect a free market to lead to a convergence of supply and demand. Eventually, all the people willing to buy will transact with all the people willing to sell at a given price, until a point is reached called the equilibrium price. This is where the quantity demanded is equal to the quantity

supplied (for instance, gains from trade reduced to zero) \[13\].

The theory of production

These ideas can be combined into the core explanation of how the market system works. The theory of production deals with how firms use resources (inputs, such as labour, capital and raw materials) to produce goods and services. Firms aim to maximise profits and do so by selecting the optimal combination of inputs that will produce the desired level of output. This is known as the production function, which shows the maximum output that can be produced with any given set of inputs.

The theory of exchange

The theory of exchange focuses on how goods and services are traded between individuals or firms using the price mechanism. The theory explains how markets reach a state of equilibrium where supply and demand are in balance, and this point determines the price at which goods and services are bought and sold. The theory implies that in a market system, individuals will pursue their self-interest and achieve the best ‘deal’ in selling their labour and purchasing goods and services through a system of ‘voluntary exchange’ (meaning that they are not forced to buy or sell goods or services). Voluntary exchange continues until individuals cannot make an exchange that they find worthwhile – this is called the Pareto equilibrium and is the point where it is not possible for any individual to achieve a higher level of satisfaction without taking something away from

other individuals and reducing their satisfaction \[14\].

Competitive equilibria theory

Both theories feed into the theory of competitive equilibria, which describes how markets collectively reach a state of equilibrium where supply and demand are balanced. The theory assumes that firms maximise their profits (by choosing the optimal combination of inputs) and that individuals maximise their utility (by choosing the optimal combination of goods and services to consume). In a competitive market, firms compete to produce goods and services at the lowest cost, and individuals compete to buy goods and services at the lowest price. The interactions of these two ‘markets’ generates the overall distribution of prices in the economy, and specifically the ‘market-clearing price’ where

supply and demand are in balance \[15\].

You do not need to understand the details of these theories. All you need to know is that these theories provide a basis for understanding the entry of new firms into markets, and

provide the shared basis that policy makers use to understand the effects of government policies on markets, and the consequences of market failures, such as monopolies or externalities. It is important to note that economists don’t believe that their models are very good approximations of all markets. Most economic research looks at various deviations from the theory (known as market failures) and how the misallocations that they generate can be addressed.

Understanding market dynamics

The theory of production implies that a market system will generate new entry into the market of new competitors whenever there are opportunities for ‘above normal’ profits. The resulting increased competition assures the greatest possible efficiency in the production of goods and services, as any inefficiency will be punished by loss of market share and, ultimately, bankruptcy.

‘Normal profits’ are those required to attract investment. If profits are higher, people can borrow money and capture them. It is assumed that this process of market entry will effectively prevent the accumulation of market power (ability to raise prices significantly above the costs of production) and other sources of inefficiency (such as poor management). Risky activities, like innovation, require high levels of market growth or profits to attract investment.

Imperfect competition

Within this theory there is a ‘strong assumption’ that all surviving producers (firms) will have equivalent knowledge of production possibilities and all consumers have complete knowledge of their choices. It’s important to note that this is a theory for understanding the allocation of existing resources. It is not necessarily a good theory for understanding where those resources came from. Economists initially saw innovation as something that comes from outside their analytic system. Today, economists understand innovation as an investment – like making an investment in new equipment or in training of individuals to perform new jobs. This can be used to help explain the ‘take up’ of innovation – whether a firm will decide to take up an innovation that works for some other firm can be based on enough information to make a good decision.

However, this does not explain the initial innovation. For one thing, competitive firms in a competitive market do not have any ‘extra’ money to invest in the ‘gamble’ involved in making innovations. To deal with innovation, economists must move towards thinking about imperfect markets.

The market for technology is imperfect because it is difficult to understand the impact of technology, and firms that access technology may develop new, improved versions. Given these market imperfections, governments have introduced policies to improve markets. For example, patents allow firms to capture more value from investing in R&D and so improve the incentives to invest. Governments can also socialise some of the risks that investors face in funding innovation.

Given the imperfections in markets for innovation, and its inherent uncertainties, investment in innovation is often driven by resolution, and innovators and entrepreneurs who have atypical views about their potential for success. Often, these views are wildly overly optimistic. So, the private returns may be negative. However, a small number of successes can generate significant benefits for the economy.

Schumpeter was an economist who argued that entrepreneurs played a key role in developing new technology within the economy. He suggested that the introduction of innovations generated ‘creative destruction’, where the foundations of existing industries were disrupted, and new industries emerged. In his first theories, he stated that small ventures drove innovation. In his later theories, he argued that innovation was too complex for small firms and was now being undertaken in large firms with centralised R&D labs. Today we see a balance between the two, and often we see large firms using

networks of small firms to help with their innovation \[16\].

Schumpeter’s theory of creative destruction highlights how innovation drives radical changes in the economy. He suggested that it drives economic cycles. Initial innovations drive revolutions, and these eventually calm down, creating the conditions where new innovations might emerge. Schumpeter argued that these radical changes are driven by changes in the profits that are available. These profits from innovation decline as new entrants enter the market and compete to drive down prices and profits.
