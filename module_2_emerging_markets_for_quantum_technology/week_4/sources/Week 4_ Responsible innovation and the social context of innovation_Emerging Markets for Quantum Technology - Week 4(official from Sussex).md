4.1 Responsible innovation governance

Let us begin by discussing the notion of ‘responsible innovation’. Science produces

knowledge as well as economic and social value, but it also raises questions, dilemmas and unintended, sometimes even undesirable, impacts.

Historically, these unintended consequences have normally been managed after products have already emerged and those impacts have begun to be felt. This can be a problematic approach if the negative outcomes are very significant and long term. For complex, uncertain technologies that interact in a dynamic and global fashion and often have inter-generational impacts, such risk-based approaches are not always useful.

An alternative approach to reducing risk is to try to understand the likely impacts before negative events happen. This ‘precautionary’ approach helps to ensure that any problems – such as side effects of new drugs or the causes of plane crashes – are learned from and not repeated.

This raises the question about how to govern innovation that is subject to considerable uncertainty. Furthermore, it is necessary to understand potential ignorance about negative impacts and ambiguity about the social distribution of how those differing impacts might be valued.

Codes of conduct and ethical reviews will often ask scientists and researchers to consider potential impacts, but it is not clear whether this generates effective longer-term stewardship.

Responsible innovation aims to expand beyond these approaches to include broader reflection and deliberation on the purposes of innovation. Why do it? We should ask not only what the benefits will be, but also who will benefit.

This typically includes deeper reflection on how priorities should be (democratically) defined and by whom.

Reflecting on purposes

Reflection on the purposes of innovation means that responsible innovation must take into consideration what we do and do not want innovation to achieve.

The social contract for innovation

There has been (since the 1640s) a social contract between science and society in which society provides funding, support and a degree of freedom to researchers in return for an expectation that they will generate knowledge and other economically and socially valuable outputs.

In the early 20th century, science was very affordable and scientists were largely left unregulated.

As science became more expensive and has had a larger and higher impact on our

considerable amounts of GDP. This has had a very significant impact on the economy,

society and therefore on politics, to the point where the social contract has changed \[1\].

There is now much more attention given to external evaluation and auditing of research to ensure that the most valuable work is done. This, in turn, has led to greater attempts

to manage and direct science towards more beneficial outcomes and away from

negative effects.

As the impact of science and research has continued to increase, the governance of this social contract has continued to change. There is now greater acknowledgement of the negative impacts of science and technology on society and the environment. In the past, there was the widespread assumption that the impact of technology was positive; however, this is no longer the case. Moreover, we now recognise that the balance of positive and negative impacts is not evenly distributed across society, between generations and around the world.

Rachel Carson’s Silent Spring (1962) \[2\] helped to highlight the complex and long-term

impacts of chemicals, as she drew attention to pesticides that were accumulating in the shells of birds’ eggs, which was weakening them, and leading to a new phenomenon of nature without birdsong.

More recently, there has been an increasing amount of attention paid to second-order effects. This is where the impacts of new science and technology continue along complex, interacting chains of causation: for example, social media’s impact on the mental health of young people.

The extensive evidence on negative impacts of innovation starts to raise questions about the extent to which the social contract is being upheld. A lot of research now is funded to deal with the negative impacts of previous investments in science and technology.

Although the overall balance of the social contract continues to be positive – science continues to provide valuable knowledge and technologies that enhance health, industrial productivity and the quality of life – there are now enough negative effects that it can be assumed that they are not atypical. This raises the question of how innovation should be governed to reduce these negative impacts.

Governing negative impacts – externalities

For some negative impacts, in particular ones that can be readily identified and costed, markets can work well in pricing the costs. But when these costs are hard to measure, understand and capture in terms of price, especially when the costs and benefits fall upon very different groups of people, markets don’t work well. For example, economists estimate that the social costs of the air pollution externalities of using coal to produce energy are so high that, were they to be included, the entire industry would be commercially unviable. The costs of coal substantially exceed the benefits.

In such circumstances, the main way in which technology is governed is through legal authorisation and control, which is often based on probabilistic risk assessments.

These governance methods can be enhanced through adaptive learning, where institutions gather and analyse data to find better ways to manage the costs and benefits.

Governing negative impacts – second order

Another important governance method involves liability regimes. In some high-risk sectors, such as drug discovery, this can involve extensive testing and production of data about risks, before products are authorised to go on the market.

This pre-authorisation data normally only addresses a limited number of issues. In the pharmaceutical industry, for example, product safety is the main priority. Rarely do they look at second order impacts – for example, the impact of genetically modified seeds on the structure of the global industry supplying seeds to farmers.

This kind of governance typically requires knowledge about negative (and positive) impacts, the causal pathways that drive change and knowledge about how they might be influenced. With many new technologies, these may be very difficult to assess. Impacts may be long term, interactive, unevenly distributed and cumulative over time.

This can lead to long time delays between innovation, understanding the negative impacts thereof, and then the development and implementation of policies to address these costs.

The Collingridge dilemma

David Collingridge \[3\] highlighted an important dilemma caused by these complex

temporal and organisational chains of causality linking innovation to negative outcomes.

Because it takes time for negative effects to be noticed and addressed, by the time we understand those negative impacts, the technology itself may be well established and adapted to other technologies and ways of working, which makes it much harder to change.

Sometimes the technology may be so embedded in the economy and society that it becomes locked in and extremely difficult to change.

For example, it took decades for scientists to understand the health impacts of lead in petrol, or the environmental impact of CFCs on the ozone layer. By the time this was understood, the technologies were well established, with significant markets and powerful economic actors supporting them. Changing the regimes around the use of CFCs and phasing out lead in petrol, therefore, took much longer and was more difficult than it would have been had these costs been known earlier.

The Collingridge dilemma: Lock-in

With some technologies, such as carbon intensive forms of energy, the technologies are so embedded in wider socio-technical systems that they can be very difficult to change. Other systems, such as the patent system, are such that now we know their negative impacts better. We would probably not have introduced them in their current form, but it is just too complex to replace them with a better system.

The classic example of this kind of lock-in is the QWERTY keyboard brought to attention

by the economist Paul David \[4\]. It was originally introduced to slow down typing when

typewriters were mechanical and would seize up if letters were typed too fast. When the technology changed and we moved to personal computers rather than mechanical typewriters, the keyboard stayed the same because so many people had learned to use it. This technology remains with us, even though other, much faster designs are possible. The network of people with QWERTY specific skills keeps us locked into an inferior

technical option \[5\].

The Collingridge dilemma: Dynamics

The second aspect of the Collingridge dilemma of governing emerging technologies relates to changes in technologies over time. In the earliest stages of innovation, we have the most opportunity to influence the development of future products and services.

However, this is precisely the time where we have the most limited knowledge and

when uncertainty is at its peak.

If we wait too long, we can increase the risks of lock-in and see technologies develop along suboptimal pathways.

An important feature of the early stages of technology is that many technologies are

‘promissory’.

This means that, at an early stage, we may think that the technology will work in a certain way, and we can use this assumption to try to access and coordinate resources. However, it is likely that problems will emerge, new options might open up and competing technologies and ways of doing things may be developed. So often we find that technologies do not live up to their initial promise, or only do so after many years.

The uncertainty about technology can lead to alarmist views that also end up being unrealistic. We often find that people, especially academics, take the promises of the proponents of technology as given and accept at face value their assurance of

against them.

This can be seen in the history of nanotechnology \[6\], \[7\], where proponents of this

technology hyped its potential, and opponents believed them and campaigned against the risks of ‘grey goo’ taking over the world. Similar things can be seen today with

artificial intelligence (AI) \[8\].

Under conditions of extreme technical uncertainty at the early stages of the development of emerging technologies, there is often a ready market for ‘experts’ who are prepared to advance hype or predict disaster with extreme confidence. This makes governance of technology at its early stages more complex, as policymakers and managers must sift through the hype and doom.

Managing uncertainty

Given these uncertainties, there is an element of luck about the impact of technology for firms and society. They may have very positive impacts, or they may generate significant harms and risks. Public dialogue exercises consistently show that the public would like researchers to take more responsibility for managing the outcomes that their work generates.

Managing uncertainty – taking responsibility

If researchers know that there may be negative impacts, they need to take responsibility for ensuring that those risks are well managed.

4.3 Challenges of responsibility

What does it mean to be responsible? Responsibility has two dimensions that are particularly relevant to innovation:

Care – being concerned about others and the outcomes for them. Responsiveness – continuously looking to discover and act on decisions that have

been made erroneously:

responsiveness requires being adaptive and responding actively to changes responsiveness also requires deliberation with stakeholders to understand their values, views and aims.

With technologies, these dimensions of responsibility need to be extended. The

traditional focus of responsibility was on those people close to us for whom we are responsible. With technology, the negative impacts may extend globally and affect people many generations into the future. Nuclear waste sites, for example, may be dangerous for hundreds of thousands of years.

Following Owen et al, we can define responsible innovation as: ‘a collective commitment of care for the future through responsive stewardship of science and innovation in the

present’ \[9\].

Challenges of responsibility

The literature on responsible innovation typically stresses discussions about what futures we collectively want our investments in science and innovation to generate.

This is typically a more equal, just and sustainable society that is more ethically acceptable to people. These issues create a strong link between responsible innovation and politics.

Often, the discussions of politics in the responsible innovation literature are not very sophisticated, and they sometimes involve calls for ‘more democratic decision-making’ without explaining what they mean by democratic. One might be critical and ask whether the underlying assumption that ‘having more people involved’ in participating and reflecting on ends is always going to lead to better outcomes. There is rarely much

discussion of the actual institutions that are involved in democratic deliberation and the

specific problems that they face.

The results of engagement exercises are often very difficult to integrate and sometimes end up with ‘lowest common denominator’ outputs. These can end up being platitudes without much practical currency.

Finally, the process of conducting public engagement exercises has a big influence on the outcomes and, in particular, on how those outcomes are communicated to actors with influence. There is an old Russian joke that, in a democracy, power doesn’t land with the voters, but with the people who count the votes. Many people who push for public deliberation – often for very legitimate reasons that are widely shared – are not neutral actors.

Challenges of responsibility – expertise

A significant issue with the politics of regulating technology is the role of experts. Often, technical issues have a major impact on potential outcomes and a degree of expertise is often required to be actively involved in debates and be wellinformed.

This does not mean that people who lack that expertise either have no valuable inputs or that they cannot quickly learn the key issues.

However, it does raise concerns. In a liberal democracy the underlying idea is that people engage as equals and come to a collective decision on future actions that are binding, even on those who disagreed with it.

Having experts, who have a degree of specialised knowledge that is unavailable to others, seems to conflict with the ‘rough equality’ that is needed for democratic decision-making. Experts seem to have something that makes them ‘better positioned’ than others.

Secondly, experts are often supported by the government – through grants, financial support of other kinds, or through access to decision-making bodies – which means that the state is not neutral towards their views. In a liberal democracy, neutrality towards endpoints is often seen as a precondition of effective deliberation.

These issues are important because experts often do not have political views that map closely to the public. They are often from particular, and sometimes atypical, demographics, and often have atypical levels of social capital.

not on top’. Their views should feed into decision-making, but not determine outcomes.

However, the importance of expertise to many decisions around technical issues often means that decisions are not made in classical ‘town hall’ meetings. Instead, committees and other bodies made up of experts are often involved. Being part of a committee and coming up with collective decisions, require that some issues are ‘taken for granted’, so that key issues of contention can be discussed. Importantly, within political theory, it is recognised that deciding that something isn’t a political question is itself a political question. Good committees will often have open deliberation about these foundational, already accepted assumptions.

Moreover, expertise is largely international – for example, experts on quantum sensing in Germany are likely to also be experts in the UK. Since international networks of expertise are very common, this raises concerns about the scope of deliberation. Are decisions in one nation influencing and being influenced by decisions in others? Responsible innovation approaches seek to extend deliberation beyond local communities and consider inter-generational impacts. However, many of the bodies that make decisions have very specific and geographically confined foci.

Responsible innovation approaches, therefore, need to extend beyond individual responsibility towards engaging with a more institutional and institutionalised process of governance.

This raises an interesting paradox – do constant calls for decision-making to be opened up and made more democratic negate the processes that enable some fundamental concepts to be taken for granted and, therefore, preclude effective decision-making?

This potential problem can be seen clearly in debates about the use of quantum

technologies for security related issues: for example, in enabling the safe

transfer of information or for detecting submarines or other military technologies. In cases such as these, there are issues of national secrecy among many other barriers to sharing information, even if shared military protection was a desirable outcome. Under such circumstances, asking for more opening up of deliberation is unrealistic and other forms of oversight might be needed.

Challenges of responsibility – scope

Many of the positive and negative impacts of innovations are felt far from their original settings.

The economic historian Nathan Rosenberg, for example, identified that one of the biggest economic impacts of innovations in cotton production in the industrial

revolution was through its impact on health \[11\]. Before the production of cheap

cotton, English workers often wore woollen underclothes, which they might be sewn into at the start of winter. These were breeding grounds for lice and other hygiene problems. Cheap cotton enabled workers to have multiple underclothes, which they could keep much cleaner, leading to better health outcomes.

As a negative counterpoint, the introduction of the internet has had a negative impact on the ability of groups to diffuse and sometimes commercialise misinformation. This has increased political polarisation and, in some instances, led to political violence.

These longer chains of causal impacts raise questions about the scope of responsible innovation and responsibility. Are the inventors of the internet responsible for conspiracy theorists? Where does impact and responsibility start and end?

Key features of responsible innovation

Quantum technologies have several significant potential economic and social impacts.

outcomes of innovation are positive. Key features of responsible innovation include the following:

Anticipatory – intelligently exploring potential intended and unintended economic, social and environmental outcomes.

Reflective – thoughtfully considering the purposes and ends to which technologies might be used and the extent and limitations of knowledge about processes and outcomes.

Deliberative – opening up decision-making to wide collective deliberation about the aims, issues, dilemmas and visions that arise. Incorporating a wide range of views helps the process to explore assumptions and values.

Responsive – continuously seeking to influence the direction of innovation and ensure that the benefits are realised.

This week, we learned that responsible innovation allows researchers to explore how they want technology to develop, ideally, to enable a wide range of views and concerns to influence outcomes through a process of structured deliberation.

Ongoing deliberation helps to avoid the problems posed by lock-in and prevents situations where society ends up with suboptimal outcomes from technological choices.

Undertaking responsible innovation requires pragmatic and proportional responses to the concerns that are raised. The extent of the potential positive and negative impacts will influence how extensive the process is and who is involved. For many innovations, it may only require a continuous assessment of potential negative outcomes and reflection on the values that inform judgements. For others, particularly ones with long-term and global impacts, a much wider consultation process is needed.
