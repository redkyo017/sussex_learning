# Quantum Innovation Strategies and Value Capture Frameworks (week 5 -3)

These notes provide a comprehensive synthesis of innovation sources, strategic frameworks, and the application of business models to the quantum technology (QT) sector, as presented in the module resources.

### \*\*1. Sources of Innovative Ideas\*\*
Innovation is rarely a linear progression but rather a "coupling process" that links technical capabilities with market needs \[1, 2\].
\*   \*\*Knowledge Push and Need Pull:\*\* Ideas originate from either pushing the frontiers of science (e.g., R&D labs creating new quantum effects) or being triggered by market demands and customer frustrations \[3, 4\]. This interaction is often described as "knowledge spaghetti," where science and market signals continuously shape one another \[3\].
\*   \*\*Users as Active Innovators:\*\* Firms source ideas from \*\*lead users\*\*, who identify requirements long before the general market, and \*\*extreme users\*\*, who operate under demanding constraints \[5, 6\]. These users frequently modify products themselves to improve performance, providing blueprints for radical breakthroughs \[7, 8\].
\*   \*\*Recombinant Innovation:\*\* This involves taking established concepts or business models from one industry and applying them to a new context, such as QT \[6, 9\].
\*   \*\*Other Triggers:\*\* Innovation can also be sparked by system shocks (e.g., pandemics), regulation changes, accidents, and benchmarking against competitors \[9\].

### \*\*2. The ‘Open Innovation’ Paradigm\*\*
The open innovation model, popularized by Henry Chesbrough, suggests that ideas flow across organizational boundaries through licensing, joint ventures, and spin-outs \[10, 11\].
\*   \*\*Is it new?\*\* While the term is modern, the concept of paying attention to external sources was established decades ago (e.g., the SAPPHO project in the 1970s) \[11\]. However, modern \*\*markets for technology\*\* have improved, allowing firms to more effectively use licenses to both access and capture value \[11\].
\*   \*\*Nature of Technology Markets:\*\* These markets are often imperfect because it is difficult to value intangible knowledge and predict the impact of new technology \[12\].
\*   \*\*Role of Stakeholders:\*\* Open innovation is a distributed process involving suppliers, customers, and even regulators, especially in QT where national security implications drive policy \[13\].

### \*\*3. Pavitt Taxonomy: Quantum Tech as Science-Intensive\*\*
According to Keith Pavitt’s taxonomy, quantum technology firms fall into the \*\*Science-Based\*\* category \[14, 15\].
\*   \*\*Links to University Research:\*\* Science-based innovators draw heavily on internal R&D and deep links to academic research \[14, 15\]. In QT, this reflects the "Stanford School" view of science: researchers do not just observe nature but \*\*intervene to create new phenomena and 'effects'\*\* (like lasers or entanglement) \[2, 16\].
\*   \*\*Locus of Activity:\*\* Technological activity is centered in high-investment corporate R&D and scientific research teams that seek to turn these created phenomena into marketable products \[14, 16\].

### \*\*4. The Nature of Strategy: Good vs. Bad\*\*
Richard Rumelt defines the core of strategy as discovering critical factors in a situation and designing a coordinated way to deal with them \[17\].
\*   \*\*The "Kernel" of Good Strategy:\*\* 
    1.  \*\*Diagnosis:\*\* Honestly defining the obstacles the firm faces \[18\].
    2.  \*\*Guiding Policy:\*\* An overall approach chosen to cope with or overcome those obstacles \[18\].
    3.  \*\*Coherent Actions:\*\* Feasible, coordinated resource allocations and actions that mutually reinforce one another \[18\].
\*   \*\*Bad Strategy:\*\* Characterized by "fluff" (broad goals, vision statements, and ambitions like "we want to be the top firm") that skips over the hard choices and the diagnosis of actual problems \[17\]. It often mistakes financial forecasting or "budget drumbeats" for actual strategy \[19, 20\].

### \*\*5. Rationalist vs. Incrementalist Strategies\*\*
\*   \*\*Rationalist (Planning) School:\*\* Follows an "ends-means" logic: appraise, determine, act \[21\]. It often uses tools like \*\*Porter’s Five Forces\*\* to seek market imperfections and monopoly profits \[21\]. 
    \*   \*Criticism:\* It tends to be top-down and often \*\*underestimates technology’s power\*\* to disrupt the very market forces it analyzes \[22, 23\].
\*   \*\*Incrementalist (Capabilities-based) School:\*\* Based on what firms actually do, recognizing that the future is unpredictable \[24, 25\]. It focuses on \*\*cumulative learning\*\* and trial-and-error \[24\].
    \*   \*Logic:\* Success comes from developing \*\*firm-specific knowledge\*\* and being flexible enough to adapt to unplanned market signals, as seen in Honda’s historical success \[26, 27\].

### \*\*6. Teece’s Business Model Framework in QT\*\*
David Teece’s framework explains that profiting from innovation requires both value creation and \*\*value capture\*\* via \*\*complementary assets\*\* \[28, 29\].
\*   \*\*Appropriability Regimes:\*\* This refers to how easy it is for competitors to imitate an innovation. Most QT exists in "weak" regimes where technology is complex but can eventually be copied \[30, 31\].
\*   \*\*Specific Examples in QT:\*\*
    \*   \*\*IBM:\*\* Uses \*\*Intellectual Property Rights\*\* as a primary complementary asset, holding over 1,000 QT-related patents to create a legal monopoly \[32\]. IBM also shifted to a "services" model to capture more value \[33\].
    \*   \*\*Toshiba:\*\* Leverages \*\*global distribution and after-sales support\*\* to dominate markets even when they weren't the sole innovator (as seen historically with the CAT scanner) \[34\].
    \*   \*\*Rigetti Computing:\*\* Developed a \*\*process innovation\*\* (hybrid quantum-classical computing) and a cloud platform (Forest) to help capture value from software developers \[35\].
    \*   \*\*General QT Risks:\*\* If a firm develops a technically superior quantum computer but lacks \*\*distribution channels, brands, or co-specialized assets\*\* (like maintenance teams), they will likely lose the market to a "fast follower" who possesses these assets \[34, 36\].
