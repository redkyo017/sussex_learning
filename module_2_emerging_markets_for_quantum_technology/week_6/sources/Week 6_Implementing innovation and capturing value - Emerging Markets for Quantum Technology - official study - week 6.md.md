6.1 Implementing innovation

BACK TO TOP

We will use the Tidd and Bessant innovation model again and look at the last two stages.

As we have seen, innovation is an inherently uncertain coupling process that matches a technical capability to a commercial need. Given the technological, market, organisational and strategic uncertainties involved, it is an inherently risky process. However, organisations can manage the process in ways that reduce the risks and enable them to build new capabilities that lead, hopefully, to superior outcomes. This section explores some of the research on successful implementation and applies it to high-tech quantum markets. It looks at the specific features involved in developing quantum products. These are characterised by greater than normal technological uncertainty and implementation difficulties, because quantum firms are typically ‘specialist suppliers’ that require close working with customers and other external partners.

Implementation

There are a variety of ways in which firms can implement their innovation strategies to build new markets. We are going to distinguish between implementation in large firms – such as Google – and implementation in new (small firm) ventures.

#### The following are key ideas to bear in mind:

large firms have resources advantages

small firms have behavioural advantages.

This means that larger firms typically have, for example, more managerial expertise on hand, more finance and capital, existing structures and processes in place and established relationships with customers and suppliers. In many ways, this makes innovation much easier.

Small firms, on the other hand, lack these resources and often must look externally for them. This might suggest that small firms are at a significant disadvantage compared with larger organisations. In many ways, this is true. However, the development of a lot of new technology is carried out in small firms. This is because small firms have behavioural advantages. Larger firms can be bureaucratic and, because they have formal structures, they can be slower to make decisions. Small firms are nimble, fast-moving and flexible. They are also often better incentivised to succeed. Managers in large firms have established careers – if the project fails, they move on to the next project, and if it succeeds, they don’t always get large rewards. An entrepreneurial team in a small firm is likely to lose out if the project fails. However, they may have equity in it, so they will benefit significantly if it is a success.

Non-innovative organisations

The relative benefits of implementing a project in a small firm or team can be seen by looking at the factors associated with poor innovation outcomes.

#### The following factors are associated with constraints:

poor communication

limited tools and resources

top-down managerial style

formal, with limited opportunities for change

culture of inferiority

a lack of focus whether organisational or within the team

accounting practices dominating creativity

status quo focused rewards

managerial career paths, for example technical experts may be forced into management to advance their career. This means that their technical expertise is no longer present ‘at the workbench’.

These are all found in stable, well-established large firms. Ideally, it would be good to combine the resources of large firms with the flexibility of small firms. Large firms often do this by setting up internal ventures that are treated and managed like new, entrepreneurial ventures, but are supported and resourced like large firms.

Innovative organisations

Innovative organisations, on the other hand, are characterised by very different factors. These enable the uncertainties involved in innovation to be better managed, and they support improved coordination across the firm.

#### Key features of innovative organisations include:

a culture of creativity and experimentation that promotes risk-taking and rewards employees for coming up with new ideas

open communication and collaboration between employees, departments and external partners, to support the exchange of ideas, knowledge and skills

continuous learning and development, through investment in their employees’ skills and knowledge, for example, by providing training opportunities and mentoring programs

agility and adaptability – innovative organisations are agile and adaptable, and able to respond quickly to changes in the market or business environment; they are not afraid to pivot or adjust their strategy when necessary

customer-centric approaches that, as the ‘Scientific Activity Predictor from Patterns with Heuristic Origins,’ (SAPPHO) project (Rothwell et al.

) found, focus on what customers need and use customer feedback to inform their innovation strategy; firms design products and services with the needs of the customer in mind, rather than focusing on novel, exciting technology that doesn’t address a problem or deliver value for customers

well-managed processes – often with ‘heavy-weight’ project managers with the managerial skills and organisational support needed to force change across the organisation; such managers will typically have the support of the senior managerial team, who will govern the organisation in a way that encourages disruption and innovation.

Innovative organisational structures

Effective innovation needs to take place within an organisational structure that enables learning, communication, the allocation of resources and decision-making. There are many different kinds of organisations and organisational structures.

Henry Mintzberg is a famous Canadian professor of strategy and is an organisational theorist. He has proposed a taxonomy of five basic organisational structures, each with its own characteristics that have an impact on different kinds of innovation. Depending on the type of innovation being undertaken and the context in which it is happening, different organisational forms may be more or less effective

#### Simple structure:

this is typically characterised by a small organisation, such as a start-up or small firm, with informal communication and decision-making processes. Simple structure organisations tend to be agile and flexible, which makes them well-suited to adapting quickly to changing circumstances. Staff often have a range of generic skills and can be reallocated to new jobs as required. Many new quantum technologies will be developed in small simple structures.

Machine bureaucracy

: this structure is characterised by standardised processes, formalised rules and procedures, and a clear hierarchy of authority. It is typically found in large, stable organisations such as manufacturing plants or government agencies. Machine bureaucracy organisations are better suited to incremental innovation or improvements in efficiency. They are often excellent structures for implementing scale-intensive production that depends on production engineering, risk management and the co-ordination of specialist supply chains. Given the consequences and costs of radical change, such firms tend to be risk-averse and slow to change. Many of the customers of new quantum technologies are likely to be such firms – often called ‘scale intensive’. The advanced technologies coming out of the revolution in second generation quantum technology can be used by firms to improve the performance of their production processes, which are often undertaken at such scale that even small improvements are extremely valuable.

Professional bureaucracy

: this is characterised by a high degree of specialisation, with employees possessing specific skills and knowledge. It is typically found in professional service firms such as law firms, accounting firms or government agencies. Professional bureaucracy organisations tend to be better suited to innovation in service design and often have business models based around the delivery of standardised services that are modified to fit the specific needs of customers. Organisations that are involved in the regulation of quantum technologies are often professional bureaucracies. Decision-making in such organisations can be slow and sometimes ponderous.

Divisionalised form

: this structure is characterised by semi-autonomous divisions or business units, each with its own functional hierarchy. It is typically found in large corporations with diverse product lines or geographic markets. Divisionalised form organisations are well suited to product innovation, as this allows the development of new products or services within each division or business unit, and the adaptation of products and services developed centrally to the needs of local markets and customers.

: this structure is characterised by a decentralised, flexible organisation that emphasises teamwork and collaboration. It is typically found in creative industries such as advertising, design or technology start-ups. Adhocracy organisations are well suited to radical innovation or breakthrough ideas, as they provide a high degree of autonomy and freedom for employees to experiment and take risks. Organisational forms are often project based, with staff coming onto projects as and when they are needed. This kind of organisational structure is often useful for undertaking radical innovations.

Innovative organisational structures

Mintzberg came up with a new organisational form known as ‘mission oriented’ in his 2015 book

Rebalancing Society

. This is a mission-driven type of organisation that typically focuses on creating social and environmental value rather than maximising profits. Examples might be charities or social enterprises.

#### Mission-oriented organisations are characterised by having:

a purpose that inspires staff and is central to the organisation’s identity; this guides the organisation’s decisions and actions, creates a shared culture and values, and creates a sense of community and purpose among employees

a decentralised structure that empowers employees to take responsibility for achieving the organisation’s mission and encourages collaboration and innovation

a networked organisation that engages with external stakeholders, such as customers, suppliers and partners, to achieve the mission and create social and environmental value.

Such organisations, typically, need a leadership style that empowers employees rather than controls them. Mintzberg suggests that these organisations are particularly well suited to addressing complex social and environmental challenges that require a co-ordinated effort across different sectors and stakeholders. They create non-economic values, and he argues that they are increasingly important to society.

Firms and Implementation

By understanding the link between different types of firms and the needs of different types of innovation, it is possible to understand the emergence of new markets and market structures with a little more nuance.

However, structures are not the only things that drive success in emerging markets. It also depends on firms developing a shared vision, leadership and will to innovate. This, in turn, depends on the people within the organisation and its organisational culture. Key individuals within the firm play a vital role, and high involvement of management is normally needed to ensure effective and creative teamwork across boundaries to manage the inherent uncertainties involved.

Shared vision

Innovation is about learning and change and, as a result, it is often disruptive, risky and costly. As Machiavelli pointed out, for people within an organisation the costs are obvious, but the benefits are uncertain and occur in the future

This asymmetry means that there are cognitive, structural and behavioural barriers to innovation that reinforce the status quo. Leaders and their teams need to overcome these barriers, but it is often very difficult. Kodak and Polaroid are examples of organisations that failed to change (Melvin

, Tripsas and Gavetti

To do this, innovative organisations don’t just have a single prescription or structure – they have an integrated set of components that work together to encourage innovation. Shared visions, effective experimentation and acceptance of risk are needed.

Strategy and shared vision

Often the shared visions that senior managers try to implement are vacuous. They need to be strategic. As we saw previously, strategy is not about goals, vague objectives, unrealistic ambitions, achieving tautological end results, or achieving catch-all broad outcomes.

Strategy Professor Chales Baden Fuller has highlighted that strategy is how an organisation conceives and deliberately organises and mobilises its resources in a coherent way to achieve its objectives (see Volberda et al 2021 for a discussion)

). It is a process not a plan, or even a document.

As we saw earlier, strategy requires the identification and diagnosis of a problem, and the analysis, selection and implementation of potential solutions that work together to achieve a desired outcome. Conceiving, evaluating, comparing options (if possible, how they might reinforce each other) and selecting options are required. This is a process of implementation and continuous modification that executes the strategy and mobilises people, money and other resources. These resources aren’t fixed, and organisations can build or acquire new capabilities.

Good strategies have actions that are coherent and work to mutually reinforce each other to generate superior outcomes. Poor strategy-making leads to actions that conflict and generate worse outcomes.

The dynamics of strategy

Strategy is inherently dynamic as objectives and environments change, and organisations build new capabilities. Good strategies are therefore not fixed, deterministic plans. For Mintzberg, organisations may start with intended strategies, but abandon unrealised strategies that fail, leaving their deliberate strategy, which becomes the organisation’s realised strategy when combined with the emergent strategy generated by unforeseen events

Put simply, organisations normally start with a broad sense of direction, drop plans that don’t work, build on the things that do work, and integrate them with new options as they emerge. Firms in emerging markets will try their best to forecast changes in technology and market opportunities, but, given the inherent uncertainty, they will need to make changes.

#### To ensure that they change in an effective way firms will often:

explore the implications of a range of possible trends

ensure broad participation and informal channels of communication

encourage multiple sources, debate and (constructive) skepticism

be prepared to change strategy in the light of new (and unexpected) evidence.

Organisational processes and tools

The preceding points suggest that a clean distinction between formulating a strategy and implementing it is misleading. During implementation, the organisation will learn, and that learning is likely to lead to the strategy being modified. Organisations dealing with emerging technologies differ in their ability to do this. However, they can build the capabilities needed to continuously improve and perform better in the following areas:

– random problem-solving, no formal structures, bursts of activity and short term.

– formalised problem-solving processes, participation, training, idea management and recognition in parallel.

Goal oriented

– all of the above, plus monitoring and measurement against strategic goals and inline systems.

– all of the above plus devolution of implementation to problem-solving units and formal experimentations.

– automatic capture and sharing of learning; everyone is actively involved; it’s incremental and radical innovation.

Key management tasks

In emerging technology markets, innovation leadership and strategy are built on the identification, development and exploitation of firm-specific capabilities to match a technical opportunity to a customer’s need, in ways that add value for the customer and enable the firm to profit.

With emerging technology markets, there are a range of forecasting methods that can be used to reduce some of the uncertainty involved. These typically require firms to create an open, sceptical organisational culture and a scientific approach to decision-making by means of which assumptions are made clear and explicit, and decision rules are simple so that the impacts of decisions can be properly assessed.

It is important for firms to identify the types and sources of risks and uncertainties, and to manage, avoid, transfer and mitigate them effectively. To do this typically requires leadership, creating an appropriate organisational climate, having good processes and using appropriate tools and techniques (See Tidd and Bessant

6.2 Capturing value

BACK TO TOP

One of the most important ideas in this module is the notion of complementary assets. Innovation is more than just creating value; firms need to capture value, and ‘complementary assets’ help firms to do that. We have focused before on how firms capture financial value, for example, through patents. We now want to add to that by looking at how firms might capture non-financial value as well, through learning and organisational development. We now move from implementation to capturing, as per the Tidd and Bessant innovation model.

Two sides of business models

The business models that firms use determine how they create and capture value. Thinking strategically, we can divide this up into current operational ways to create and capture value and longer term, strategic changes that the firm will undertake to create more value and find new ways of capturing it. This extends to finding new ways to create and capture non-financial forms of value, such as learning and improving. Intellectual property

As we have seen previously, there are many kinds of complementary assets such as brands, hard-to-copy technology and tacit knowledge. One complementary asset that is particularly important in quantum technology emerging markets is patents. These prevent competitors from copying innovations and capturing value and can themselves be licensed and sold.

#### As we saw previously, patents require:

– no ‘prior art’ – in the US ‘first to invest’, while in the rest of the world, novelty is defined by the firm that is ‘first to file’.

An inventive step

– that is not obvious to someone skilled in the art.

Industrial application

– a test of utility, and specific applications.

Patentable subject

– discoveries and formulas, for example, cannot be patented.

Clear and complete disclosure

– makes patents a very useful source of information for other firms.

Patents also need to be enforced, and kept up to date, possibly across many jurisdictions, which can be expensive, especially for resource constrained small firms.

– to improve the image or position of a company, for example, to attract partners, talent or funding, or to build brands and enhance market position.

Strategic patenting

It is very important to highlight that patents are strategic complementary assets because they can be used as part of wider strategic plays. This is particularly important in new markets based on emerging technologies. With more established technologies, where the products are well established and ‘dominant designs’ have emerged, innovation may depend far more on achieving economies of scale in production.

Many firms in markets that are subject to rapid technological change use patents strategically. Apple aggressively defends its patents against alleged infringements, for example, against HTC and Samsung in 2011, when it sought to ban sales of competing mobile devices. Nokia similarly won a patent dispute regarding touch-screen technology with Apple in 2011. It received 2% of iPhone revenues, that is, in excess of $30 billion a year

Oracle launched a case against Google, alleging that Android infringes Java patents and claiming $6.1bn in damages. Nortel sold its entire patent portfolio in 2011 for $4.5 billion to a consortium of firms: Apple, Microsoft, Sony Ericsson and RIM (Blackberry). In response, Google acquired Motorola’s mobile telephony patents in 2011 for $12.5bn, because of the vulnerability of its Android platform, and sold the manufacturing business to Lenovo in 2014

Markets for technology

Patents and other intellectual property rights can also be used as a source of funding. They can be licensed in and out of the firm. The resulting markets for technology are increasingly important for innovation. Firms may wish to license technology and use markets for technology rather than developing the technology themselves if:

managing intellectual property and markets for technology is excessively complex

the costs of searching, registering and renewing a portfolio are very high

strategic costs of positioning with respect to competitors are high

they need to register and manage their new technologies and products in international markets where access is difficult and legal systems differ.

Full disclosure may be required, and firms may be reluctant to disclose. They need deep pockets and financial resources to enforce their patents in international courts. It may be easier to license the technology than to prevent other firms from using it forever.

Technological assets

When firms think strategically about how they might use their technologies in markets based on novel technology, it is important to consider the risks and uncertainties. Research by Heeley and Jacobson

suggests that:

firms that exploit median-age technologies (relative to sector) often outperform firms operating at the technological frontier and firms that are following behind with older technology

firms using frontier technology often incur excessive risk and costs

firms using more mature technology face limited opportunities to create value.

Firms using median-age technology are often in a better place to balance cost, risk and opportunity. This raises the question of how to identify optimum timing.

Capturing learning

A vital way in which firms build capabilities over time is to capture learning and continuously improve. There are a number of tools that firms can use to capture learning during and after their innovations. These include:

post-project reviews

embedding continuous learning within the organisation

agile methods – formalised learning cycles

benchmarking – within and across the firm(s), and within and across sectors/nations

innovation audits – understand what works well (or not) and why.

It is important to gather information and use it to reflect upon where the firm was successful and where it needs to improve, and then to improve the management of the process.

#### When capturing learning, there is a range of key questions that can be asked:

How well do we search for opportunities? Where can we improve?

How well do we manage selection? What could we do better?

How well do we manage each stage of the full innovation process?

Does our organisation support innovation effectively?

Do we have a clear and communicated innovation strategy?

Do we build and maintain high quality and appropriate external linkages?

How well do we capture learning from innovation? Where can we improve?

Developing innovation capability

As we have seen, the innovative dynamic markets associated with emerging technologies are inherently uncertain. We have also seen that firms can learn to manage that uncertainty. Uncertainty can never be avoided, but firms can learn to manage it better than their competitors, and they can build an ability to improve how they innovate across the entire firm.

Firms can develop awareness of the need to change and the capability to improve. This allows them to move from piecemeal informal approaches to more managed approaches based on effective systems and continuous improvement.

One way to do this is through formal and informal innovation audits that allow firms to:

see what they are doing well or badly

generate checklists to see if what they are doing is right

benchmark to compare themselves against others

establish a process of continuous improvement

develop learning resources to help them to acquire knowledge.

This provides a way of focusing on subsystems and working with process owners, suppliers and customers to improve.

Capturing value is a key part of innovation management and needs to be done in a systemic, and strategic way. The ability to capture value determines who profits from innovation, and hence which firms grow and which firms exit markets in emerging technologies. Firms that lack complementary assets, or fail to manage them, are likely to exit the market, no matter how good their technology is.

Value capture requires complementary assets that can be internal and external. They can be static (for example, patents) or dynamic (for example, learning). Learning is important because firms can build an ability to continuously improve the ways in which they manage innovation. This has the potential to increase their financial returns and reduce the risks involved in future projects. Organisations need to learn from experimentation. Systematic measurement and benchmarking support continuous improvement.
