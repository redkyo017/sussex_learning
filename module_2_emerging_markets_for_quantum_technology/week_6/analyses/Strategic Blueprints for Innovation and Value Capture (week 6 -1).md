# Strategic Blueprints for Innovation and Value Capture (week 6 -1)

Based on the week 6 resources, the following synthesis and analysis examine the mechanisms of business model design, organizational management for innovation, and the strategic deployment of assets to capture value.

### \*\*1. Key Parts of a Business Model: Static vs. Dynamic Terms\*\*

A business model is defined as the blueprint for how a venture creates, delivers, and captures value \[1, 2\]. It can be analyzed in two distinct temporal dimensions:

\*   \*\*Static (Operational/Present):\*\* This focus is on the immediate delivery of value and the mechanics of current revenue.
    \*   \*\*Value Creation:\*\* Focused on fulfilling hidden user needs through a reliable delivery system \[3\].
    \*   \*\*Value Capture:\*\* Concerned with immediate transactional questions—"Who pays?" and "How?"—while distinguishing between users and the funders who act as customers \[3\].
\*   \*\*Dynamic (Strategic/Long-Term):\*\* This focus is on how the firm evolves to sustain its position and scale.
    \*   \*\*Value Creation:\*\* A shift from an early state of "no-advantage" toward establishing a durable, long-term competitive advantage \[3\].
    \*   \*\*Value Capture:\*\* Developing methods for scaling the business, such as branching, franchising, licensing, or selling the venture entirely \[3\]. 

Strategy in this context is viewed as an inherently dynamic process where firms start with a broad direction, abandon unrealized plans, and integrate emergent strategies generated by unforeseen events \[4\].

### \*\*2. Differentiating Management Styles of Innovative vs. Non-Innovative Organizations\*\*

The resources contrast the behavioral and structural patterns that either foster or stifle innovation:

| Feature | \*\*Innovative Organizations\*\* | \*\*Non-Innovative Organizations\*\* |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| \*\*Culture\*\* | Promotes risk-taking and rewards experimentation; failure is seen as a necessary learning step \[5, 6\]. | Rigidly focused on the status quo; accounting practices and cost-reduction dominate creativity \[7, 8\]. |
| \*\*Communication\*\* | Open collaboration between departments and external partners; utilizes "technological gatekeepers" \[6, 9\]. | Poor internal communication; strict functional silos and top-down managerial styles \[7, 10\]. |
| \*\*Management Role\*\* | Uses \*\*"heavyweight" project managers\*\* with senior authority to force change across the firm \[6, 9\]. | Technical experts are often forced into management to advance, removing their skills from "the workbench" \[7\]. |
| \*\*Learning Style\*\* | Continuous learning via mentoring, agile cycles, and "organizational slack" for exploration \[5, 6\]. | Lacks focus and formal structures for capturing learning; random problem-solving without formalization \[7, 11\]. |
| \*\*Customer Focus\*\* | \*\*Customer-centric\*\*; uses feedback to inform strategy rather than just pushing novel technology \[6\]. | Lacks market orientation; often ignores user needs in favor of internal R&D priorities \[6, 7\]. |

Structural design also plays a role: \*\*Simple structures\*\* (start-ups) are agile but vulnerable, while \*\*Adhocracies\*\* are exceptional at managing the extreme uncertainty of radical innovation through multidisciplinary teams \[12-14\].

### \*\*3. Deployment of Complementary Assets to Capture Value\*\*

Innovation requires not just the assets to create a product (R&D), but "complementary assets" to capture the resulting profits and prevent imitators from taking the market \[15, 16\]. These deployments vary based on the type of value targeted:

#### \*\*Capturing Financial and Legal Value\*\*
\*   \*\*Strategic Patenting:\*\* Patents provide a legal monopoly but can also be used as \*\*bargaining chips\*\* for alliances, \*\*offensive tools\*\* to limit competition, or \*\*defensive shields\*\* to minimize imitation \[17\]. 
\*   \*\*Licensing:\*\* Firms may choose to license technology through "markets for technology" if the costs of enforcing their own portfolio internationally are too high or if they lack the "deep pockets" required for legal battles \[18\].

#### \*\*Capturing Market and Strategic Value\*\*
\*   \*\*Lead-Time Advantages:\*\* Firms capture value by speeding up innovation cycles to get to market before competitors can copy their current offerings \[19, 20\].
\*   \*\*Brands and Reputations:\*\* These act as signals of quality, reducing the cost of working with customers and building trust \[20, 21\].
\*   \*\*Co-specialized Assets:\*\* Providing after-sales support, maintenance, and training creates "lock-in," making a firm's product more attractive than a technically superior but unsupported rival \[22, 23\].

#### \*\*Capturing Dynamic/Non-Financial Value\*\*
\*   \*\*Organizational Learning:\*\* This is a dynamic complementary asset where firms use tools like \*\*innovation audits\*\*, post-project reviews, and agile cycles to build a cumulative ability to manage uncertainty better than competitors \[24, 25\].
\*   \*\*Absorptive Capacity:\*\* Investing in internal R&D acts as an "admission ticket" to global networks, allowing a firm to recognize and commercially exploit external scientific discoveries \[26, 27\].

In \*\*Science-Based sectors\*\* like quantum, the deployment of patents, hard-to-copy production knowledge, and secrecy are particularly critical for long-term survival \[28, 29\].
