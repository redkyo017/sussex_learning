Emerging markets

Markets where there is a lot of emerging technology differ from traditional markets. There are specific features that are essential to appreciate if we are to understand emerging quantum markets based on the commercial application of new quantum technologies.

\*\*Rapid change

Due to constant technological advancements and displacement of older products and technologies. This dynamism can make it difficult to predict the future direction of the market and increases the degree of ‘churn’ as new firms constantly enter and exit the market.

\*\*High uncertainty

Because market players are often unsure about which technologies will succeed, how they will generate value for customer markets and how the resulting markets will evolve.

\*\*High risk

Because companies often have to invest significant amounts of capital in developing new technologies and bringing them to market, without knowing whether they will be successful. These high risks often mean that larger firms, with more assets are better able to manage innovation, or entrepreneurial firms within the ecosystem draw on providers of external equity finance who can manage those risks across an entire portfolio of firms.

\*\*Disruptive nature

Because emerging technologies disrupt established markets and potentially create new subsectors, sectors and even entire industries. Novel, innovative, emerging technologies can change how firms operate and have the potential to radically change entire production processes, product and service offerings, and markets. The customers of emerging technology firms may change substantially and the innovating firm may be able to capture significant amounts of that downstream value.

\*\*Strong innovation

With high levels of spending on research and development and often extensive international links to the global science system undertaking research in related areas (including customers’ technologies).

\*\*A collaborative ecosystem

Where companies, start-ups and researchers work together to create new technologies and products. These collaborations are not just about creating value together, as they also include collaborating to navigate the market and find new and longer-term methods of capturing value.

\*\*Global reach

Emerging technology markets often have a global reach, as companies and products can easily cross borders and reach customers around the world. This creates additional costs for small firms who may need to collaborate and makes finding ‘lead customers’ vital.

When exploring emerging markets for quantum technology, it is therefore important to understand the firms that are involved; specifically, how well those firms are going to be able to manage the innovation process and bring new products and services to market that create value for their customers while they capture that value. This requires a firm to effectively manage their innovation process, have a clear and well-designed strategy and build an innovative organisation.

Large and small firms

Within any market for emerging technologies, we will typically find a mixture of large and small firms. These firms have different capabilities and often focus on different parts of the innovation process. The balance between them changes over time and we often find that many small firms exist at the early stages of innovation, but as the industry matures, more large firms grow to dominate markets.

As we discussed in Week 6, research at the Sussex Science Policy Research Unit (SPRU) has determined that

large firms have material advantages and small firms have behavioural advantages

). This means that large firms often have more money, more managers, more specialised staff and more machinery and, in some instances, that gives them the advantage. But they also have disadvantages, as their sheer size means that decision-making can be slow, and the organisation can be difficult to change. On the other hand, small firms are often superior at making fast decisions and are more adaptable and agile. Smaller firms have fewer people to engage with, which makes communications easier. The staff in small firms are often better incentivised as they are often more likely to lose their jobs if things go badly wrong. Also, individuals might have equity stakes in start-ups which means that they can benefit significantly if things go well.

Large firms

Large firms with more than 10,000 employees are important for emerging technologies such as quantum, because they are major sources of technology and innovation. Large firms are very important in both the supplier sectors (for example, chemical, electrical and electronics) and in many customer sectors where large firms will use quantum technologies and products to produce their own goods and services (for example, telecommunications).

Large firms are remarkably resilient and outlive the entrepreneurs who founded them; they can often manage waves of radical innovations.

In addition, large firms are often very good at producing efficiently because they can exploit economies of scale and scope.

The Chandlerian firm

To understand the benefits that large firms have it is worth exploring the work of the historian Alfred Chandler. He asked: Why did large firms simultaneously emerge in the 1860s in the USA very suddenly across a range of sectors? (Chandler

This was not due to new technology (as the technology didn’t change in many sectors and differed across sectors), or new management techniques (as some sectors stayed small firm based).

Nor was it because a new organisational innovation emerged – large firms didn’t emerge in every sector, as some sectors remained dominated by small firms.

Moreover, scale-intensive production was already possible, but was considered too risky. Large blast furnaces, for example, had already been built, but owners had to ensure that they never ran out of fuel, which was not always possible. When they ran out of fuel, they cooled down and cracked.

Chandler showed that large firms emerged when new managerial methods were developed that allowed managers to control the flow of goods and materials within firms. This allowed them to manage flows with improved regularity, volume and speed, which generated new economies of scale and scope

The key issue for Chandler’s explanation is the

In the 1860s, new managerial skills diffused from the US railroads and enabled the potential of larger scale production to be realised at much lower risks and costs. Technology created potential economic improvements, but managerial innovations turned

The higher fixed costs involved in production mean that, if production volumes go down, then firms are in financial trouble as it is much harder to spread the higher fixed costs. Modern paper mills, for example, must operate at greater than 98 percent of their capacity (McCarthy and Urmanbetova

Hence, to manage the risks of having large production systems, firms must invest in ‘managers, marketing and manufacturing’. This allows firms to take advantage of the economies of scale generated by high fixed cost (but lower variable cost) production.

Scale and scope

This combination of managerial capabilities and large-scale production systems generate economic advantages. These can be characterised as follows:

#### Economies of scale:

these occur when producing more than one of the same product together is cheaper than producing them apart. A larger blast furnace, for example, has a lower heating cost per kg of iron, so producing more can be done at less cost.

#### Economies of speed:

these occur when increases in production per unit time period lead to reductions in costs. For example, speeding up a production line will increase the amount of output, but the fixed costs of paying for the building remain the same and can be spread over more output.

#### Economies of scope:

these occur when producing two or more different products together is cheaper than producing them apart, that is, when the same processes can be used to produce multiple products, for example, beer and marmite.

Large and small firms in an economy

We can apply these ideas to understand the systemic nature of an economy or sector. Recall that small firms have

behavioural

advantages (flexibility, speed of response, fear, relationships, and so on) and large firms have

advantages (finance, managerial skills, and so on).

Large firms can generate economies of scale but are only viable in (a) large stable markets, typically with (b) standardised products that can be (c) mass produced.

Small firms, on the other hand, can still find niches protected from competition where there are small markets for specialised (customised) products that are ‘bespoke’ or batch produced: for example, specialist suppliers of capital goods – machinery sold to large firms and designed to match their very specific production systems.

With that said, we would expect to find a mix of large and small firms interacting in the economy, with very different levels of productivity and business performance.

Internal versus external technology development

The increasing complexity of modern products and the production systems that are required to make them, mean that firms need more and more knowledge. In the past, they might have been able to develop this knowledge in-house. But today, firms are finding that they must increasingly source knowledge and technology externally. This has led to an expansion of technological

outsourcing

beyond production to new tech-based firms. This is very common with emerging technologies, where large firms may adopt an external portfolio approach to work with many firms to manage the risks and uncertainties involved.

Importantly, firms retain in-house competencies as ‘

absorptive capacity

’ to enable them to understand and absorb external knowledge and technology. Firms don’t face a choice of either doing research internally or doing it externally. They typically need to do research internally to understand and make quality judgements about what is being done externally.

This external engagement is complicated because customers’ demands and technological opportunities constantly change. In fast-moving environments, firms need to be able to change their capabilities (dynamic capabilities) and to align internal and external activities in real time to constantly changing networks.

Large firm external tech development

One way in which firms do this is through links with other large firms: for example, through strategic alliances in technology, marketing or other business activities. The rising costs of R&D and lengthening R&D ‘cycle times’, together with shorter product lifecycles mean that traditional internal forms of organisation break down and there is pressure on firms to outsource peripheral technological and product development to other companies. This helps to speed up development. For example, a firm may focus on the technologies that it is uniquely skilled at and outsource the production of other less important parts of their products.

Large-to-small and small-to-small firm links

This outsourcing is often done from large to small firms. For example, these supplier networks for components are a key part of the Toyota model of innovation.

Importantly, the effectiveness of these kinds of relationships depends on national characteristics. In Japan, for example, cross-share ownership and culture can make knowledge sharing and creation more effective than in the UK or the USA.

We also see networks of innovation between small firms. This was significant in the early years of Silicon Valley and Route 128 in the 1970s. Despite both being based on networks of small firms, they had very different characteristics.

From networks to systems

How effectively these local networks of different kinds of firms align is strongly influenced by persistent national, sectoral and local characteristics. For example, there have been differences in UK and German approaches to investment that have persisted for over 100 years.

Different local and sectoral economic systems vary in relation to their endowments of knowledge and technologies, the kinds of actors that are there (in other words, large firms, small firms, universities, and so on) and the ‘institutions’ or rules of the game that the local economy follows. It is important to emphasise that such systems need to ‘work’ productively. Simply having features doesn’t guarantee they will work well together and generate economic benefits.

Product complexity leads to organisational complexity

To understand the importance of systems, it is useful to consider what happens as the complexity of products increases. This increase in product complexity typically increases the complexity of production. This, in turn, increases cognitive complexity, because firms need to have a greater depth of knowledge about more technologies. This leads to greater organisational complexity with new divisions of labour and patterns of specialisation as firms try to access greater degrees of knowledge.

This all requires co-ordination, which increases the difficulty of managing innovation.

From the 1970s, this expansion in cognitive complexity has increasingly required external connections between large firms, between large and small firms, and between small firms.

Within these networks, it is not enough to just have links and connections – they also need to work well together and be productive. How effective they are depends on managerial action, but that action is strongly constrained by features of the firms’ national context.

Industrial dynamics and structure

Having set out systems, we can now look at their dynamics, with the focus on emerging technologies and their markets. We do this by exploring ‘

industrial dynamics,

’ which captures the turbulence of long-term economic growth.

#### A key idea is that industries differ in their ‘structure’ in two dimensions:

vertical relationships between industries (for example, grocers’ supply chains that go back to farmers, and methods of secure information transmission supplied by telecom firms that go back to quantum firms)

Industry structure is determined by a

distribution of firm sizes

Distribution of firm sizes

Nations of different wealth differ in the number of firms of particular sizes. Micro firms with a few employees are more common in developing countries which also have many industries with a few large firms. This leads to the idea of a ‘missing middle’ which makes it more difficult to generate stable employment and growth.

In wealthier countries, the distribution of firm size changes because of the three previously mentioned processes:

entry – a ‘birth’ process by which new firms come into existence

growth – a process during which an existing firm may grow or shrink while still surviving

exit – a ‘death’ process whereby firms leave an industry – bankruptcy, sold or merged.

Entry and exit rates over time determine the number of firms in an industry and their age structure. The following are some points to note:

High rates of entry can reduce the average age of firms.

High rates of exit are more ambiguous – in some industries, exit rates of smaller firms are high and the average age of firms is not affected much by their ‘churn’.

Dramatic new technology – ‘major innovations’ that come from new emerging technologies can lead large firms to exit: for example, new waves of technology have led to the exit (or merger) of many large media firms.

Distribution of firm sizes and technical change

The distribution of firm sizes is an indicator of the effects of technical change:

It may allow smaller firms to become more efficient.

For example, printing was historically done using ‘cast lead’ type, which required expensive machines to melt and form the ‘type’ (letters) which were assembled to form printing plates. Modern printing with photographic processes creates printing plates more cost-effectively, which reduces the cost advantage of large firms.

Alternatively, it may allow larger firms to become more efficient.

In the US, in 1905 there were 125 automobile companies. But, in 1908, Henry Ford introduced a standardised product, the Model T, which benefitted from mass production and, a decade later, only a few of the 125 firms were still in existence.

Efficiency from an economic perspective

Looking more closely at the effects of technical change on the size distribution of firms leads us to consider ‘efficiency.’ But what does ‘efficiency’ mean?

The concept of efficiency is closely linked to the idea of ‘unit cost’ or ‘cost per unit’ and these costs are influenced by size and experience. Economists have developed several concepts for understanding these influences.

#### Here are the two most important concepts:

‘Static’ economies of scale – why size in terms of a single output or multiple outputs may matter.

‘Dynamic’ economies of scale – learning and market development.

Static economies

The scale (or size) of output may influence the unit cost of output, with (at its most basic level) average costs (cost per unit produced) initially declining with increasing output.

‘Static’ economies of scale are related to both the distribution of fixed costs and additional savings made by operating at a larger scale. These savings often come from distributing the higher fixed costs, which means that overall costs fall as output increases. A greater output reduces long-run average costs (LRAC).

Firms also ‘learn-by-doing’ to improve co-ordination with suppliers and distributors to shorten delivery times and reduce costs from:

delays caused by missing inputs

costs of holding inventories of parts

costs of holding inventories of finished goods.

Appropriability and complementary assets

Efficiency is not enough to explain the survival of firms in a market. They also need to capture the economic or financial returns from innovation. Recall that, in many (perhaps most) instances, the innovator doesn’t get all the benefits of innovation, as some benefits are captured by customers, suppliers, imitators or followers, and other competitors.

Obviously, if you incur costs in innovating and someone else immediately reproduces your innovation, they will have a cost advantage over you, as you still have to recover the costs of making the innovation.

As David Teece highlights, capturing value requires a particular kind of ‘complementary asset’ such as a patent or some other kind of intellectual property protection that stops other firms copying and benefiting from your innovation. It is hard to overstate the importance of this idea. It also means that there is a lot of potential for firms to capture value from the innovations other firms pay for (Teece

Early studies of appropriability found that secrets were often more important than intellectual property. Even more important were dynamic methods of appropriability, such as ‘production lead time’ which reflects how innovations are improved over time and the fact that, having made an innovation, one immediately begins to improve upon it for the next offering.

Production lead times fall under dynamic appropriability. It involves learning and exploiting the fact that anyone seeking to imitate a product is always going to be behind if they only imitate a firm’s current offering – when the rival gets their product in the market, the firm will offer its ‘new and improved’ version to capture the dominant share of new purchases.

Why venture?

There are many possible motivating factors that might compel an organisation to embark on a venture. They may be a combination of the following:

to exploit underutilised resources

to pressure internal suppliers

to divest non-core activities

to satisfy managers’ ambitions

to spread risks of product development

to combat cyclical demand

to learn and develop new competences

to diversify.

The most effective structure for ventures depends on the balance between leverage and learning as companies exploit their own existing competencies and learn new competencies.

The management style of leveraging existing resources and capabilities, as opposed to learning competencies that can be resource-intensive, is quite different. Additionally, there are trade-offs between learning and enabling flexibility. For example, some corporations may create a ‘new venture department’ with a high degree of autonomy and flexibility to bring the workforce very close to users. But this may lead to a situation where ‘lessons learned’ are so context specific that it is not possible to apply those lessons to other projects even within that department.

Starting technology ventures

There are a lot of issues to consider when starting a new venture, not all of which are economically rational. The brutal reality is that most ventures fail, and fail quickly.

Businesses that fail can include successful firms that overtrade. The good news is that if a firm has a technological capability, complementary assets and other suitable advantages, then their chances of success improve. One factor that provides an indisputable advantage is support: financial support, market share, and positive personality traits in leadership are often vital.

Financing innovation

A key issue with emerging technologies is funding. Within (large) firms, the development of new technologies is typically funded from existing cash flow or other established sources of funding.

However, many emerging technologies are developed in small firms and start-ups. These firms have behavioural advantages over large firms, as they can adapt and be more agile, flexible and innovative. They can also incentivise staff with share options, which encourages them to put in the very long hours needed to develop an emerging technology and bring products to market. Such firms often do not already have products and services that can produce profits that can be used to fund innovation. Also, they can struggle to access borrowing from banks because they are high risk and their ‘capital’ is often intangible (in the form of smart people and technology) which cannot be used as collateral for loans.

How then do they finance innovation?

There are a variety of financial instruments, which can be understood as promises:

Debt is a promise to repay at a future date with interest.

Equity is a promise of a share of future profits in an enterprise.

Grants are promises to deliver against the proposal.

Bonds are a promise of future payments staged through time.

Importantly, although they transfer financial resources, they may also transfer risk and knowledge, across organisations.

Debt transfers resources but does not transfer risk. As such, it creates a substantial cost for the innovative entrepreneur as they will have to pay back the debt, no matter how well (or badly) the venture does.

Equity, on the other hand, transfers both resources and risk; with the transfer of risk comes an incentive to transfer knowledge. Details to note are that:

investors get a percentage of an uncertain venture and take on the risk of that investment

if the venture does well, they get more returns (unlike debt).

Because equity is so expensive, only a small number of firms consider it and only a small number of those firms are suitable.

For small amounts of equity, informal equity investors – often called business angels – are used. They vary widely in their abilities, but some can be very valuable sources of funding as a firm grows.

As firms grow, their funding needs to expand and they will, typically, have to move through rounds of funding, transferring equity and ensuring that investors from one round make a suitable return. This can create a significant problem of equity dilution because early investors may not be able to ‘keep up’ and may end up owning a decreasing proportion of the firm as more investors come in. An oft cited adage amongst entrepreneurs is ‘I used to own 100% of a firm worth £1 m, and now, after 10 years’ hard work, I own 1% of a firm worth £100 m’.

Venture capital

A distinct kind of equity investor is a venture capital fund. These are typically time-limited partnerships, and are formed by highly professional investors, who often have extensive experience of developing new technologies. This knowledge is vital for helping the firms they invest in achieve the very high rates of return that the funds are looking for. The venture capital (VC) industry is characterised by the following traits:

Small – with only a very tiny percentage of firms being suitable for VC funding. Despite the small number of firms that get investment, their impact on the overall economy is very large.

Specialised – because so much skill and knowledge is needed to develop firms, they tend to specialise in particular areas of technology, such as biotech.

Skewed – most investments don’t make any money and only a small number in a portfolio will generate the very high returns that will pay for all the others and help, hopefully, the fund to return a profit. The same skew can be seen in the overall performance of VC funds themselves, with some being very successful whereas many others do not make a decent return for their investors.

Skilled – given the difficulty of growing a global firm in an emerging technology market, VC fund partners are often highly skilled.

Scale intensive – the high costs of paying for all the partners and offices means that small funds are often financially unviable. The funds need to be large to generate profits for their investors.

Supported – VC is often seen as a ‘free market’ form of capitalism, but, in reality, in every nation where they operate, they need very significant government support. The market for equity finance for emerging technology firms has numerous market failures, and nations need effective policies to support a VC ecosystem.

Structured as funds – VC is typically structured as a fund, with the partners charging an annual fee to their investors and taking a percentage of the profits. This might be 2% a year over the ten-year life of the fund and then 25% of the final profits.

Significant – VC-backed firms have had a very significant impact on the global economy. Most major tech firms were once VC backed and it is estimated that about a third of the US stock market is VC backed. This is despite its very small size. ‘Since 1970, VC has funded approximately 820 new firms per year. These 820 start-ups have enormous impact. In 2003, they represented less than 0.05% of 2 million start-ups, employed 10 m people (9.4% of US) and generated $1.8 trillion in sales (9.6% of US). Moreover, in 2000, the 2,180 public companies that received VC backing between 1972 and 2000 comprised 11% of sales, 13% of profits, 6% of employees, a market value in excess of $2.7 trillion and one-third of market value of all public companies’ (Shane,

Slaughter not selection – VC funds invest in a portfolio of firms and will often be brutal in killing off firms that are not looking like they will make a good return. These resources are then recycled into the most valuable firms. Getting VC funding is therefore not something firms should do lightly.

Venture capital ecology

Just as sectors such as quantum will emerge in local areas with other related firms so that they can take advantage of agglomeration economies, we see something similar with VC. It is only really effective in a very small number of locations around the world – such as Boston, Silicon Valley, Israel and similar places due to the factors considered in preceding section.

#### To have a successful VC institutional ‘ecology’ a firm needs:

VC funds that are large enough to generate economies of scale and have knowledgeable staff that can make good investments and mentor firms to successful and highly profitable exits (for example, an initial public offering (IPO) or trade sale)

institutional investors willing to take on the risks of investing in VC funds

a supportive ecosystem of lawyers, consultants and other service professionals who can ensure that the funds and their portfolio of firms are properly supported; inexperienced lawyers, for example, can mess up the corporate governance of firms and make them much harder to value

a flow of very high-quality start-ups to generate the high level of deal flow of high potential firms worth investing in that is needed to make the funds viable

exit opportunities such as trade sales, or an active IPO, and markets so that the funds can capture some of the value they create.

These all need to be in place and operate successfully for multiple 10-year cycles. Such conditions are very rare, which is why VC has required significant levels of government support everywhere. The US is the main success story, but only in a few areas, and even there we see extensive government support.

Venture-capital-friendly business models

Given these features of the VC sector, it is vital that portfolio firms have business models that match the very specific funding requirements of VC funds, particularly in terms of being able to make very high returns very quickly. VC investors are often extremely demanding because of the time cost of capital, whereby the high fixed costs of running a fund accumulate very quickly and VC funds’ own investors seek high levels of returns to compensate for their higher risks.

VC aims to turn a small amount of money into a large amount of money in a short period of time. Innovation often turns a large amount of money into a larger amount of money, over a long period of time.

The business model of a firm, which documents how they create and capture value, has to be able to generate significant returns. Hence, the value that is created has to be very large and the firm itself must be able to capture much of that value. This is one reason why VC investing is concentrated in technology and emerging technology markets. These are typically the types of markets where innovation can be disruptive to existing industries and generate large returns quickly.

#### Here are some venture capital terms that you should be familiar with:

Valuation – what you agree the value of the entity is (pre-money is valuation by another name).

Dilution – new shares that are issued can 'dilute’ the ownership of existing owners and significantly affect their final returns; as a result, they are often given a first refusal to enable them to invest in future rounds of investment to maintain their percentage ownership.

Cap table – who owns what.

Board meetings – shareholder meetings.

Dynamic boards

Dynamic boards can help firms to raise money, transfer ownership between investors, ensure that they get a good return and change as the firm grows. If they are highly skilled or can change and bring in new highly skilled members as the firm grows, the firm can source other kinds of non-VC funding and get the benefits of VC expertise in other ways.

This is important as equity can be (or is) extremely expensive and risky, but it is an excellent way of transferring risk and resources. It only makes sense if you match the business and financing model with an ability to scale value capture.

Venture capital’s excessive cost makes it a last resort or a deliberate planned (high risk) choice. There are, however, other sources of money such as grants, high-net-worth individuals and business angels, corporate VC and alternative investment markets (AIMs). These will not bring in the experts and expertise of VC funds, but this problem can be overcome if you can have an effective dynamic board.

Funding innovation recap

Innovation is about both creating and capturing value; a key part of which is generating a decent financial return for a firm’s investors.

There are different kinds of investment, (debt, equity and similar) and there is a hierarchy or pecking order of finance. Within this pecking order, professional equity is the most expensive and difficult to manage, but it has the potential to offer very high returns to a small number of atypical firms. Hence, it is often the last resort source of funding, in particular, as it requires owners to give up part ownership of the firm.

The needs of a growing firm will obviously change over time and senior managers need to match their business models to the changing requirements of different kinds of investors. Professional equity investors (a type of VC) are incentivised to bring in expertise because they own a percentage of the firm, so, if it is extremely successful, they will make much higher returns. That expertise can be complemented or replaced by a dynamic board if the board decides that equity is too expensive and they can use other forms of funding.

Business plans

Given the uncertainty involved in funding innovation, it is often necessary to develop a business plan. Business plans are guides, which, given the inherent uncertainties, should be designed to be modified. The process of producing a plan that makes a firm’s reasoning explicit and opens it up to external review has significant value. With the ever-changing market dynamics, when developing business plans, a firm must ‘plan to pivot’.

description of the proposed venture that includes the objectives and characteristics

details of the management structure and compensation required.

Business plans – success strategies

Time spent on researching ‘success factors’ can be used to guide the design and selection of business plans. These success factors can include:

strategic fit and competitive advantage of the product or service

potential financial return of investment

existence of market and potential sales

risk–reward ratio

product champion and team

synergy with other businesses and opportunities

new market creation

close to current technology innovations.

Well-designed business plans can signal to external supporters the quality of the management and their thinking, which, in turn, cultivates confidence in the venture.

Business plans – common failings

There are a number of reasons why a poorly developed business plan can contribute to the failure of a new venture. Here are a few factors to consider:

Technology push – being caught up in a novel technology without doing due market analysis can be a danger to successful value capture.

Competitor awareness – little or no analysis can leave a venture open to risk from a better-positioned competing business.

Financial planning – poor cash-flow projection; no sensitivity analysis to forecast a lack of financial resilience for the venture.

Management team – emphasis on technical ability rather than management experience can expose the venture to management blind spots.

Technology based firms – these might have very specialised knowledge but do not have enough experience in finance and innovation, which could be a liability in a competitive market.

#### Here are some observations to note:

Invention is an event; innovation is a process. Creativity in producing an idea (invention) is important, but creativity is not ‘being wacky’. Creativity feeds into the innovation management pipeline and is needed throughout all stages.
