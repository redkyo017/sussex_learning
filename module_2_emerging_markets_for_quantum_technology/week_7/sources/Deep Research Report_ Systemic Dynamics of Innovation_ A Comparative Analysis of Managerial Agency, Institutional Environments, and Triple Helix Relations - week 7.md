Historical and Theoretical Foundations of the National Innovation System

The conceptual framework of the National Innovation System (NIS) arose in the late 1980s as a systemic critique of the neoclassical, linear model of technological change \[cite: 1, 2\]. Historically, mainstream economic analysis treated scientific progress as an exogenous force, positioning R&D as a linear sequence that begins with public investment in basic research, proceeds through applied development, and culminates in market commercialization \[cite: 3, 4\]. The systemic paradigm, pioneered by Christopher Freeman, Bengt-Åke Lundvall, and Richard Nelson, reconceptualized innovation as an interactive, non-linear learning process deeply embedded in national institutional structures \[cite: 5, 6\]. Within this perspective, a national innovation system is defined as the network of public and private institutions whose interactions initiate, import, modify, and diffuse new technologies \[cite: 6, 7\]. The capacity of an economy to innovate depends not on the isolated performance of individual organizations, but on how these diverse components interact as a collective system of knowledge creation and use \[cite: 8, 9\].

The intellectual roots of this approach trace back to Friedrich List’s nineteenth-century critique of Adam Smith’s cosmopolitan free-trade economics \[cite: 5\]. List formulated the concept of the national system of production to advocate for a German industrial catching-up strategy against British industrial dominance \[cite: 5\]. He argued that economic development requires the purposeful construction of national infrastructure, educational institutions, and protective frameworks to foster "mental capital" and productive forces, rather than relying strictly on the invisible hand \[cite: 5\]. Modern NIS literature has bifurcated along similar structural lines, distinguishing between narrow and broad definitions \[cite: 5, 10\]. The narrow approach, dominant in United States policy circles, focuses primarily on high-technology sectors and the systemic linkages between formal R&D efforts, scientific organizations, and public policy \[cite: 5, 10\]. The broad approach, championed by European and Scandinavian scholars, views innovation as a cumulative process of interactive learning that permeates the entire production structure, drawing on procurement, marketing, sales, and localized tacit knowledge \[cite: 5\]. This broad perspective incorporates Myrdal’s theory of cumulative causation, illustrating how institutions establish virtuous or vicious circles of competence-building and industrial development \[cite: 5\].

The Dialectic of Firm Agency and Institutional Selection Environments

The performance of an innovative firm is shaped by the tension between internal managerial strategy and the external institutional framework \[cite: 1, 11\]. While private firms are the primary agents of technological change, their actions are governed, enabled, and constrained by the national systems in which they reside \[cite: 1, 12\]. Managers operate within path-dependent institutional rules, macroeconomic realities, and technological trajectories that define the boundaries of strategic choice \[cite: 13, 14\]. This systemic balance of influence dictates that even the most aggressive corporate management cannot completely decouple its operations from the structural assets and liabilities of its home territory \[cite: 14, 15\].

Managers are responsible for cultivating organizational routines—the standardized procedures that govern how firms act and interact \[cite: 5\]. In an increasingly complex technological landscape, no single firm can internalize all required knowledge \[cite: 12\]. Consequently, a critical managerial competence is the maximization of external knowledge absorption, a process requiring substantial investment in internal R&D to build the absorptive capacity needed to identify, assimilate, and exploit external scientific discoveries \[cite: 1, 4\]. However, the efficiency of this process is heavily influenced by external "scientific instrumentalities" \[cite: 16\]. Derek de Solla Price characterized these instrumentalities as methodological or technical breakthroughs, such as Galileo’s telescope in 1610 or advanced statistical techniques, which bind scientists and engineers into "invisible colleges" that transcend corporate boundaries \[cite: 16\]. While managers can organize these invisible colleges internally, their stabilization relies on public science policies and regulatory support structures \[cite: 16, 17\].

This interplay is formalized in the Triple Helix model of university-industry-government relations, which conceptualizes the innovation ecosystem as a non-linear interaction among three distinct subdynamics \[cite: 17, 18\]. First, the market serves as a short-term clearing mechanism, seeking economic equilibrium between supply and demand \[cite: 17\]. Second, scientific research and technological development introduce an upsetting dynamic, producing novelties that disrupt existing market equilibria \[cite: 17\]. Third, public and private institutional arrangements function as retention and stabilization mechanisms, managing transaction costs and cushioning the economy against market fluctuations \[cite: 17\]. These three subdynamics operate as mutual selection environments \[cite: 17\].

Decoupling and Divergence: Are Firms in Sub-Optimal Environments Doomed to Fail?

The evolutionary-institutional approach rejects the notion of a single, optimal national innovation system, emphasizing that institutional arrangements are highly path-dependent and country-specific \[cite: 1, 6, 14\]. This raises the question of whether firms positioned in sub-optimal, weak, or highly volatile national environments are inevitably doomed to fail \[cite: 15, 22\]. In countries suffering from systemic failures—characterized by severe R&D underinvestment, weak intellectual property protection, bureaucratic barriers to university-industry linkages, and fragmented regulatory frameworks—firms face immense structural barriers \[cite: 12, 23, 24\]. Yet, historical evidence shows that firms in developing and catching-up economies can survive and achieve international prominence through strategic adaptation and institutional workarounds \[cite: 13, 25, 26\].

During the transition period of the 1990s, Central and Eastern European (CEE) economies achieved rapid productivity growth primarily through non-R&D factors, including severe labor shedding, plant closures, and cost-cutting, rather than domestic technological improvements \[cite: 11\]. As these catching-up economies approached the technological frontier, their low-cost advantages eroded, necessitating a transition from simple production systems to interactive, learning-based innovation systems \[cite: 11, 13\]. Successful firms in these transition environments survived by shifting their strategic focus from domestic knowledge generation to international knowledge diffusion and absorption \[cite: 11, 13\]. Good managers in deficient environments can actively decouple their firms from localized weaknesses by implementing compensatory open-innovation strategies \[cite: 4, 27\].

These compensatory strategies are manifest across several operational dimensions:

#### International Sourcing and Global Value Chains:

Rather than relying on weak domestic university systems, managers can partner with foreign knowledge institutions, license international patents, and attract foreign direct investment (FDI) to acquire advanced technology at lower cost \[cite: 14, 26\].

#### Exploiting Transnational Networks:

In sectors with high technological complexity, such as the maritime and petroleum clusters in Norway, firms achieve high efficiency by building transnational triple-helix networks that access structural holes in global knowledge dynamics, mitigating local constraints \[cite: 28\].

#### Frugal Innovation and Localized Bricolage:

In highly constrained developing contexts, firms can thrive by serving the "bottom of the pyramid" through frugal engineering \[cite: 10\]. Managers employ strategic bricolage—recombining existing, limited local resources to address unmet market needs—thereby creating sustainable business models that do not require sophisticated national innovation infrastructures \[cite: 10\].

However, there are absolute limits to the capacity of managerial agency to overcome environmental adversity \[cite: 29\]. Long-term, high-technology innovation requires structural trust, rule of law, and predictable policy frameworks \[cite: 29\]. Under authoritarian populist regimes, the erosion of institutional pluralism, academic autonomy, and open international collaboration damages the foundations of creative destruction \[cite: 29\]. Managers in these environments are forced to shorten their strategic horizons, shifting capital away from long-term, high-risk deep-tech research (such as medical or energy breakthroughs) toward short-term, low-risk, or politically favored projects \[cite: 29, 30\]. This dynamic leads to "technological involution," where high R&D spending is locked into reproducing existing ideas under state control rather than generating pioneering technological breakthroughs \[cite: 29\]. Thus, while capable managers can turn firms in weak environments into regional successes or low-cost competitors, establishing a sustained, frontier-tech international success requires a supportive public science infrastructure and open institutional governance \[cite: 12, 29\].

Empirical Indicators of Coherence and Systemic Synergy

The comparative analysis of national innovation systems relies on distinct empirical metrics that capture the alignment between academic science and commercial technology \[cite: 9\]. A key diagnostic indicator is the thematic coherence index, which compares the technological distribution of patent applications submitted by businesses with those submitted by academia \[cite: 9\]. High thematic overlap indicates that a country's public research institutions are successfully generating scientific knowledge aligned with the competitive needs of its domestic industrial base \[cite: 9\].

Conversely, a negative correlation between research freedom and thematic coherence in certain countries suggests a structural tension \[cite: 9\]. While high academic autonomy fosters long-term, curiosity-driven breakthroughs, it can lead to a divergence from short-term industrial applications, requiring policy interventions to bridge the gap \[cite: 9, 12\].

The Political Economy of Failure and Wealth Concentration: Reconciling Private and Public Outcomes

The evaluation of success and failure in a national innovation system requires separating private commercial returns from public socio-economic welfare \[cite: 10\]. The evolutionary processes that drive industrial dynamics do not seek neoclassical optimality or static equilibrium \[cite: 14, 17\]. Instead, they function through non-equilibrium dynamics where private losses frequently generate public goods, and private gains can impose public costs \[cite: 10, 29\].

The Social Utility of Startup Failure

In a healthy national innovation system, a high rate of startup failure is not a sign of systemic dysfunction, but a necessary vehicle of evolutionary experimentation and collective learning \[cite: 12, 36\]. Because the future commercial utility of new technologies is highly uncertain, the economy must test a vast array of competing hypotheses through market entry \[cite: 12, 14\]. The collapse of individual startups serves several vital functions for the wider innovation ecosystem:

#### Tacit Knowledge Spillovers:

Failed startups function as decentralized R&D laboratories \[cite: 29\]. The knowledge gained through their operational failures—including identifying non-viable technological pathways—spills over to the broader scientific and entrepreneurial community, preventing duplicative societal investments \[cite: 10, 37\].

#### Reallocation of Scarce Human Resources:

The bankruptcy of a venture releases highly skilled engineers, programmers, and project managers back into the labor pool \[cite: 29\]. These individuals carry valuable experience and cross-pollinate surviving firms, enhancing the aggregate absorptive capacity of the economy \[cite: 5, 29\].

#### De-risking and Cost Reduction for Successors:

The physical assets, software code, and intellectual property of failed ventures are liquidated and acquired by other firms at deep discounts, lowering capital barriers for subsequent waves of innovators \[cite: 10, 12\].

If governments intervene to artificially suppress startup failures, they risk creating institutional rigidities, trapping capital in unproductive trajectories, and blocking Schumpeterian creative destruction \[cite: 12, 29\].

The Externalities of Wealth Concentration

Conversely, the extreme financial success of a small number of private individuals can introduce severe systemic distortions \[cite: 10\]. While high financial returns are necessary to incentivize high-risk investments, extreme wealth concentration can lead to structural imbalances \[cite: 10, 29\]:

#### Wage Polarization and Inequality:

Skill-biased technological innovations disproportionately reward capital owners and elite knowledge workers, driving wage inequality and creating socio-geographic polarization between high-tech hubs and lagging regions \[cite: 10\].

#### Regulatory Capture and Entry Barriers:

Monopolistic rents allow successful incumbents to capture regulatory frameworks, lobby for protective intellectual property laws, or acquire emerging rivals, thereby freezing the dynamics of creative destruction \[cite: 10, 29\].

#### Societal Alienation:

When the gains of technological progress are highly concentrated while the social disruptions (such as structural unemployment and labor displacement) are socialized, the political consensus required to fund public scientific infrastructure and maintain open borders can collapse \[cite: 10, 29\].

The Pathologies of Top-Down Triple Helix Models

Recognizing these trade-offs exposes the limitations of standard, top-down Triple Helix models that rely on formal collaborations among governments, universities, and large corporate incumbents \[cite: 15, 38\]. Critical scholarship on regional innovation systems highlights two systemic pathologies:

#### Venture-Centric Bureaucratic Alienation:

Traditional Triple Helix models are often macro-level, bureaucratic concepts that exclude the individual entrepreneur and innovator \[cite: 38\]. Structured assessments indicate that scientists and early-stage entrepreneurs frequently feel alienated by, or actively avoid, top-down government programs, viewing them as slow, compliance-heavy, and disconnected from the rapid pacing of venture creation \[cite: 38\].

#### The Pathology of "Successful Failure":

In many regional ecosystems, Triple Helix organizations degenerate into instances of "successful failure" \[cite: 39\]. In these scenarios, the hybrid institutions established to foster collaboration (such as technology transfer offices or science parks) continue to secure public funding, publish reports, and meet bureaucratic survival goals, while completely failing to generate commercially viable startups or self-sustaining industrial clusters \[cite: 39\]. This occurs when partners enter collaborations to extract public subsidies or fulfill institutional mandates rather than to pursue genuine, risk-bearing innovation, underscoring the need for rigorous transparency and credible sanctions against rent-seeking behavior \[cite: 39\].

Strategic Implications for Environmental Governance and Policy Design

To resolve these systemic frictions, modern technology policy is shifting from traditional, market-failure-correcting frameworks to transformative and inclusive innovation policies \[cite: 10, 40, 41\]. This perspective emphasizes that public authorities must manage not only the

of innovation, but also its

\[cite: 10\].

#### Systemic governance should seek to optimize three core dimensions:

#### Transformative Mission Orientation:

Rather than funding generic commercial software or low-risk digital applications that yield high private wealth but low societal spillover, public R&D portfolios should target complex, long-term deep-tech challenges \[cite: 30, 42, 43\]. This requires mobilizing directed public funding to de-risk pioneering breakthroughs in renewable energy, healthcare, and sustainable agriculture, effectively crowding-in private venture finance to address critical social needs \[cite: 42\].

#### Inclusive Integration of Grassroots Innovation:

Policies must actively support diverse, non-R&D learning processes, including frugal innovation, pro-poor initiatives, and grassroots citizen-led developments \[cite: 10, 40\]. This is achieved by creating "Quadruple Helix" frameworks that formally integrate civil society, media, and local public participation with traditional industry-academia-government spheres \[cite: 18, 40\]. Such integration ensures that technological solutions are socially embedded, responsive to localized needs, and structured to mitigate wage inequality \[cite: 10, 40\].

#### Agile Ecosystemic Structures:

To prevent systemic lock-in and the pathology of bureaucratic "successful failure," governments must design flexible institutional frameworks \[cite: 14, 39\]. This includes adopting the Helix organizational model—characterized by dynamic networks of teams that form and disband fluidly—which enables public institutions to operate with the agility of startups while maintaining systemic stability \[cite: 44\]. Furthermore, policies should incentivize international scientific mobility and cross-border research collaborations, preventing regional inward-looking biases and enhancing the overall resilience of the national knowledge base \[cite: 28, 29\].

Ultimately, the strength of a national innovation system is determined by its institutional capacity for continuous adaptation and self-organization \[cite: 14, 19\]. By maintaining a stable, competitive macroeconomic environment, investing heavily in basic public science, and establishing transparent, inclusive governance structures, states can build robust innovation ecosystems where both private managerial excellence and public social welfare can co-evolve \[cite: 10, 12\].
