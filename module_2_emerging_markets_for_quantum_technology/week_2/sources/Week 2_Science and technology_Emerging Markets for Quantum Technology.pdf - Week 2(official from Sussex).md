2.1 Science

As an initial approximation and first working definition, basic research can be defined as

a systemic process of generating knowledge, data and explanations about nature and human-made artefacts. It is methodical in its empiricism and systematic in how it collects data, tests hypotheses, and draws unbiased conclusions from that evidence. It is generalisable to related settings, and replicable in the sense that other scientists with the appropriate training and technology would reproduce the research process and come up with identical or similar findings.

Scientific research is often peer reviewed, meaning that it is (anonymously) evaluated by scientific peers before it is published. This peer-review process hopefully helps ensure quality and that research conclusions are robust, accurate and reliable. Science is characterised by openness to revision and refinement, so that theories and frameworks are modified as new evidence emerges. As a result, scientific knowledge evolves over time and new frameworks join the scientific toolbox. Scientific research is usually performed in, or near, universities.

Finally, science is collaborative and cumulative, so that research builds on other researchers’ work to make faster progress towards new discoveries and insights.

We will elaborate on the traditional definition of science by looking at its outputs, the growth of science, its unique properties, its norms, and its dynamics.

The outputs of science

The outputs of scientific research are varied. One key output is academic papers or articles that are typically published in peer-reviewed journals.

Peer review is also used to award grants that fund research. Academics have to go through a formal process of grant writing, which is reviewed by peers within the community, who advise panels on the quality, novelty and robustness of proposed research.

#### Other valuable outputs of scientific research include:

The growth of science

One feature of science that is important to understand is that it is rapidly expanding. Science is currently growing at about 8–9% a year; in other words, it doubles every nine years or so.

under 1% a year up until the middle of the 18th century 2–3% a year from 1900 to the 1940s 8–9% a year from the 1940s to 2012.

However, exponential growth can’t go on forever on a finite planet. Derek de

Solla Price \[2\] suggested that science must eventually reach a steady state

where this exponential growth slows down and stops.

This growth has seen increasing specialisation in science, and as Adam Smith

predicted \[3\], an increase in scientific productivity.

Popper: What sets science apart?

What makes science different from other forms of knowledge? Karl Popper \[5\]

tried to explain. He proposed that science progresses through trial and error (conjecture and refutation), so the currently best theories survive. His ideas exploit an asymmetry between lots of correct predictions never proving a theory, while a single counterexample will falsify it. Hence, he argued, good science is testable and falsifiable: clear, simple, it explains more, without ad hoc modifications.

Later, he made this dynamic – not just ‘is a theory falsifiable?’, but ‘is a theory more falsifiable than its rivals?’. A theory is judged better if it falsifies cautious conjectures or confirms bold ones.

Popper largely overlooks the steps from perceptual experiences to statements, which depend on implicit low-level theories. As a result, explicit scientific theories are not tested in isolation.

If theories are falsified, we can: a) reject a theory; b) reject an observation; or c) modify assumptions. To some, this opens up relativism (maybe), but repeated constraints make finding theory harder, not easier.

Lakatos‘s \[6\] research programmes addressed these problems with Popper’s

work and argued that core assumptions form a negative heuristic beyond challenge. A positive heuristic defines how research should be done.

#### The norms of science are:

Communism (or communalism): this norm emphasises the importance of sharing scientific knowledge and data openly and freely as part of a collective enterprise. The progress of science depends on scientists’ ability to build on each other’s work. Scientific findings should therefore be made public, and scientists should share their data and methods if asked. Universalism: this norm states that scientific knowledge should be judged based on its merit (quality of evidence and robustness of the methods used), not on the personal biases, or prejudices about the race, gender, nationality or social status, of the scientist who produced it. Disinterestedness: this norm requires scientists to conduct research in an objective and impartial manner, without being influenced by personal interests, financial gain or other external factors. They should be motivated by a desire to uncover new knowledge, rather than personal ambition or profit. Organised scepticism: this norm requires scientists to be critical of all scientific claims, including their own. As scientific knowledge is constantly evolving, no theory is ever completely proven. Scientists must always be open to new evidence and be willing to revise their theories in light of new findings.

These norms form the acronym CUDOS (using both ‘O’ and ‘S’ from ‘Organised scepticism’) and bear a certain similarity to the ancient Greek term ‘kydos’, from which we derive the term ‘kudos’.

Science is progressive, cumulative and impartial, undertaken by people socialised into the norms of professional communities. These norms provide the standards and practices needed to generate and evaluate knowledge claims. Norms provide the social regulations that bind the scientific community together.

Kuhn: The dynamics of science

Thomas Kuhn developed a theory of scientific revolutions to explain the dynamics of science. According to Kuhn, science goes through periods of stability, which he called ‘normal science’, followed by periods of revolutionary

science and upheaval, which he called ‘paradigm shifts’ \[8\].

Kuhn argued that science is socialised into paradigms – shared ways of solving problems (methods, values and frameworks), based on a shared set of assumptions, values and practices. A paradigm represents a consensus among scientists about the world and how it should be studied.

This paradigm shift changes how scientists understand the world and conduct research, and brings new assumptions, values and practices.

Kuhn argued that these paradigm shifts are not purely rational and are driven by social, cultural and psychological factors. This is because the ways in which you judge quality are often defined within a paradigm, making comparisons across paradigms very difficult, if not impossible.

For Kuhn \[8\], normal science uses a paradigm’s tools to solve problems, while revolutionary science changes the underlying cognitive framework and the

tools themselves (making new theories often incommensurate with the old ones). According to Kuhn, science isn’t progressive: it moves away from older

science, not towards the truth \[8\].

The Stanford School

Philosophers of science working at Stanford have continued to explore what

science is. For example, Ian Hacking, in his book Representing and Intervening,

criticised previous work for focusing too much on theory and knowledge \[9\]. He

suggested that scientists don’t just think about and describe the world; they also modify it to create new phenomena (like laser technology that didn’t exist before). Hacking argued that a lot of what modern physics involves is the creation of new phenomena and ‘effects’. This is an important insight that is useful for understanding the links between research and the commercialisation of quantum technologies. Basic research produces new ‘effects’ and the commercialisation process finds new uses for them and turns them into new products.

Hacking also highlighted that science has different styles that have emerged through its history. These styles define shared ways of arguing about evidence and showing evidence in public scientific settings. These styles are:

mathematical postulation and proof experiment hypothetical modelling taxonomy statistical historical derivation of genetic development.

Each style has new objects of study, evidence and truth criteria (and realism debates). Since truth exists within a style, styles are partially self-authenticating,

even if theories don’t. So, quantum phenomena may continue to exist, even if there is a revolution in theory and a new theory of quantum mechanics is found.

Changes in science

At the start of the 20th century, when quantum mechanics was first developed, science was very different from how it is today. Scientists were usually men and were seen as lone geniuses who stood out against the tide of conventional thinking. This was a time of the liberal theory of science, as articulated by two physical chemists – Michael Polanyi at

the University of Manchester \[10\] and James Bryant Conant at Harvard \[11\].

#### The basic elements of this theory of the small-scale science were:

1. Scientists were autonomous – and the only people qualified to judge which research problems were worth exploring.

2. Funding for research was small-scale and came from local sources, such as university department budgets.

3. Scientists were interdependent and used informal processes to validate each other’s work.

4. Science was self-policing and governed within networks of personal connections.

In the post-war period, after the Manhattan project, science became much larger – it became Big Science. The growing costs meant that the scientific establishment needed to justify expenditure. They argued that it was inherently unpredictable and that, as a result of this unpredictability, we should just fund the best scientists and benefits will naturally arise for society.

20th-century Big Science

Grants in this new system were competitively awarded, based on the judgements of peer reviewers, who looked at the past performance of scientists and inferred their future prospects. As science grew, teams mattered more and there was more attention paid to ensuring staff could be funded over time. Science became a system in which financial and other inputs needed to be linked to outputs to justify the larger amounts of money involved. This led to a growing focus on metrics such as citation scores, which were unknown to the previous generation.

This change – increased size, costs, needs for justification, and new ways of measuring

science – led to what Stephen Turner and Daryl Chubin \[12\] called ‘a new ethic of

productivity’. New justifications for military and industrial funding linked inputs to outputs. And new managers took a greater role in co-ordinating scientific research.

jobs were emerging, and they were typically high-paying. This created a new focus on ‘impact’. Scientists were less free to select what they wanted to work on, and it is possible that risk-taking declined as budgets grew. Research increasingly focused on external problems, in industry and society. The focus of research changed to be less about fundamental understanding and more about prediction and manipulation.

The new norms of science

Within this new system, the norms of science changed. In the UK, there is much more attention on impact and showing that research has generated benefits for society and the economy. Scientists are more likely to work with industry. Industry contributes both new problems and a market for new solutions that emerge from research.

The new norms of science that replaced Merton’s CUDOS norms were from John Ziman

\[13\] and can be represented as PLACE:

Proprietary: the products are owned by funders and universities, and not necessarily communal and shared. Local: as a norm, scientists are not concerned about general problems. Authoritarian: managerial hierarchies are followed rather than individual academic freedom. Commissioned: specific problems to be solved are targeted. Expert: expertise is more important than creativity.

As science has changed – its funding, hierarchy, organisation, relationship to the public and politics – its norms have changed too. 2.2 Technology

Technology is something that is very common and surrounds us as we go about our day-to-day activities. But what is it? As we shall see, technology is very complex.

This interactive material will help you understand the main reading this week, ‘What is

technology?’ (Nightingale \[14\]), which is one of the most difficult readings you will

encounter in this module. It will also help you to understand why technological knowledge is partly tacit, uncertain, value-laden, costly to develop, inherently experimental, localised, socially embedded, firm-specific and path-dependent.

Defining technology

As a starting definition, technology is both physical artefacts and person-embodied knowledge needed to design, develop, test and make artefacts that perform useful functions.

In many ways, science and technology are similar. They both emerge from experimental activities and are both performed by scientists and engineers. Large firms develop technology but can also spend heavily on basic research. Industrial research and development (R&D) scientists publish papers, make discoveries, and sometimes get Nobel prizes. In some fields – such as quantum biology and molecular biology – scientific research has a very large influence on the development of technology.

Science and technology: Similarities and differences

However, while science and technology overlap significantly, they also have different

purposes. As Keith Pavitt \[15\] noted:

‘One of the main purposes of academic research is to produce codified theories and models that explain and predict natural reality. To achieve analytical tractability, this requires simplification and reduction of the number of variables (for example, “Under laboratory conditions...”, “Other things being equal...”).

‘On the other hand, the main purpose of business R&D is to design and develop producible and useful artefacts. These are often complex, involving numerous components, materials, performance constraints and interactions, and are therefore analytically intractable (in other words, theory and formal models are a weak guide to, and predictor of, practice). Knowledge is therefore accumulated through trial and error.’

These differences in purpose lead to differences in the extent to which science and technology are exposed to the full complexity of the real world. Technologies need to function reliably, for a wide range of users, in a messy, dynamic, complex outside world. Engineers are legally responsible for products, and they have to understand their environment.

Engineers focus on what works reliably rather than what is new and untested. They require a broad understanding, rather than very specialised knowledge. Because costs of failure are typically high, they normally rely on a legal process of accreditation, based on an established and formalised body of knowledge, to ensure the quality of their work.

Scientists need to produce new knowledge that is often from simplified environments that have been protected from the complexity of the outside world – in other words, laboratory conditions. They are not legally held responsible for their results being robust. They don’t need to understand applications and uses of their discoveries. They focus on what is new, not on what works reliably. They are very specialised.

The dual nature of technology

There is a difference between features that are intrinsic (the mass of an electron) and

imposed (‘van Gough’s Sunflowers is a great painting’), depending on whether they are

dependent or independent of people. If people didn’t exist, electrons would still have a mass. However, if people didn’t exist, there would be no-one to assert anything about the quality of paintings.

Technologies’ functions are imposed rather than having intrinsic properties. This is why the same artefact can be used to perform multiple functions – for example, a CD can store data or stop a hot coffee cup marking the table. What it functions as depends on our decisions. These imposed functions, and not the technology’s intrinsic physics,

define what a technology is. As the philosopher John Searle \[16\] noted: a safety valve is

still a safety valve even if it malfunctions and fails to stop an explosion.

This implies that part of what a technology is exists in our shared understanding. This is easy to see with a technology such as money. Dollar bills only have value because we collectively believe they have value. The value of a dollar bill is not because of its intrinsic physics. In fact, a perfect replica would be valueless counterfeit money.

This tacit nature has important consequences:  technical knowledge is partly non-codified, is typically passed on through face-to-face interactions, and is person-embodied, socially embedded, localised, and historically path dependent.

A different fit between mind and world

Science and technology differ in their ‘direction of fit’ between mind and world.

Scientific theories, explanations and so on, are meant to be true, and match how the world is. When they don’t match, scientists change their ideas to match the world. When we build technologies, on the other hand, we start with an idea (typically in the form of a design) and change the world to match that idea. When technologies malfunction, we repair them, and so change the world to match the idea (design) about how they should behave.

#### This difference has a number of important consequences:

Science can be true or false: technologies never are. Technology’s functions relate to subjective normative frameworks – we can judge a stone to be a better or worse paperweight, but not a better or worse stone. Scientific knowledge is objective, but technological functions are inherently social and subjective. Designs (like desires) can only be for the future. Theories (like statements) can apply in the past.

Differences in causes and effects

As a simple approximation, scientific knowledge allows you to (imperfectly) predict unknown end results (effects) from known starting conditions (causes). On the other hand, when we are developing technology, we typically start with known, desired end results. We may want to build a quantum computer. But we won’t know at the start the actual final design of it. At the start of the innovation process, we do not know the ideal starting conditions that will generate our desired outcomes.

Science doesn’t always provide answers to questions about which unknown starting conditions will generate a known and desired end result. Technology is therefore a distinct body of knowledge from science. In fact, you can produce technology without scientific input. The Wright brothers were flying before aerodynamics, and steam engines were common before thermodynamics. Today, there are lots of drugs on the market that work, even though the exact biochemical mechanisms that cause them to work are still unknown.

Due to the differences we have examined thus far, for most of history, science and technology have been largely distinct.

R&D spending is mainly two-thirds development rather than research. Hence, it is misleading to think of technology as applied science, or to think that innovation always requires science or necessarily comes from science.

If technology doesn’t come from science, where does it come from? Technology is

produced using what Polanyi calls ‘operational principles’ \[17\] \[17\]. These take the form

‘x can be produced with y’, based on the assumption that ‘similar problems will have similar solutions’ . Unlike scientific theories, technical solutions are inherently uncertain and therefore require cycles of empirical testing. These redesign cycles add to the cost and time of innovation (and can spread in complex technologies).

Applying operational principles creates a new sub-problem (how to create y) that is solved by new operational principles. This then creates a hierarchy of inter-related problem-solving tasks in turn. These tasks feed into each other and are constrained in the order in which they can be solved, and how they define an ‘innovation process’.

Science and technology interactions

For most of history, technology has been too complex, and science too simple, for them to overlap much.  Over time, as science has improved, it has been able to address more technical problems, and innovation in the 20th century became much more scientific.

Even today, science and technology are largely distinct. Scientific papers, for example, mainly cite scientific papers, while patents mainly cite patents. Only about 10% of patent citations are to scientific papers, which is much less than the 17% universities contribute to R&D.

However, research is important. Innovation and science share a common infrastructure of tacit skills, instrumentation, networks, and so on. Basic research contributes to

technology through the movement of people, with the person-embodied capacity to solve complex problems. This is probably more important than the transfer of

information in scientific papers.

2.3 Implications

Technology and science are distinct. As we noted previously, you can know how to produce an effect without knowing how an effect is produced.

The production of technology involves the integration of multiple components and subsystems, which often exhibit unpredictable behaviour that cannot be fully explained by theory. Science generated in atypical laboratory conditions may not be applicable in real-world scenarios. As a result, the behaviour of technology cannot be predicted from

knowledge to address the complex design, development and production problems that emerge. Innovation is largely empirical, experimental and advances in incremental steps.

Because theory is a weak guide to practice, the knowledge of how to generate outcomes and where to look for improvements is partly tacit. And because innovation draws on tacit, person-embodied, problem-solving skills and knowledge, it is hard to transfer. The tacit nature of technical knowledge means that it is typically geographically and linguistically localised, embodied in institutions and individuals, and transmitted through face-to-face, personal contacts (often over extended periods). The technical knowledge necessary for innovation is specific to particular technologies, complex, and cumulative. As a result, it varies by firm, products and processes.

Firm-specific capabilities

While science is increasingly important to innovation, it is not freely available. Firms need to build research capabilities in order to understand science. Consequently, firms invest in research as an admission ticket to global science and technology networks.

The cognitive complexity of technology implies organisational complexity, requiring firms to have the capabilities to experiment and integrate external and internal knowledge. When firms produce new innovation, they need to match uncertain technology to customers’ (changing) needs.

If firms invest successfully in experimenting and are able to accumulate the resulting knowledge, they can potentially use this knowledge to understand how to co-ordinate innovation more effectively in the future. This has the potential to reduce the costs, time and risks of innovation.

Capabilities in diverse technologies

Firms are active in a broader range of technologies than just their products. They also have to be able to develop and use process technology to produce their products. So, while a quantum firm will have capabilities in relation to quantum, they will also need capabilities in fabrication technologies, and so forth. We see a lot of diversity in the technologies that firms are active in across sectors. When we look at where firms patent, we see that firms develop new capabilities very slowly, and the ones they have are stable over time.

On the other hand, we see limited diversity in technologies among firms in the same sectors that are making the same products. For example, Pfizer and GSK patent in similar areas, as do Boeing and Airbus. It seems that some technological capabilities that we pick up when we look at where firms patent amount to technological imperatives –

determines what they can make and where they look.

The technological activities that firms undertake seem to be strongly constrained and differentiated. This suggests that the feasible technological choices open to firms depend heavily on what the firms already do. So, pharmaceutical firms may move into speciality chemicals but not into automobiles. IBM is likely to find it easier to build a quantum computer than Airbus.

Given that firms in the same sector often patent in similar areas, it seems that many large firms compete on their ability to turn similar technology into valuable products and services (and capture more value). This is a management issue and reflects a firm’s ability to collaborate across different corporate functions – such as R&D, marketing and manufacturing.

Managerial capabilities

Whether firms have unique technology or are competing on their ability to turn similar technologies into better products, managerial ability to co-ordinate is vital. Managers must make sure that resources are in place and learning is effective between people, functions and organisations:

Junior managers – co-ordinate learning among the teams within a particular function. Middle managers – co-ordinate learning across functions within the organisation. Senior managers – co-ordinate across the organisation and position the firm within its wider institutional and regulatory context.

Cognitive capabilities

As products and the processes to build them get more complex, more knowledge is needed. This growing cognitive complexity has implications for organisational complexity, as more knowledge from more people needs to be integrated. Firms need capabilities to both integrate knowledge internally and link to and integrate knowledge from wider external sources. Firms also need capacity to experiment and learn across functional boundaries.

Firms may rely more on external institutions that develop and diffuse technological knowledge.  Such institutions may include their suppliers and customers, professional networks and trade bodies, and public education and research organisations.

#### This cognitive and organisational complexity has the following implications:

Learning within firms is path-dependent (the past defines and constrains the future). There are significant advantages to physical proximity, as the knowledge being transferred and integrated across complex boundaries within and between the firm

This need to integrate and co-ordinate knowledge can create major problems for ‘corporate coherence’.

This week we have explored the changing nature of science and highlighted how technology is produced by a distinct body of knowledge that in the past was largely independent of science. In the 20th century, however, science changed and now increasingly contributes towards innovation. Science increasingly focuses on understanding technology and generating ‘effects’ that can be exploited for new purposes.

However, theory is only a weak guide to practice. R&D is still mainly development and innovation is mainly a long, uncertain, largely experimental process that draws on tacit understanding of technology. So, while quantum technologies will typically draw on advanced science, they also require technical skills and knowledge, particularly around design, project management and production.

Innovation is a process of matching changing technologies to changing customers’ needs. It is inherently uncertain because it is hard to accurately predict how technologies will behave and to know what customers want, and how other firms and a changing external environment will influence the process.

If firms can build and co-ordinate the knowledge they need to innovate, then in theory, they can reduce the risks to innovation, and therefore its costs and time. To do this, they need to build the managerial and organisational skills needed to co-ordinate knowledge within the firm, and connect to, and exploit, external sources of knowledge.
