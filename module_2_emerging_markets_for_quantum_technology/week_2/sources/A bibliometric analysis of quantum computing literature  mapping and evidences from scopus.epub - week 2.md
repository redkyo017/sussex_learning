# A bibliometric analysis of quantum computing literature  mapping and evidences from scopus.epub - week 2
1. Introduction

Quantum information technology is the next-generation technology for future human society and may forever change our economic, industrial, academic, and social landscape (Lekitsch et al.2017). The field comprises four domains: quantum communication, quantum simulation, quantum computation, and quantum sensing and metrology (Acín et al.2018). Quantum computing achieves large computational space by orchestrating the superposition, entanglement and interference of quantum bits (qubits), and has the potential to surpass any classical computer (Arute et al.2019; Harrow and Montanaro2017; Oliver2019). It has great potential to solve some of the most challenging computational tasks and explore many possibilities that classical processors cannot handle (Nguyen et al.2020). A good example is that quantum algorithms and artificial intelligence hold good promise for mutual reinforcement: quantum algorithms can enhance machine learning in data clustering, while machine learning can be used to perform quantum state tomography of highly entangled states with more than a hundred qubits (Palmieri et al.2020; Torlai et al.2018). Experimental demonstrations of quantum computing date back to the 1980s, when Charles H. Bennett discussed the logical reversibility of computation (Bennett1973), Paul Benioff proposed building a quantum mechanical model of computers as represented by Turing machines (Benioff1980), and Richard P. Feynman proposed the idea of designing computers working according to the laws of quantum mechanics (Feynman1982). The first theoretical breakthrough with major practical implications in this technology came in 1994 when Shor of AT&T Bell Labs developed a polynomial time algorithm, a quantum algorithm capable of breaking any existing encryption (Shor1994). Later in 1996, Grover developed a fast quantum mechanical algorithm for unsorted database search (Grover1996). These two algorithms are now at the core of the entire field of quantum algorithm research.

Scientists have implemented architectures for quantum computing on various material systems such as superconducting circuits, semiconductor quantum wells, single-photon emitters, trapped ions, topological quantum computing elements, and other hybrid quantum systems (Forn-Díaz et al.2019; Liu and Hersam2019). Currently, the US and China have achieved quantum of advantage in superconducting and photons architectures, respectively (Arute et al.2019; Zhong et al.2021), and the latest effort realised a quantum spin model with tunable interactions for system sizes ranging from 64 to 256 qubits on a programmable quantum simulator (Ebadi et al.2021). However, even the current state-of-the-art systems have limitations in terms of size and coherence (coherence means the states of electron spin to the right and to the left are correlated, and can be created, manipulated, and measured at the single-quantum level) (DiVincenzo and Loss1999). What is more, we are still some time away from fault-tolerant quantum computers, which require error correction mechanisms to be incorporated into the computing and storage components to ensure quantum coherence and low error rates (Setia et al.2020). The latest research has taken a big step forward in this direction by achieving exponential suppression of bit or phase errors through cyclic error correction (Chen et al.2021). The development of quantum systems capable of performing practical calculations or simulations, like those that implement Shor’s algorithm, requires a deep understanding of the dynamics of quantum correlations, entanglement and information scrambling in quantum many-body systems, such as Nash embedding that allows for faultless traversal of the quantum-classical physical divide (Khan and Humble2019; Lewis-Swan et al.2019), and the integration of hardware, software, cloud access, benchmarks, and error correction and mitigation (Corcoles et al.2020).

Many countries are pursuing strategies and research plans in quantum science and technology, such as the US, which announced a massive investment to ensure its global competitiveness (U.S. Department of Energy,2020). China has proposed fully understanding the importance and urgency of promoting quantum science and technology and strengthening the strategic planning and systematic layout of quantum information technology. IBM promises to achieve a quantum computer with 1000 qubits by 2023, while Google plans to build a quantum computer with one million qubits within ten years (Biamonte, Dorozhkin, and Zacharov2019). However, the scientific community still has doubts about the prospects of quantum computing. Many believe that quantum computing still has considerable hurdles to overcome before it can be used for some of the most challenging computing tasks, such as 5G communications, power grid management, smart factory optimisation, and drug discovery (‘A Precarious Milestone for Quantum Computing,’2019). For this reason, a comprehensive and in-depth analysis of the current global development status of quantum computing is urgently needed.

Although there have been several bibliometric studies on quantum computing, they are rather outdated and do not reflect the latest academic trends. What is more, the predominant error for these previous studies lies in their flawed search methods, such as using (‘quantum computing’) as the sole keyword (Dhawan, Gupta, and Bhusan2018), or a search query of (quantum NEAR/5 comput\* or quantum NEAR/15 algorithm\* or quantum NEAR/10 simulat\* or quantum NEAR/10 error\* or ‘quantum circuit’ or ‘quantum Turing machine’) (Tian et al.2020). Another research was confined to four thematic journals in the SCIE database (Terekhov2020). The limitations of these studies lead to unreliable and incomplete findings that do not truly reflect the development of quantum computing worldwide.

Based on the in-depth understanding of the technical substance of quantum computing, we have made improvements in the search method to comprehensively reflect the global development of quantum computing. Then multiple metrics were used to analyse (i) research trends and hotspots; (ii) the regional distribution of authors, institutions, and countries, and the characteristics of international collaboration, academic-corporate collaboration, and research excellence. The results can provide ideas and suggestions for future researches and policymaking and help promote quantum science development. The main contributions of this paper include four aspects: (1) The global research hotspots of quantum computing have been identified, and the latest development trends of relevant hotspots have been summarised; (2) Determining the academic output pattern in quantum computing, including the distribution of scholars, institutions, and countries/regions, and found that quantum computing is still under development and has not yet become the mainstream hotspot of academic development; (3) The characteristics of international cooperation and research impact of quantum computing were evaluated by multiple metrics; (4) Most active sources journals and top articles with the highest citation count identified.

2. Literature review

Due to the need for research on optical quantum computing, quantum key distribution, quantum metrology, and quantum networks, scientists are vigorously investigating semiconductor quantum dots in various material systems and developing solid state single photon emitters to manipulate single photons (Arakawa and Holmes2020). In photonics-based quantum computing, photonic qubits are created and manipulated with high precision using interferometry for computational applications and data transmission. Photonic synthetic materials use superconducting qubits and cavities to manipulate the quantum state of microwave photons, thus provide a means of tailoring the interaction between light and matter (Flamini, Spagnolo, and Sciarrino2019). Tremendous success has been achieved in the ability to generate large numbers of photons, improved light sources and detectors, and novel noise-resistant theoretical approaches. The main issues are optical losses, large scaling, matching of different photonic elements, and near-deterministic generation of the photon as a flying qubit (Carusotto et al.2020; Elshaari et al.2020). Integrated quantum photonic circuits are a particularly desirable technology platform. In December 2020, Zhong et al. obtained up to 76-photon coincidence on a photonic quantum computer, yielding a state space dimension of about 10 30 (Zhong et al.2021).

Trapped ions are one of the most promising systems for practical quantum computing, in which ions confined in radiofrequency traps serve as qubits, with entanglement achieved by using the shared ion motional modes as a quantum bus (Bruzewicz et al.2019). Trapped ions have fundamental advantages over superconducting qubits, such as high qubit quality, coherence, and connectivity (Murali et al.2020). The main challenges for trapped ions are how to generate entanglement between spin and photonic quantum bits efficiently and increase the scale of quantum computers while reducing decoherence and control errors (Gao et al.2012). An 11-qubit quantum computer in a trapped ion system was developed in 2019, with single-qubit gate fidelities of 99.5%, average two-qubit-gate fidelities of 97.5%, and SPAM errors of 0.7% (Wright et al.2019). A two-dimensional Penning trap arrays was developed as a scalable platform for quantum computing with trapped atomic ions to allow for fault-tolerant scale devices (Jain et al.2020). An exact protocol has been devised that simultaneously entangles arbitrary pairs of qubits on a trapped-ion quantum computer and exhibits an exponential improvement in both classical and quantum resources (Grzesiak et al.2020). The trap sizing, communication topology, and operation implementation methods need to be further improved to realise a 50–100 qubits quantum charge-coupled devices-based trapped ion system (Murali et al.2020).

Quantum-state engineering in Josephson junction based electronic circuits has shown great promise for quantum computation and communication (Makhlin, Schön, and Shnirman2001; Nakamura, Pashkin, and Tsai1999). Superconducting qubits are manipulated by using electromagnetic pulses to control the magnetic flux, the electric charge, or the phase difference across a Josephson junction (Clarke and Wilhelm2008). Over the past two decades, superconducting quantum circuits have evolved from basic research to the engineering of large-scale superconducting quantum systems, with core concepts and challenges including qubits design, noise properties, qubit control and readout techniques (Krantz et al.2019). Key experimental breakthroughs have been made in thermal interferometers, diffractors, modulators and routers based on superconducting tunnel junctions (Hwang and Sothmann2020). Circuit quantum electrodynamics (QED) implements the fundamental requirements of quantum computing in a single architecture. Recent research directions for creating circuit QED based hybrid quantum systems include mechanical oscillators, quantum acoustodynamics with surface acoustic waves, quantum magnonics, and coupling between superconducting circuits and ensembles or single spins (Blais, Girvin, and Oliver2020; Clerk et al.2020). Quantum computing platforms based on superconducting qubits have emerged as a leading candidate for scalable high-fidelity quantum processor architectures and achieved the milestone of proving quantum superiority by creating quantum states on 53 qubits for the first time in 2019 (Arute et al.2019). Experimental efforts towards the large-scale superconducting quantum computer include qubit design, quantum control, readout techniques, and the implementations of error correction and quantum algorithms (Huang et al.2020).

Further development of quantum computing requires advances in materials science in selecting, synthesis, fabrication, simulation, and characterisation of nanomaterials underlying quantum devices (Richardson et al.2020). Nanodiamond can be used to fabricate materials with photonic bandgaps with properties to suppress spontaneous light emission and improve light harvesting, which can be used for applications such as optical waveguides, filters, laser resonators, and colour-centered quantum devices (Chia et al.2021; Kan et al.2020). Recent researches focus on nanodiamond synthesis, such as the growth of colour centre-rich nanodiamond on prefabricated silicon carbide microdisk resonators to enhance diamond colour-centered light emission (Radulaski et al.2019), wafer-scale preparation of nanoscale diamond containing optically active silicon vacancy centres (Nelz et al.2019), and self-assembling colloidal diamond capable of optical properties of photonic bandgaps (He et al.2020).

Then, SciVal was used to further analyse the 28,412 peer-reviewed papers from 2016 to 2020, to obtain the current academic output, including the distribution between countries, institutions, and authors, with emphasis on development trends and research hotspots. SciVal is a research management and analysis platform launched by Elsevier, with data sources from Scopus, the world’s largest abstract and citation database (Cantú-Ortiz and Fangmeyer2017). Scopus is more comprehensive in its coverage of computer science publications and has a series of bibliometric metrics that can analyse the current status, hotspots, and trends in the research field, help researchers discover core teams and institutions (Liu et al.2020; Martín-Martín et al.2018).

We employed multiple metrics of the Scopus: Scholarly output, Field-Weighted Citation Impact (FWCI), Publications in Top Journal Percentiles, Output in Top Citation Percentiles, Patent-citations per scholarly output, Publications in Top Journal Percentiles. These metrics were extracted from SciVal, and each indicator and its bibliometric significance will be explained below.

The scholarly output indicates the number of publications and measures academic productivity. Scholars publish their research results during their tenure, but the number of publications does not reflect their scientific impact. Other critical dimensions include journal impact, scientific impact, and social popularity (Liu et al.2016).

Citations vary spatiotemporally and are affected by differences in research fields, and need to be normalised (Gao et al.2013). As an article-level field-normalised metric, FWCI indicates how the number of citations received by an entity’s publications compares with the world average. It benchmarks the comparative research impact of entities regardless of differences in their size, disciplinary profile, age, and publication-type composition (Chang, Phoa, and Nakano2019; Purkayastha et al.2019)

Two metrics, publications in top journal percentiles and outputs in top citation percentiles, are often used to illustrate research excellence. As a result of the editors’ selection of the best manuscripts, publications in top journal percentiles often show top citation performance within their fields (Bornmann et al.2011). The metrics used in this study are publications in the top 10% journal percentiles by citescore percentile (%) (abbreviated as JP10%) and output in top 10% citation percentiles (%) (abbreviated as CP10%).

The economic impact indicators can quantify the relationship between science and invention. Patent-citations per scholarly output is the patent citation counts divided by an entity’s total scholarly output for that period and multiplied by 1000. It indicates how much, on average, research is being used to create products and provide information about research’s economic impact.

There is a positive correlation between the share of academic-corporate collaboration share (abbreviated as ACC%) in a country and the innovation performance indices (Pohl2021). The effects of international collaboration share (abbreviated as IC%) are positive and statistically significant in enhancing publication impact (Leydesdorff, Bornmann, and Wagner2019).

## Screening of leading topics

The research frontiers of quantum computing are filtered through leading topics. Active topics give insight into the research planning process and help researchers keep up with the latest research trends. The Scopus database has approximately 97,000 topics in its citation information and is ranked according to the new metric Prominence percentile. The prominence percentile is calculated from the number of citation count, Scopus view count, and journal CiteScore to determine the topic’s popularity and momentum. Topic prominence is positively correlated with research funding and grants; finding topics with a high prominence percentile can help researchers and policymakers.

In 2019, Google achieved a quantum of advantage in superconducting architecture, which was the first milestone in quantum computing in recent years, evidenced in its 948 citations (Arute et al.2019). Prior studies developed room-temperature, polarised and ultrabright single-photon emission from a colour centre in two-dimensional hexagonal boron nitride (Tran et al.2017), a bright source generating near-unity indistinguishability and pure single photons made of quantum dots in electrically controlled cavities (Somaschi et al.2016), and plasmonic nanocavities providing single-molecule strong coupling at room temperature (Chikkaraddy et al.2016). Other architectures include 51 qubits programmable Ising-type quantum spin model (Bernien et al.2017), and proximate Kitaev quantum spin liquid, which generated Marajona fermions that could be used as the basic material for quantum computers (Banerjee et al.2016). Many-body localisation, which is an important phenomenon in superconducting quantum field, has been studied in a quantum simulator with programmable random disorder (Smith et al.2016).

New quantum algorithms have been the focus of research. A quantum algorithm on superconducting quantum devices has been developed to determine the ground-state energy for molecules (Kandala et al.2017). Another article focuses on new quantum materials, monolithically integrated lithium niobate electro-optic modulators, which are useful in photonic circuits (Wang et al.2018).

5. Conclusions

This article aims to give a comprehensive and in-depth analysis of the global development status of quantum computing. To achieve this aim, we reviewed the current development status, analysed research hotspots and technological trends, and the contribution of authors, institutions, and countries in quantum computing from 2016 to 2020. We found that quantum computing is still under development, with the US and China in the leading position, from where most critical scholars came. The most important institutions are scattered in the United States, China, and France. Topics with the highest Prominence percentile, distinctive keyphrases, and leading source journals were also analysed. The research findings are expected to provide ideas and suggestions for future researches and policymaking and help promote quantum science development.

Despite the meaningful contributions that have been made in this study, there are several limitations. First, the data analysis in this study was based on Scopus, and the analysis was performed in SciVal. The research findings can be validated by comparison with other databases and bibliometric analysis tools. Second, the review section of this study is limited to only a few of the current hotspots, which is not comprehensive enough. In future works, the development status, research fronts, knowledge flow, and technology transfer and transformation within quantum computing can be further explored by citation network analysis and science paper-patent integration analysis.

The results show that although the age of quantum computing has not yet arrived, it already promises some exciting things, and many important milestones have been achieved. Noting the interdisciplinary nature of quantum science, scholars and research institutions worldwide should pay more attention to international cooperation to promote the growth of this discipline. Continual funding and investment in quantum computing are also essential. The FWCI of quantum computing indicates a relatively low research impact in the science community. By focusing on research and funding on the hotspots, promoting the cooperation between academia and corporate, and laying out the critical technologies in advance, the next generation of quantum computing technology will be developed even faster.

In conclusion, the scientific community needs further breakthroughs in quantum computing and more in-depth cooperation and communication between universities, national laboratories, companies, and governments to promote the development and fabrication of quantum hardware and software and the production of quantum control materials. This paper presents a clear blueprint of the quantum computing field in the past five years, which will help researchers and policymakers to develop a more explicit strategy for the development of quantum computing.
