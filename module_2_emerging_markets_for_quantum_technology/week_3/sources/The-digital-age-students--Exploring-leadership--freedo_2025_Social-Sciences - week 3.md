1. Introduction

The rapid integration of digital technology, including the rise of generative artificial intelligence, presents both opportunities and challenges for education, necessitating a renewed focus on fostering responsible digital citizenship—individuals who navigate the digital world ethically, critically, and effectively. While research has explored aspects of leadership and personal freedom in virtual learning environments, the complex interplay between these factors and the evolving landscape of digital responsibility, particularly in the context of AI- powered learning, remains under-explored (Hai et al., 2021; Lum, 2021; Malik, Bansal, & Tyagi, 2021; Murgia, 2024). This under-exploration is concerning given the increasing prevalence of misinformation and ethical dilemmas in digital spaces. This study examines the relationships between leadership skills, personal freedom, and digital responsibility among university students, seeking to contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of cultivating ethical digital citizenship.

The digital age reshapes leadership (Eryeşil, 2021; Kasińska-Metryka & Gajewski, 2020), making it crucial for online environments (Israfilov

et al., 2020; Nonaka, 2023). Digital leaders promote critical thinking and responsible communication (Buckingham Shum & Luckin, 2019; Creusen, 2017; Daniels et al., 2017; Sheninger, 2019), while personal freedom is essential for digital identities and engagement (Faldetta et al., 2021; Johri et al., 2023; Marshall, 2009; Young, 2017). However, this freedom must be balanced with responsibility (Filippo, 2024; Raju, 2023; Whitehead & Collier, 2022).

Research Gaps: Despite the importance of digital citizenship, two

key research gaps persist. First, existing studies underemphasize student leadership in promoting ethical online behavior (Jobin, Ienca, & Vayena, 2019; Read, 2012; Solari, 2016), primarily focusing on administrators or teachers (Pajnik & Sauer, 2017; Wang, 2023), and often overlooking student leadership potential. Second, research tends to focus on the negative consequences of unfettered autonomy (Johnston, 2015; Sternberg, 2012), without exploring the positive impact of responsible freedom.

This study seeks to address these gaps through the following research questions.

2. Literature review

The rapid growth of digital technology has reshaped society (Adeola et al., 2022; Li et al., 2018; Siebel, 2019), impacting daily life and societal structures (Lum, 2021; Hai et al., 2021; Malik, Bansal, & Tyagi, 2021). While offering opportunities, it also presents challenges, necessitating the cultivation of responsible digital citizenship—individuals who navigate the digital world ethically and effectively (Ahmad et al., 2021; Gould & Macrae, 2021; Hai et al., 2021). This requires an understanding of the factors that foster responsible digital behavior, particularly among young people (Nonaka, 2023; Ma et al., 2019; Taylor & Hogenbirk, 2001). This initial overview of the current scholarship highlights a crucial tension: while technology offers immense potential for societal advancement, its pervasive influence also necessitates a careful consideration of ethical and behavioral norms (Charan et al., 2024; Contino, 2019). The literature consistently emphasizes the need to move beyond a purely technological understanding of the digital landscape to address its social, ethical, and educational implications (Choi et al., 2018; Jobin, Matwin, & Levesque, 2019).

Educational environments present a particularly pertinent context for examining the integration of technology, given its central role in both learning and communication processes (Nonaka, 2023; Ma et al., 2019; Taylor & Hogenbirk, 2001). While a considerable body of research has investigated leadership dynamics within virtual environments (Ko & Rossen, 2017; Ma et al., 2019; Normore & Brooks, 2014; Purcell, 2017; Tillman & Scheurich, 2013), the prevailing focus tends to be on established institutional leadership roles. This emphasis often overlooks the crucial influence of student-led initiatives in shaping online learning communities. Further, a critical examination of the interplay between individual autonomy and digital responsibility remains underdeveloped in current scholarship. Although literature has explored the potential negative implications of unchecked autonomy in digital spaces (Johnston, 2015; Sternberg, 2012), there is less emphasis on how to cultivate a sense of digital responsibility alongside the opportunity to explore one’s own initiative online.

This body of research exposes a noticeable gap in our current understanding. Despite a persistent call for investigations into the effects of leadership within online contexts, this call primarily concentrates on leadership manifested by educators and administrators, neglecting the crucial leadership contributions of students themselves. Additionally, while the dangers of online freedom are frequently discussed, less emphasis has been given to the potential for that freedom to promote responsible online behavior. This scholarly gap highlights the need for further research that specifically addresses student agency and the positive manifestations of freedom in the digital realm, exploring how personal autonomy can nurture responsible online citizenship and influence leadership potential within students.

Defining and Understanding Digital Responsibility: Digital re-

sponsibility (DR) goes beyond legal compliance (Wade, 2020), encompassing ethical online behavior. While existing research highlights digital literacy and ethical practices (Trier et al., 2023; Wenzel, 2021), a

comprehensive approach considers the broader implications of online actions (Adi et al., 2015; Dörr, 2021). The concept of digital responsibility (DR) has evolved beyond mere legal compliance to encompass the ethical dimensions of online behavior (Wade, 2020). While research has explored digital literacy and ethical practices (Trier et al., 2023; Wenzel, 2021), a comprehensive understanding of DR requires considering the broader implications of online actions (Adi et al., 2015; Dörr, 2021). As such, DR is now understood as a multifaceted construct that includes online safety, responsible information consumption, ethical online communication, digital equity, and digital participation. Existing literature, while highlighting the importance of these components, also underscores a critical shift away from rule-based approaches and towards emphasizing the broader ethical implications and social responsibilities of digital citizens (Shaban, 2024; Walter, 2024). This conceptual shift calls for further investigation into how these components of DR can be effectively addressed, particularly within educational settings. Moreover, a gap remains in the literature regarding the role of leadership skills, specifically outside of formal positions of power, and the impact of personal freedom on the development of DR within these settings. To this end, this study addresses a gap in the literature by examining how leadership skills and personal freedom influence the development of these specific components of DR.

Leadership in Digital Environments: While research examines

school leadership in digital contexts (Davis et al., 2013; Purcell, 2017), there is a need to explore leadership beyond traditional roles to encompass student-led initiatives and peer influence. For example, Purcell (2017) highlights the potential of “eService-Learning” in developing community-engaged and digitally-competent leadership. This study investigates the specific leadership skills (e.g., critical thinking, problem-solving, communication, ethical decision-making) that contribute to student digital responsibility, along with how these skills interact with perceptions of personal freedom. This is particularly important given the oversight concerns surrounding online education and the need to explore student agency (Congressional Research Service, 2017; Davidsson & Honig, 2023), highlighting that effective leadership extends beyond traditional administration to encompass empowering students to navigate the digital world ethically (Blasé & Blase, 2004). Furthermore, this study addresses the under-examined role of student agency, recognizing that ethical online behavior requires both institutional support and the active ethical decision-making of students. Cur-rent scholarship on leadership in digital environments increasingly advocates for a reconceptualization of leadership, moving beyond hierarchical models to recognize leadership as a developmental skill that students can cultivate and utilize (Castillo-Montoya et al., 2023). Furthermore, research highlights the necessity of expanding the conceptualization of leadership beyond traditional authority figures to acknowledge the significant role of peer influence in promoting responsible digital conduct (Shaban, 2024; Todaro, 2024; Walther, 2024). This evolving perspective necessitates future studies that deconstruct the multifaceted components of student-led digital leadership and assess the potential for students to exert a positive influence within digital landscapes.

Recognizing the growing concerns regarding online education oversight (Congressional Research Service, 2017; Davidsson & Honig, 2023), this study acknowledges the importance of institutional leadership in guiding students’ effective use of technology (Nonaka, 2023; Dexter, 2011; Tam et al., 2017). However, consistent with the emerging view of leadership described above, this study specifically focuses on the development of specific leadership skills that contribute to individual digital responsibility. Therefore, it moves beyond a traditional perspective of leadership focused on institutional administration to emphasize the importance of empowering individuals to navigate digital environments ethically (Blasé & Blase, 2004). Furthermore, and aligning with the above assertion about peer influence, this study directly addresses the under-examined role of student agency and peer dynamics in fostering ethical online behavior. It recognizes that responsible digital

3. Research methodology

## Method

This quantitative study employed a cross-sectional survey design to examine the relationships between leadership skills, personal freedom, and digital responsibility among university students. Data were collected from 1226 students at Beni Suef University in Egypt. A stratified random sampling technique was used to ensure representation across different academic disciplines and student demographics. Strati-fication variables included year of study (freshman, sophomore, junior, senior) and major (categorized into broad disciplinary areas: engineering, humanities, social sciences, business, and others). Proportional allocation was used to ensure that the sample reflected the overall proportions of these variables in the university population. Prior to data collection, a power analysis (using G\*Power, version 3.1.9) was conducted to determine the required sample size. This analysis indicated that a sample size of 1226 participants would provide 80% power to

detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s d =

0.5) at an alpha level of 0.05. The sample comprised 50.2% female and 49.8% male students. Data collection was conducted online using Qualtrics. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Board, Beni Suef University (IRB No: 00024–18). Informed consent was obtained from all participants, who were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without penalty. All data were anonymized to protect participant confidentiality.

## Measures

Three validated questionnaires were administered.

1. Digital Responsibility Questionnaire (DRQ): A 25-item, 5-point Likert

scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) assessing six dimensions of digital responsibility: Participation, Functionality, Data Privacy, Transparency, Norms & Values, and Accountability. The split-half reliability (using Spearman-Brown correction) was 0.86, indicating high reliability.

2. Unlocking Your Leadership Potential (ULPSA): A 15-item, 5-point

Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) assessing six dimensions of perceived leadership skills: Strategic Vision, Communication & Relationships, Team Leadership, Decision-Making Skills, and Motivation & Development. The split-half reliability (using Spearman-Brown correction) was 0.82, indicating high reliability.

3. Personal Freedom Survey (PFS): A 26-item, 5-point Likert scale (1 =

strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) assessing five dimensions of personal freedom: Public Freedom, Personal Autonomy, Goal Setting & Initiative, Adaptability & Openness to Feedback, and Personal Growth & Autonomy. The split-half reliability (using Spearman- Brown correction) was 0.88, indicating high reliability.

Content Validity: Content validity was established for all three in-

struments through expert review by 7 professors with expertise in the relevant fields (digital ethics, leadership studies, and social psychology) (see Table 1).

## Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS and AMOS (Version 22). Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies) were calculated to characterize the sample (Table 2). Pearson’s correlations were computed to examine the relationships between variables (Table 3), with Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) used to assess multicollinearity.

Multiple linear regression analyses were used to examine the predictive power of leadership skills and personal freedom on digital responsibility (Table 4). A structural equation model (SEM) was employed to test the hypothesized mediating role of leadership skills (Fig. 1).

Table 2 presents the demographic characteristics of the 1226 university students who participated in the study. The sample was evenly split between male and female participants (50% each). The mean age was 21 years with a standard deviation of 2 years. The modal year of study was sophomore year, and the most frequently represented major was engineering.

The study’s participant pool consisted of 1226 undergraduate students, characterized by an equitable gender distribution (50% female, 50% male) and a mean age of 21 years (SD = 2). A stratified random sampling technique was implemented to ensure proportional representation across all academic years. This methodology resulted in a cohort composition of 24% freshmen, 25% sophomores, 25% juniors, and 27% seniors. With respect to academic disciplines, engineering constituted the most prevalent major, encompassing 28% of the sample, as detailed in Table 2.

Specifically, as shown in Table 2, the sample was comprised of 613 female (50%) and 613 male (50%) students, reflecting a balanced representation of genders. The distribution of students across academic standing was as follows: 290 freshmen (24%), 310 sophomores (25%), 300 juniors (25%), and 326 seniors (27%). Furthermore, the most commonly represented academic major was engineering, accounting for 350 participants (28%), with the remaining 876 participants (72%) representing a diverse range of other disciplines.

## Ethical considerations

This research was conducted with ethical approval from the Research Ethics Board at Beni Suef University. All participants provided informed consent before data collection commenced. Anonymity of the data was maintained by removing all personally identifying information, and strict confidentiality protocols were followed throughout the study.

## Leadership skills as a mediator

This section examines the potential mediating role of leadership skills in the relationship between personal freedom and digital responsibility. While prior analyses have established significant positive associations between leadership skills and both personal freedom and digital responsibility, this investigation seeks to determine whether leadership skills serve as a mechanism through which personal freedom influences responsible digital behavior. A structural equation modeling (SEM) approach was employed to explore this mediating effect. The hypothesized model is depicted in Fig. 4, and the overall model fit, as indicated by the fit indices, can be found in Table 9.

### Assessing model fit

The fit indices in Table 9 indicate a poor fit of the hypothesized

model to the observed data. Several fit indices fall outside of acceptable

ranges. Specifically, the chi-square test is statistically significant (p <

.001), and the RMSEA value (0.127) exceeds the recommended threshold of 0.08. While the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker- Lewis Index (TLI) values are above the commonly used cutoff of 0.90, they are relatively close to the threshold, indicating only marginal fit. The Normed Fit Index (NFI) falls even further below the acceptable threshold of 0.90. The poor model fit suggests that the hypothesized relationships between personal freedom, leadership skills, and digital responsibility may require revision.

### Mediation analysis: leadership skills and the personal freedom-digital responsibility relationship

Our research sought to address this gap through the following questions: (1) To what extent do specific leadership skills predict digital responsibility, and how does this relationship vary across dimensions of digital responsibility? (2) What is the relationship between leadership skills and perceived personal freedom, and how does this influence digital responsibility? Previous research has explored leadership and personal freedom in virtual learning, but the intricate interplay of these factors in shaping digital responsibility, particularly concerning the rise of misinformation, cyberbullying, and ethical dilemmas related to AI in educational settings, remains largely under-explored (Hai et al., 2021; Lum, 2021; Malik, Bansal, & Tyagi, 2021; Murgia, 2024). Our findings significantly advance this field by providing empirical evidence on the relationships among these constructs and their implications for fostering responsible digital citizenship.

## Addressing research question 1: leadership skills and digital responsibility

Our findings reveal strong positive relationships between leadership skills and digital responsibility (Tables 3 and 4), providing an affirmative answer to our first research question. Specifically, dimensions such as participation, data privacy, and adherence to ethical norms and values were particularly strongly associated with leadership skills, aligning with existing literature highlighting leadership’s crucial role in shaping positive online behaviors (Ko & Rossen, 2017; Ma et al., 2019; Normore & Brooks, 2014; Purcell, 2017; Tillman & Scheurich, 2013). Furthermore, our regression analysis indicated that strategic vision, communication, motivation, and team leadership were significant predictors of overall digital responsibility, with the impact varying across the different aspects of digital responsibility. This answers the question of how this relationship varies across dimensions, with leadership skills having a comprehensive positive influence. This extends previous findings by focusing specifically on student-level leadership and its predictive effects on students’ responsible digital practices.

## Addressing research question 2: leadership skills and personal freedom

The study also explored the relationship between leadership skills and perceived personal freedom, as per our second research question. Our analysis showed statistically significant positive correlations between all individual leadership skill components and the composite measure of personal freedom (Table 5). This suggests that individuals with higher levels of leadership skills tend to perceive greater personal freedom, which could, in turn, influence their digital responsibility. However, while leadership skills were shown to predict personal freedom, the predictive capacity was not as strong as seen for digital responsibility. Furthermore, when investigating the predictive power of leadership on digital responsibility with our regression analyses, team leadership and decision-making did not contribute significantly to the model suggesting a more complex relationship.

## Addressing research question 3: personal freedom and digital responsibility

Regarding the relationship between personal freedom and digital responsibility, the findings showed a significant positive correlation, even when considering the influence of leadership skills, as per our third research question. However, we found that public freedom showed a stronger predictive capacity than other dimensions of personal freedom. This suggests that not all aspects of personal freedom contribute equally to fostering digital responsibility and indicates that the aspect of responsible autonomy is what is truly needed.

## Addressing research question 4 and 5: predictive effects and variations

Our analysis further addressed the fourth and fifth research questions by assessing whether the predictive effect of personal freedom on digital responsibility varied based on the level and types of leadership skills. While our regression analysis with mediation modeling was not conclusive due to poor model fit (Table 9, Fig. 4), we found significant correlations between leadership skills and digital responsibility and between public freedom and digital responsibility, implying that leadership and responsible autonomy are independently associated with digital responsibility. The model fit issues preclude us from commenting on the interaction of these variables. However, the strong predictive effect of public freedom on digital responsibility suggests a direct and powerful link that is independent from leadership skills and further research is needed to understand the interplay between these constructs and how they promote ethical online practices.

These findings strongly suggest a need for explicit instruction in digital ethics and responsible online behavior at all levels of education, from primary school through higher education. For instance, primary school curriculum could integrate lessons on online safety and respectful communication, while secondary schools could introduce more advanced topics such as responsible information consumption and critical evaluation of online sources. At the university level, courses could focus on digital governance, data privacy, and ethical leadership in the digital age.

Furthermore, our results highlight the significant role of public freedom in shaping digital responsibility (Tables 5 and 8). While some research has emphasized the negative consequences of unfettered online autonomy (Johnston, 2015; Sternberg, 2012), we demonstrate that empowering students with responsible freedom in the public sphere fosters more ethical digital engagement (Adi et al., 2015; Dörr, 2021). This underscores the importance of integrating civic engagement and digital literacy into curriculum design at all educational levels, encouraging students to participate actively in online communities while adhering to ethical standards. The strongest associations between public freedom and digital responsibility were seen in the bivariate correlations (Table 7).

The strong positive relationship between public freedom and digital responsibility suggests that cultivating a sense of civic participation, particularly concerning responsible technology use and ethical digital practices, is essential for promoting responsible digital citizenship. This could involve incorporating opportunities for student-led initiatives to address digital issues within their communities or designing projects that encourage active participation in online discussions and decision- making processes related to digital ethics.

While the hypothesized mediating role of leadership skills between personal freedom and digital responsibility was not supported (Table 9, Fig. 4) due to poor model fit, this does not diminish the substantial in-dependent effects of both leadership skills and public freedom on digital responsibility. This finding highlights the intricate nature of the relationship and points to the need for further research exploring potential alternative mediators, such as self-efficacy, critical thinking skills, or digital literacy.

Limitations: This cross-sectional study limits our ability to infer

causality, necessitating longitudinal research (Nonaka, 2023; Taylor & Hogenbirk, 2001). Reliance on self-reported data increases the susceptibility to biases, such as social desirability bias. The generalizability of our findings may be limited to the specific university context.

Strengths: This study’s strengths include its large sample size (N =

1226), the use of validated instruments, and its novel exploration of the complex interplay between leadership skills, personal freedom, and digital responsibility within a student-centered framework. These elements contribute to a robust framework for understanding responsible digital citizenship and provide a solid foundation for future research.

Future Research: Future research should investigate these re-

lationships using longitudinal designs to explore causal pathways, employ diverse populations to enhance generalizability, and analyze potential mediating variables, such as self-efficacy, critical thinking, and digital literacy, to provide a more comprehensive model of responsible digital citizenship (Jobin, Ienca, & Vayena, 2019; Lobschat et al., 2019; Mihale-Wilson et al., 2021; Mueller, 2022; Valor, 2021; Verk; Golob; Podnar, 2019). Further research is also needed to examine the specific mechanisms through which leadership skills and public freedom foster responsible digital behavior and to explore how these findings can be practically translated into curriculum design and instruction at various educational levels.

6. Conclusion

This study has illuminated the crucial, yet complex, interplay between leadership skills, personal freedom, and digital responsibility, offering significant insights for educators, policymakers, and researchers committed to fostering responsible digital citizenship. Our findings demonstrate robust positive relationships between these constructs, underscoring the importance of holistic leadership development, which encompasses strategic thinking, communication, motivation, and ethical decision-making. Such development must be coupled with the promotion of responsible public freedom, empowering students to actively participate in online environments while adhering to ethical standards (ISTE, 2014; Adi et al., 2015; Congressional Research Service, 2017; Davidsson & Honig, 2023; Dexter, 2011; Dörr, 2021; Mihale--Wilson et al., 2021; Nonaka, 2023; Tam et al., 2017; Valor, 2021; Verk; Golob; Podnar, 2019).

While this study confirms the influence of leadership skills on both digital responsibility and personal freedom, it also reveals the pivotal and nuanced role of public freedom as a distinct driver of responsible online conduct. These findings challenge simplistic notions of online autonomy, emphasizing that empowering students through participation in the public sphere, rather than mere individual freedom, fosters more ethical digital engagement. These insights are invaluable for educators and policymakers striving to create a more ethical and sustainable digital environment, and they carry direct implications for curriculum design and pedagogical practices, particularly within both native and foreign language education contexts.

The integration of leadership development into language curricula offers a potent avenue for cultivating responsible digital citizens. This approach can be realized through collaborative projects that emphasize responsible digital tool usage, promoting critical thinking, effective communication, and ethical problem-solving skills within online settings. For example, foreign language classrooms could leverage multilingual collaborative platforms to enhance communication and ethical online interactions, while native language courses could focus on media literacy, responsible information consumption, and the ethical application of digital tools. In both settings, the deliberate creation of opportunities for students to develop their digital literacy and ethical awareness is essential.

Moreover, this study’s emphasis on public freedom as a key predictor of digital responsibility underscores the imperative to create inclusive and participatory learning environments. Curriculum should be

designed to actively engage students in digital communities, empowering them to express their views responsibly and engage with diverse perspectives in online discussions and decision-making processes. This can involve designing activities such as online debates that encourage critical engagement with diverse viewpoints, collaborative writing assignments that model responsible online communication, and peer- feedback sessions that operate within a clear ethical framework. In language classes, this might include intercultural exchanges via online forums, collaborative analyses of digital texts, and projects aimed at developing media literacy, fostering both digital competence and ethical awareness.

Ultimately, the consistent integration of ethical considerations into all aspects of curriculum design, instruction, and assessment is paramount. Such an approach will equip students with the requisite skills to navigate the ethical complexities of the digital world and to make informed decisions regarding technology use. By embedding these ethical considerations across the curriculum, we can contribute to the development of well-rounded, ethically aware digital citizens who are fully prepared to thrive and lead responsibly in an increasingly complex digital landscape.
