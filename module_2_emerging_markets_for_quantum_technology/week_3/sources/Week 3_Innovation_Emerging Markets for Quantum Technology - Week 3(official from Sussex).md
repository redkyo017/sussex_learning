3.1 Innovation as a concept

Models of innovation: 1900–1950

As we saw in last week’s material, both science and technology have changed over the last 200 years. Science has become more important to many types of innovation, and technology has become more sophisticated and more complex to develop and produce.

Over the 20th century, several models of innovation were developed to try and capture

these changing, complex processes. In the 1930s, Schumpeter \[1\] argued that

suggested that this role had been taken over by the new R&D laboratories of large firms that emerged in the 1930s.

In the 1940s, there were ‘science push’ linear models. These models initially suggested that innovation takes place within firms, which draw on basic research in universities, but the real bottleneck holding back innovation was a lack of basic research. The idea was

that ‘basic research provides original discoveries from which all progress flows’ \[1\].

This model often shifts from ‘science provides some inputs’ to ‘the only input’, to ‘science produces innovation’. As a model of innovation it is flawed because, as we saw last week, technology often comes first (the Wright brothers were flying before research into aerodynamics began, and steam engines were running before anyone understood thermodynamics). Moreover, sometimes innovation has no input from science at all (in other words, design-intensive innovation).

Schmookler’s model and market demand

In the 1960s, a new model emerged: the ‘market pull’ linear model. This model was

developed by Schmookler in 1966 \[2\]. He used 19th century data on patents, investment

and output for sectors such as railways, petrol and construction to argue that changes in demand caused a reallocation of resources to innovation and was effective in inducing innovation. This model was supported by the strong evidence that understanding and addressing user needs is essential to successful innovation.

However, in Schmookler’s work, the conclusion shifted from ‘innovation is influenced by

market signals’ to ‘innovation is determined by market signals’ \[2\]. But this was not

justified by the methodology Schmookler used. Because he was looking at existing technologies, he couldn’t say much about innovation where the constraints were technical. There are, after all, many areas of technology where there is a very considerable demand, but innovation is not forthcoming. For example, we would like to have clean, cheap energy, or cures for many diseases. Innovations are not forthcoming because we do not yet know how to produce them.

Schmookler’s evidence was also questionable. As we saw last week, the role of science differs by sector and has changed over time. Schmookler looked at the 19th century, but that was a time when science and technology were largely distinct. That is not the case today. Schmookler looked at petrol, railways and construction, but these are sectors where science has a limited impact even today. He would have got a very different result had he looked at pharmaceuticals, electronics or quantum technologies.

The coupling models of innovation

based on evidence that firms with good technology but weak marketing tended not to be successful in innovation, and firms with good marketing but weak technology also tended not to succeed. Successful firms needed both. Hence, innovation was considered as a coupling process that matched a technology to a market demand. It was something that was uncertain, involved considerable experimentation and learning, and required coordination across the firm.

#### As Kline and Rosenberg (1986) noted:

‘Innovation is complex, uncertain, somewhat disorderly, and subject to changes of many sorts. Innovation is also difficult to measure and demands close co-ordination of adequate technical knowledge and excellent market judgment in order to satisfy economic, technological, and other types of constraints – all simultaneously. The process of innovation must be viewed as a series of changes in a complex system, not only of hardware, but also of the market environment, production facilities and knowledge and

the social contexts of the innovation organisation.’ \[3\]

Rothwell’s coupling model

This model conceptualises the innovation process as a logically sequential process, that can be divided into a series of functionally distinct but interacting and interdependent stages. It can be understood as a network of communication paths both within and outside the firm. In other words the process of innovation represents the confluence of technological capabilities and market needs within the framework of the innovating firm.

Rothwell’s model \[4\] suggests a complex flow between technology and market elements.

It illustrates the feedback loops and links between science, technology and the marketplace. For example, ‘Idea generation’ could branch off in R&D, or lead to new technologies, or just generate new leads. These could develop further to production, marketing and sales to address society’s needs, or loop back for more idea generation and technology development, or both.

Models of 1980s and 1990s

In the 1980s and 1990s, new models of innovation emerged that looked beyond the boundaries of a single firm.

Thinking at the time was heavily influenced by new understanding of what was driving Japanese success. Japanese firms like Toyota were innovating with their supply chains. In the USA and Europe, firms tended to have arm’s length commercial relationships with their supply chains. The new ‘Toyota’ model of innovation – and associated ideas such

as ‘lean production’ (a concept invented by Science Policy Research Unit (https://www.sussex.ac.uk/business-school/people-and-departments/spru)  (SPRU)) –

saw innovation as:

distributed across organisations running parallel across organisational functions having a focus on quality to avoid downstream costs of repairing problems

having a greater emphasis on learning and tacit knowledge being an organisation-wide function, not something just within R&D.

In the rest of the module, we will draw heavily on these ideas.

3.2 Drivers of innovation

The SAPPHO study: Success factors

The 1970s and 1980s saw a number of important studies of the drivers of success and failure in innovation. A very important study was SAPPHO, conducted in SPRU by Prof.

Roy Rothwell et al \[5\]. This became the basis for new ways of managing innovation and

the ideas from the study were implemented around the world.

Project SAPPHO \[5\] moved from studying personalities of successful entrepreneurs to

exploring the organisational factors within firms that differentiate success and failure in

innovation \[5\].

The most important factor determining success was attention to user needs. This may seem obvious today, but at the time a lot of people understood innovation in terms of R&D laboratories and technical factors. The study also found that a range of other factors had a big influence on success. These included the following factors in firms with successful innovation:

The individual managers responsible for innovation tended to be more senior and had more authority (the ‘heavy weight’ project manager). There was greater attention to marketing and publicity. The development process was more efficient. Firms used outside technology and advice.

Interestingly, the study found that scale, planning, scientists, lead-time and basic research didn’t have much impact on outcomes.

Project execution factors

When examining the factors that determine successful project execution, the following need to be considered during innovation:

good internal and external communication: linkages to external scientific and

for makeability’ careful planning and product control procedures: screening and appraisal efficiency and quality production with effective quality control strong market orientation with an emphasis on satisfying user needs, efficient customer linkages and involvement of users in development good technical service for customers key individuals – project champions and gatekeepers

high quality management: commitment to development of human capital \[6\].

#### Cooper \[7\] undertook a similar study and found additional success factors:

uniqueness and superiority of product and the benefit it provides to customers nature of market: market size, growth and intensity technical and production synergies between new and existing product lines.

This last factor highlights the importance of building up firm-specific capabilities to deal with technical and marketing uncertainty \[7\].

Innovation and uncertainty

One way to understand these success factors is to consider them as managerial and organisational means of dealing with the uncertainty of innovation. This uncertainty relates to technology, customers’ changing needs, and organisational complexity within and externally to the firm.

As we saw last week, technologies involve integrating numerous components and

subsystems whose behaviour cannot be fully predicted from theory. Innovation depends on costly experimentation to explore operational principles and test prototypes. Firms continuously integrate an increasing range of knowledge. Innovation

is an uncertain organisational process linking tech capability to market need.

Knowledge that emerges from experimentation is more than information. It involves know-how to solve complex technology-specific design, development, testing, production and use problems, to create (and capture) value for customers. This context-specific, partly tacit knowledge is central to interpreting performance and understanding where to look for improved performance.

Technological search and learning are therefore incremental, localised, costly, firm specific, time-consuming and difficult to trade. Learning can improve performance and give firms cumulative technology-specific capabilities to address uncertainty and generate superior performance. Many of the key success factors identified by SAPPHO

and Cooper \[7\] are managerial ways of ensuring successful learning within the firm, to

3.3 Complementary assets

Innovation is about successful commercialisation. To succeed, firms need to make a profit in the commercial sector. To do this, they need to both create but also capture value. Capturing value is vital to innovation and is achieved through ‘complementary assets’.

Complementary assets are one of, if not, the most important ideas for understanding innovation. David Teece \[10\] highlighted that often innovators do not

benefit from their work. EMI, for example, created the CAT scanner, drawing on its skills in radio and electronics. However, the firm did not profit from the innovation. New firms entered the market and quickly dominated. Despite being the original innovator, EMI quickly left the market and failed to capture value.

One of the reasons EMI failed was that it didn’t patent and protect its technology. Other firms had large international marketing and distribution networks. EMI did not, so it struggled to reach its customers. Other firms offered technical support and maintenance. EMI did not, so it struggled to support its customers. New entrants, such as Toshiba, with global distribution channels and superior after-sales support, quickly dominated the market.

Today, a firm that developed a quantum computer, but didn’t patent its technology, and failed to build distribution channels and after-sales support, would likely suffer the same fate. Producing technology is not enough; firms also need to capture value.

External and internal complementary assets

The assets a firm needs in order to capture value and profit from innovation are distinct from the assets the firm needs in order to produce a new product or service. The assets a firm needs in order to profit from innovation and capture value are called complementary assets, because they complement the assets that the firm has to generate value.

#### Complementary assets can be internal and external. For example:

Tacit and codified knowledge – the development and production of technology may depend on tacit knowledge that is very localised and only passed on through face-to-face interactions. This tacit knowledge can be localised in a local area or ‘cluster’ and

protection for innovations – they allow firms to have exclusive rights over technologies and can prevent firms from copying innovations. They are particularly useful in sectors such as pharmaceuticals. In many other sectors, patents can be innovated around, but they can still hinder competitors. Trade secrets – sometimes production technologies and ‘recipes’ can be kept secret. Coca Cola, for example, has a secret recipe that has not been copied by its competitors. Technical complexity – the technical complexity of a product can make it very difficult for competitors to copy. Lead time advantages – if a firm can produce new generations of technology faster than its competitors can innovate, it can maintain an advantage over time and capture more value. Specialised production facilities – these can allow firms to produce technology in ways that are unavailable to competitors. In high-tech areas such as biotechnology and some areas of electronics and quantum, production facilities are very specialised and firms that are unable to access them can struggle to enter the market. Global distribution, sales and marketing networks – these can be costly and timeconsuming for competitors to build. They act as barriers to entry and enable firms to capture more value. Reputation and branding – these can help to associate products with customers’ perceptions of quality. Brand names can also be covered by intellectual property. Some firms have exploited this combination of branding and intellectual property to capture value very successfully, such as Aspirin by the brand Bayer.

If you lack these assets, then other firms will copy your innovation and capture your profits.

Types of complementary assets

Different complementary assets may be more important for particular kinds of firms and at particular times within a product’s life cycle:

Science-intensive innovators are likely to draw heavily on patents, hard-to-copy production knowledge, and secrecy. Design-intensive innovators are likely to draw on copyright and rich customer relationships that enable them to understand what customers need and build trust. Scale-intensive innovators that produce standardised products at scale and focus their innovation on improving processes (in sectors like cement, steel and industrial gases) are likely to draw heavily on hard-to-copy production engineering experience, specialist supply chains and distribution channels, and so on. Project-based innovators are likely to draw on internal capabilities to manage projects, reputation, and access to skilled specialist staff.

Firms in quantum-related sectors are likely to draw on a range of complementary assets. Firms need to think carefully about how they will build an ability to capture value and how it will change over time.

Patents are likely to be particularly important for quantum innovations. Patents provide legal protections granted to inventors and provide them with exclusive rights to make, use and sell their invention for a limited period of time.

#### Some key features of patents include:

Novelty: Patents must be new and original. The invention must not previously have been disclosed to the public. It should not have been published or publicly demonstrated before the patent application was filed. Non-obviousness: The invention must not be obvious to someone skilled in the relevant field. The patent application must demonstrate that the invention is not an obvious variation or combination of existing technologies and includes an original inventive step. Utility: The invention must be useful and have a practical application. Patents cannot be granted for abstract ideas, theories or concepts. Disclosure: The patent application must include a detailed description of the invention, so that others can understand and replicate it after the patent expires. Patents are a social contract, where society provides a monopoly for a limited period, in return for the patent holder making the technology available through the patent

once the patent expires. This makes patents a very useful source of information about new technology.

Claims: The patent must include one or more claims that define the scope of the invention and the exclusive rights that the inventor is seeking. Duration: Patents typically last for 20 years from the date of filing. After that time, the invention becomes part of the public domain and anyone can use, make or sell it. Jurisdiction: Patents are granted by national patent offices, and their protection is limited to the jurisdiction in which they are granted.

Business models

In this week’s content, we stress the importance of complementing an ability to create value with an ability to capture value using complementary assets. The combination of value creation and value capture collectively defines a firm’s business model. The business model defines what the firm does, how it does it, who its customers are, how it delivers value for them, and how the firm generates a commercial return from its

A key part of any corporate strategy is finding ways to develop complementary assets and use them to capture value in new ways. McDonalds, for example, is not just a fastfood restaurant chain. It makes money from franchising. But it also makes considerable money as a property developer. Similarly, one year Porsche made more money as a hedge fund than it did from selling cars.

Many of the large firms working in the quantum space are extremely skilled at deploying complementary assets. IBM’s shift towards being a computer services firm enabled it to capture more value. Google is particularly impressive, operating a two-sided business model, where it offers a search service for free to one set of customers and captures value from its marketing activities with an entirely different set of customers.

Often, when you see a major success in the economy, it is related to complementary assets.

3.4 Diffusion and adoption in innovation

Coming up with an innovation isn’t enough. Customers need to adopt it, often in large numbers. The diffusion and adoption of innovations is a complex process. However, it can be understood and, in some instances, managed to improve performance.

The speed of technology transfer

Technology transfer is typically slow – the fast diffusion of technologies such as ChatGPT is the exception. There is a lot of variation in the rate of diffusion. It is important not to think of technology transfer as the diffusion of fixed things. Instead, it is more useful to see it as an ongoing, uncertain, dynamic, expensive and difficult process of innovation that is moved to a new place.

Performance should continue to improve, prices should decline, and complementary skills and innovations be developed to expand the potential number of users. As

Rosenberg \[11\] notes, ‘the speed with which performance characteristics are improved,

techniques modified to meet the needs of specialised users, and the price of the invention gradually reduced … determine its acceptability among an increasingly widening circle of potential users’ (p. 200).

Remember: technologies aren’t fixed things. They are continuously improving and are subject to secondary inventions – for example, new innovations in quantum software

innovation is often dismissed as less important than the initial invention, but this valuejudgement cannot be justified in economic terms. Initially, technology is primitive, so be careful not to read history backwards, and project modern success onto the past: ‘In the late 18th century the Comanche were able to ride 300 yards and discharge twenty

arrows on horseback in the minute it took a Texan ranger to reload.’ \[12\]

Because technologies are improving, future uses are hard to predict. Watson, the president of IBM, once predicted that the global market for computers would only be five

computers \[13\].

You know now that innovations typically do not diffuse instantaneously. They often take a long time, suggesting that getting innovations adopted is difficult. Also, we’ve said that some innovations diffuse quite fast, whereas some others do not diffuse at all. The speed of diffusion can be represented on a line graph. The diffusion path is the aggregate outcome of individual choices to adopt, and usually follows an S-shaped curve.

The systemic nature of technology transfer

Innovation is systemic and relies on complementary innovations. Users and makers each need to learn new skills. Older technologies continue to improve, and old and new technologies often hybridise.

Expectations about old and new tech affect the timing of adoption. Diffusion is subject to threshold effects. Different places have different innovation systems, and diffused technology may not be appropriate.

The epidemic models of diffusion

The adoption and diffusion of an innovation can be understood in terms of the spread of information about the innovation, which resembles the spread of an infectious disease. This simple model assumes that:

the innovations are better than any other existing option all adopters are homogeneous a potential adopter adopts when he/she knows about the innovation the only way information spreads is by personal contact (word-of-mouth).

The assumption that adopters are all the same is questionable. Other models assume

that there are differences among adopters, such as rank or threshold models, where

potential adopters decide to adopt when the benefits of adoption exceed the costs. In

these models, the benefits of adoption depend on a variable x (usually the size for firms

and income for individuals). There will be a certain level of x above which the benefits

exceed the costs (the threshold level). For example, the diffusion of the reaper machine in the USA \[15\] depended on farmers having a large enough farm for the reaper machine

to be worth spending money on. At a certain size of a farm, a threshold is reached, and farmers will invest. Historically, we saw that rising labour wages triggered diffusion, as they increased farmers’ costs, making it profitable for more and more farmers to adopt new machinery.

Types and characteristics of adopters

Everett Rogers’ influential book Diffusion of Innovations \[16\] suggested that adopters of

new technology differ, and some potential adopters are more inclined to adopt earlier than others. There is a five-group classification based on statistical cut-off points:

early adopters early majority late majority laggards.

#### Each group shares a common set of characteristics and values:

Innovators are venturesome but not well respected. Early adopters are opinion leaders. They are copied by the masses. Early majority massively adopts the innovation.

Departures focused on the learning process

Innovation researchers have found that there are significant costs to diffusing preexisting technology, and that it often doesn’t work or doesn’t work as well in new settings. It is misleading to think that a simple consecutive process of invention would lead to innovation diffusion. Diffusion typically requires considerable innovation to change technology to new settings. For example, coal in the USA and Europe was different, which constrained the historical diffusion of steel technology. Incremental innovations in this adaptation process are needed to generate full benefits. These incremental innovations can be considerable (in other words, having to build an R&D lab to work out how to make European steel technology work in the USA).

If you assume that technology is fixed, you miss the organisational learning that’s needed. Accessing technology isn’t necessarily the hardest part. Is innovation in quantum markets random or can it be managed? How can we structure innovation processes appropriately and develop effective behavioural patterns (routines) to help firms innovate?

Firms in quantum markets face significant challenges of undertaking innovation when they are aiming at technical and commercial targets that keep moving. New problems

uncertainty, and so on are likely to emerge continuously.

Can we manage innovation?

There is now extensive evidence that firms in high-tech markets can learn to handle the innovation process in a systematic fashion and develop organisational ‘routines’ for dealing with it. For example, how they search for new ideas and opportunities, how they choose between different options, how they manage projects, and so on. The organisational routines that firms use, as in ‘the way we do things around here’, are the firm's regular, predictable operational processes. They are collective, social and partly

tacit and are learnt over time (Nelson and Winter \[19\]).

The organisational routines tend to be firm-specific, as each organisation has its own particular ways of doing things. These differences can become a source of advantage, as they influence cognition and behaviour within the firm. These in turn have an influence on firm performance and enable co-ordination by providing a degree of behavioural stability. They therefore provide firms with both stability and the reproduction of behaviour, and the potential for creativity and change. Depending on how well they match the needs of the firm, they can either promote or inhibit innovation, impacting performance in good and bad ways. It is therefore important to understand the fit between generic processes and what drives performance and the contingencies of particular firms, technologies and markets.
