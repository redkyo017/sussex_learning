Emotion Regulation Mediates the Link Between Exposure to Childhood Adversity and Transdiagnostic Symptom Domains in Youth

Zhiyuan Liu a,b,# , MPH , Eleni Christofilea a,# , BSc , Gladys Alondra Venegas a , BS , Sasha Santini-Bishop a , Ai Nghi Diep a , Zuoheng Wang b , PhD , Ilanit Gordon c , PhD

Objective: Emotion dysregulation is recognized as a transdiagnostic feature common to child psychopathology. This study examined the unique association between emotion regulation difficulties and specific strategies (suppression, reappraisal) with transdiagnostic symptom domains of attention, externalizing, and internalizing problems in youth. This study also tested for the mediating role of emotion regulation between domains of childhood adversity (family conflict and negative life events) and transdiagnostic symptoms. Method: In a sample of 9,057 children aged 12 to 13 years in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development℠ (ABCD) Study (47.21% female), linear mixed-effects models were used to examine the association between emotion regulation difficulties and specific regulation strategies with transdiagnostic symptoms. Follow-up analyses tested replication of findings retrospectively and prospectively. Structural equation modeling was used to test whether emotion regulation mediated the association between childhood adversity (family conflict, negative life events) and symptom severity. The Child Behavior Checklist was used as a continuous measure of symptom severity. Results: Greater severity of emotion dysregulation was associated with severity of each transdiagnostic symptom domain, and these associations were replicated both retrospectively and prospectively. Sex-specific longitudinal patterns emerged for attention and externalizing problems. Emotion regulation difficulties mediated the association between childhood adversity (family conflict, negative life events) and all symptom domains, with strategy-specific patterns emerging only for internalizing problems. Conclusion: These findings have implications for developing targeted interventions to address broad emotion dysregulation as an underlying mechanism of child psychopathology linked to exposure to childhood adversity. Plain language summary: This study examined how difficulties in emotion regulation affect child mental health and interact with early life adversity, with a focus on attention, disruptive behaviors, and internalizing problems. Utilizing data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Devel-opment℠ (ABCD) Study (n = 9,057, aged 9-10 years), the authors found that emotion dysregulation was linked to increased symptom severity in children. Sex-specific patterns of disruptive behavior were identified in male participants and internalizing problems in female participants. Emotion regulation difficulties mediated the association between childhood adversity and all mental health symptom domains. Key words: emotion regulation; adverse childhood experiences; transdiagnostic symptoms; suppression; reappraisal

mpairment in emotion regulation, or the topdown control of emotion, is recognized as a transdiagnostic feature common to child psy-
chopathology, including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), mood and anxiety disorders, and disruptive behavior disorders. 1–6 This recognition has led the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry to recently emphasize the need to better understand the role of emotion regulation impairments (emotion dysregulation) in child psychopathology. 7 In addition, previous research has suggested that the use of specific emotion regulation strategies, such as reappraisal and suppression, is associated with symptom severity in youth. 8–11 Reappraisal 
and suppression are categorized as generally adaptive and maladaptive emotion regulation strategies, respectively, and are often the target of cognitive–behavioral interventions focused on building regulation capacity in youth. 12 How-ever, no studies have examined the potentially distinct association of emotion dysregulation with attention, externalizing (eg, irritability/anger, aggression, and noncompliance), and internalizing (eg, anxiety and depressive symptoms) problem dimensions in a relatively large and diverse developmental sample. Moreover, key gaps also include a lack of simultaneously accounting for commonly co-occurring symptom domains and/or a focus on DSM-5 diagnostic categories rather than transdiagnostic

dimensions aligned with the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework. 13 As such, a better understanding of how emotion regulation is linked to domains of transdiagnostic symptoms in youth could facilitate the development of more effective treatments and/or refine existing interventions targeting emotion regulation processes. 
Even though sex differences in emotion regulation have been suggested to underlie child psychopathology, there is a lack of consistency in findings. For instance, some studies suggest that emotion regulation difficulties show stronger associations with externalizing problems in boys 14 and internalizing problems in girls, 15 whereas other studies have failed to detect differences. 16 Regarding specific emotion regulation strategies, in a recent study of adults, distress was positively related to suppression and negatively related to reappraisal in females but not males. 17 However, few studies have examined differences in male and female youth related to specific emotion regulation strategies, and the few existing studies suggest potentially distinct patterns. 18 
Therefore, even though there is a general trend suggesting sex differences in emotion regulation and psychopathology, limitations include smaller samples that are often underpowered and/or unbalanced for identifying robust sex differences. Thus, the link between sex differences in emotion regulation and child psychopathology remains elusive. 
Impairment in emotion regulation is also a potential mediator between psychopathology risk and exposure to adverse childhood experiences (ACEs). 19 The detrimental impact of exposure to ACEs has been consistently shown to increase the risk of psychopathology in youth. 20–22 Prior studies have tended to focus on broad-band measures of ACEs (ie, using a sum score or latent class analysis) 21,23 or specific ACEs linked to elevated risk of psychopathology in children. 24–26 Two specific ACEs that are consistently associated with child mental health symptom onset and severity relate to maladaptive family functioning and environment as well as exposure to negative life events. 27 For instance, the family environment is critical in shaping the child’s emotional development, and exposure to psychological stress—particularly family conflict—poses a risk to later development of psychopathology. 28,29,30 Furthermore, exposure to adverse life events, such as being a victim of violence or serious illness, is thought to have a cumulative effect on the child’s well-being and risk of onset for psychopathology. 23,25,27 Prior work has also indicated that emotion dysregulation, including maladaptive emotion regulation strategies such as suppression, may mediate the association between ACEs and psychopathology. 24,26,31,32 However, it remains unclear how exposure to family conflict and negative life events differentially influence transdiagnostic symptom severity of attention, 
externalizing, and internalizing problems through both adaptive and maladaptive regulation. To the best of our knowledge, no study has concurrently examined the mediating role of emotion regulation between ACEs and symptom severity, which could inform treatment targets for cognitive– behavioral interventions that aim to mitigate psychopathology through the teaching of adaptive regulation strategies. 29 
The current study leverages a large and diverse developmental sample from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development℠ (ABCD) Study 33 to investigate distinct relationships between emotion regulation impairments and broad-band, commonly co-occurring transdiagnostic symptom domains of attention, externalizing, and internalizing problems in children. First, we examined the unique association between emotion regulation difficulties as well as the use of reappraisal, suppression, and transdiagnostic symptom severities. Second, we directly tested for interactions between sex and emotion regulation difficulties as well as specific regulation strategies in predicting each symptom domain. Third, as a follow-up to primary cross-sectional analyses, we tested the replication of findings at different timepoints, both retrospectively and prospectively. Fourth, we examined whether emotion regulation mediated the association between exposure to childhood adversity (family conflict and negative life events) and transdiagnostic symptoms. 
Based on prior work, 2–4,6,8,34 we hypothesized that greater symptom severity would be associated with greater emotion regulation difficulties. Regarding specific regulation strategies, we predicted that greater symptom severity within each domain would be related to reduced reappraisal, whereas more severe externalizing and internalizing problems would be related to increased suppression. 8–10,35 
In line with previous literature, 36,37 we also hypothesized an interaction between suppression and reappraisal strategies. Regarding sex interactions, we predicted that sexspecific associations between emotion dysregulation and externalizing problems would be greater in boys vs girls, 14 
but that the opposite pattern would be observed for internalizing problems (ie, greater emotion dysregulation in girls vs boys). 15 Although it is challenging to have strong hypotheses related to sex interactions between specific strategies and child psychopathology, given that prior work included smaller samples and/or mixed findings, we hypothesized that sex-specific differences would be observed. Regarding ACEs, we expected that greater exposure to family conflict and negative life events would be associated with higher symptom severity, 24,25,38,39 and that this association would be mediated by broad emotion dysregulation and the use of reappraisal and suppression. 19,32

Participants

Participants were enrolled in the ABCD Study (Data Release 5.1), which is a longitudinal, multi-site study of children aged 9 to 10 years during the first wave of enrollment, and which examines cognitive and neural development from early adolescence to early adulthood. 33

Collection of emotion regulation measures was initiated at the 3-year timepoint (ages 12-13 years), which was used in the current study to provide a cross-sectional assessment of associations between transdiagnostic symptom domains and emotion regulation. At this timepoint, there were approximately 11,879 subjects, of whom 2,822 were removed because of missing and incomplete records. This resulted in 9,057 children (4,277 girls) with behavioral data available for analysis (Figure 1). Participant characteristics are shown in Table 1. 42–45

Transdiagnostic Symptom Domains and Cognition. For

consistency with prior studies using the ABCD dataset, 38,46,47 we used a broad-band measure of symptom dimensions, the parent-rated Child Behavior Checklist subscales of Attention, Externalizing, and Internalizing Problems. 48 Given that cognition has been associated with psychopathology 49 and emotion regulation, 50 the NIH

Toolbox Cognition Battery 42 age-corrected total composite score was used in analyses to account for overall cognitive performance, similar to previous studies examining emotion regulation and psychopathology. 8,16,46,47 Addi-tional details for measures are provided in Supplement 1, Supplemental Methods, available online. 
Emotion Dysregulation. The parent-reported Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) 43 was used to evaluate broad emotion regulation impairment (emotion dysregulation). We selected the DERS as a broad-band measure of regulation, given that prior work indicates its utility and reliability in assessing domains of emotion regulation and self-regulation in clinical populations as well as in youth with elevated severity of symptom domains. 51,52 The DERS assesses broad emotion dysregulation across multiple domains: emotional awareness, acceptance of emotions, ability to engage in goal-directed behavior, refraining from impulsive behavior, and access to effective emotion regulation strategies. The DERS total score was used in analyses as a measure of emotion regulation difficulties, with higher scores reflecting greater challenges, as described in the Supplement 1, Supplemental Methods, available online. 
Specific Emotion Regulation Domains. Youth-reported reappraisal and suppression were measured using the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ), 44,45 which evaluates self-control of emotions across 2 domains. The ERQ provides subscale scores for reappraisal and suppression, whereby higher scores on each subscale indicate greater use of the corresponding strategy, as described in Supplement 1, Supplemental Methods, available online. 
Adverse Childhood Experiences. Adverse childhood ex-periences (ACEs) were assessed using youth-reported measures to capture family conflict and exposure to negative life events. These measures were chosen because they capture distinct but related domains of ACEs and have not been studied together in a single design with emotion regulation. Family conflict was measured using the Conflict Subscale from the Family Environment Scale. 53 Higher scores on this Conflict Subscale indicate greater levels of family conflict. Exposure to negative life events was assessed using the Life Events Scale, a self-report questionnaire. 53,54 The number of life events experienced and rated as negative by the child was used in sum scores and analyses, as described in Supplement 1, Supplemental Methods, available online.

Linear mixed-effects models were repeated but with an interaction term for sex with emotion regulation difficulties and strategies (ie, Sex-by-DERS, Sex-by-Reappraisal, and Sex-by-Suppression). To account for multiple comparisons, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the p.adjust function in R. FDR correction was performed separately within each analytic family of tests. For the main effects models, 33 fixed-effect p values (11 predictors across 3 outcome models) were corrected together. For the interaction models, 42 fixed-effect p values (14 predictors across the same 3 outcomes) were jointly corrected. Semi-partial R 2 was estimated for the linear mixed effects models using the partR2 package in R 55 to facilitate the interpretation of results and effect sizes. Semi-partial R 2 provides an interpretable measure of effect size by quantifying the unique proportion of variance explained by each fixed-effect predictor. 56 To test for potential longitudinal effects and replication of findings, supplemental analyses were conducted in which primary models were repeated but with the dependent variable (CBCL problem subscales) at baseline (retrospective models) or at the 4-year follow-up (prospective models). Follow-up analyses also tested for interactions between sex and emotion regulation. 
Mediation (Path Analysis) Models. Path analysis was con-ducted to examine whether emotion regulation difficulties and strategies (reappraisal, suppression) mediated the relationships between ACEs (Family Conflict, Total Number of Childhood Bad Events) and behavioral outcomes (CBCL Attention, Externalizing, and Internalizing Problems). Separate mediation models were developed for each outcome variable. The models estimated direct effects of ACEs on the behavioral outcomes, indirect effects through emotion regulation difficulties, reappraisal and suppression strategies, and total effects. Path coefficients were calculated using structural equation modeling in R with the lavaan package, incorporating the lavaan.survey extension to control for clustering effects, with families nested within sites. Statistical significance of the indirect effects was evaluated using the product-of-coefficients method. Covariates, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, and cognition, were included in all models. To control for multiple comparisons within each mediation model, we applied the Benjamini– Hochberg FDR correction using the p.adjust function in R. All parameter-level p values (including direct, indirect, and total effects) were included in the FDR adjustment, ensuring control of the expected false discovery rate at α = 0.05 within each model. Path diagrams illustrating the relationships among variables and coefficients were generated 
using the DiagrammeR package in R. Results were interpreted to evaluate the distinct roles of emotion regulation difficulties, reappraisal, and suppression in mediating the relationship between ACEs and symptom domain outcomes. To test for potential longitudinal effects and replication of findings, supplemental analyses were conducted in which primary models were repeated with ACEs measured at 1- and 2-year follow-ups and transdiagnostic symptoms measured at 4-year follow-up.

Longitudinal Effects and Emotion Regulation Next, we examined whether emotion regulation measured at the 3-year timepoint was retrospectively (ie, baseline timepoint; N = 9,059) (Table S3, available online) and prospectively (ie, 4-year timepoint; N = 4144) (Table S4, available online) associated with attention, externalizing, and internalizing problems. 
Retrospective Associations. Greater emotion regulation difficulties were significantly associated with greater severity of attention, externalizing, and internalizing problems at baseline (p FDR < .001) (Supplement 2, Supplemental Re-sults, Table S5, Figure S3, available online). No significant associations were observed between suppression or reappraisal and any of the transdiagnostic symptom domains. A significant interaction between sex and emotion regulation difficulties was observed for externalizing problems (p FDR = .039) (Table S6, Figure S4, available online). This association was stronger for male than for female participants (Supplement 2, Supplemental Results, Table S7, available online). There were no significant interactions between sex and suppression or reappraisal for symptom domains. 
Prospective Associations. Increased emotion regulation difficulties were significantly associated with increased severity of attention, externalizing, and internalizing prob-lems at the 4-year follow-up (p FDR < .001) (Supplement 2, Supplemental Results, Table S8, Figure S5, available online). Suppression was significantly associated only with internal-izing symptoms (p FDR < 0.05). A significant interaction was observed between sex and suppression for attention problems (p FDR = .034) (Supplement 2, Supplemental Results, Table S9, Figure S6, available online): that is, the effect of suppression was greater in female than in male participants (Supplement 2, Supplemental Results, and Table S10, available online). 
Mediation of Emotion Regulation on ACEs and Transdiagnostic Symptoms We then tested whether emotion regulation mediated the link between exposure to ACEs and symptom severity. Mediation models demonstrated significant effects of family conflict on attention, externalizing, and internalizing problems through regulation difficulties. In the first 
model, family conflict had a significant direct effect on attention problems (c1 = 0.081, p FDR < .001) (Figure 3, top panel) and a significant indirect effect mediated by regulation difficulties (a1b1 = 0.074, p FDR < .001); that is, children experiencing higher levels of family conflict showed increased attention problems that was explained in part by emotion dysregulation. However, reappraisal and suppression were not significant mediators. In the second model, family conflict had a significant direct effect on externalizing problems (c1 = 0.102, p FDR < .001) (Figure 3, middle panel) and a significant indirect effect mediated by regulation difficulties (a1b1 = 0.081, p FDR < .001); that is, exposure to higher levels of family conflict was linked to increased externalizing problems that was affected in part by emotion dysregulation. No significant mediation effects were observed for reappraisal or suppression. This study leveraged a large and diverse sample to examine how emotion regulation difficulties and the use of specific regulation strategies (ie, reappraisal and suppression) are linked to transdiagnostic symptom domains in youth, as well as sex-specific associations. Three main findings were observed, which extend prior work. First, emotion regulation difficulties were associated with greater symptom severity across all transdiagnostic domains. In addition, suppression was positively associated with severity for each symptom domain, but not reappraisal. Second, sex interactions with emotion regulation were observed for the retrospective and prospective models, but not for the crosssectional models. Third, emotion regulation difficulties mediated the relationship between exposure to family conflict, negative life events, and symptom severity across each transdiagnostic domain.

Association of Emotion Dysregulation and Strategy Use With Transdiagnostic Symptom Domains Findings revealed that greater emotion regulation difficulties were related to greater severity in each transdiagnostic symptom domain, and this association remained significant after accounting for pubertal status. These results align with and extend previous work suggesting emotion regulation impairment as a mechanism linked to symptom dimensions in youth. 3,6,11 For instance, a recent study in a clinical sample of youth with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), ADHD, and anxiety disorders reported associations with emotion regulation impairment and severity of externalizing and internalizing symptoms. 6 Moreover, studies leveraging community-based samples have reported associations between emotion regulation impairment and domains of psychopathology, including attentional-related, externalizing, and internalizing problems. 11,31,34 
We also found that greater use of suppression was associated with greater severity across each symptom

Follow-up analyses also indicated that suppression was linked to reduced reappraisal use and greater internalizing severity (Figure S2, available online). In contrast, reappraisal was not significantly associated with symptom severity cross-sectionally, which diverges from research suggesting a protective role. 35 Considering that cognitive regulation abilities (eg, use of reappraisal) show age-related improvements, 57 the absence of significant associations with reappraisal may be attributed to the present sample that comprised early adolescents (aged 13-14 years). Indeed, previous literature reporting significant associations of reappraisal with symptoms has focused on a broader age range (eg, 13-18 years). 35 Another possible explanation could be that the effective use of reappraisal may depend on developmental or contextual factors that were not necessarily captured in this sample. In support of this, studies have reported lower use of adaptive regulation strategies during early adolescence. 41 
Sex-Specific Associations of Emotion Dysregulation and Strategy Use With Transdiagnostic Symptom Domains Contrary to our hypotheses, no sex interactions with emotion regulation impairment or specific strategies were observed for any transdiagnostic symptom domain cross-sectionally. The literature on sex differences is limited, and our study may not be directly comparable to previous research reporting significant effects. Prior studies have examined specific symptom domains 14 (eg, defiance) or have relied on youth self-reported symptoms across broader age groups, 15 whereas we assessed parent-reported symptoms in a narrow developmental window. In follow-up analyses, removing cognition from the models resulted in a significant sex interaction with emotion regulation difficulties for externalizing problems as well as poorer model fit. Future research is needed to understand whether cognition suppresses sex differences in emotion regulation or whether these variables interact in a non-linear way. Future research is also needed to examine whether sex differences in emotion regulation emerge only for specific symptoms or are differentially associated with youth self-report vs parent-reported symptoms. 
Although there were no significant cross-sectional interactions, sex interactions were observed prospectively, consistent with prior work suggesting that differences in how male and female youth express their emotions are dynamic across development. 58 In particular, 
sex interactions with suppression were prospectively observed for attention problems, where greater use of suppression was associated with greater severity of attention problems in female youth. Although increased use of suppression in females has been linked to internalizing symptoms predominantly in adult samples, little is known regarding suppression in female youth with ADHD. A possible interpretation is that suppression may be used as a compensatory mechanism during early- to midadolescence in female youth to mask symptoms of attention problems. In support of this, emerging evidence suggests masking of symptoms in female individuals with ASD and other mental health conditions, which might involve cognitive suppression. 59,60 It is important to note that the majority of studies focusing on specific regulation domains in youth are relatively few compared to adult studies. We also found a significant retrospective interaction between sex and emotion regulation difficulties. Specifically, male youth who currently demonstrate greater emotion regulation difficulties displayed greater externalizing problems earlier in development than their female counterparts with comparable current emotion regulation difficulties. Overall, the longitudinal findings suggest that emotion regulation may be an important developmental marker for psychopathology. 
Mediation of Emotion Regulation on ACEs and Transdiagnostic Symptoms Emotion regulation was found to mediate the link between family conflict and severity of each symptom domain. These findings suggest a potential pathway linking emotion regulation impairments to family conflict and child psychopathology. Thus, our findings replicate and extend prior work linking the following: (1) exposure to ACEs and child psychopathology, 21,23 and (2) emotion regulation impairments to psychopathology. 11,31 Prior research suggests that family dysfunction is associated with increased severity of externalizing and internalizing symptoms, 23 
potentially mediated by emotion regulation. 24 It is possible that children experiencing higher levels of family conflict may struggle to regulate their emotions effectively, which in turn is associated with greater severity of psychopathology. 24 Reappraisal and suppression strategies emerged as significant mediators only for internalizing symptoms. Specifically, decreased use of adaptive (reappraisal) and increased use of maladaptive (suppression) strategies mediated the association between family conflict and internalizing problems, consistent with prior work showing that specific emotion regulation strategies mediate the link between adversity and psychopathology. 19,32 Taken together, the mediation analyses suggest that family conflict

may foster an emotionally unstable and unpredictable environment, potentially undermining the child’s sense of security in expressing emotional needs and/or exposing them to modelled emotion dysregulation during conflict. 23 
This may impair the development of adaptive regulation strategies, contributing to heightened psychopathology risk. 61 Alternatively, children with greater emotion dysregulation might be more likely to experience parent–child conflict because of maladaptive regulation and/or symptom severity, such as the non-compliance dimension of oppositional defiant disorder. 14,62 
Negative life events showed no significant direct effects on symptom severity, but did show indirect effects via emotion regulation across models. Although prior research has linked exposure to negative life events to psychopathology, 25 these direct effects were not replicated here, suggesting that negative life events alone may not be sufficient to directly impact symptom severity. Rather, their influence appears to operate through emotion regulation difficulties and specific strategies (reappraisal, suppression). It is possible that children who experience negative life events may struggle with emotion regulation, which might in turn heighten their vulnerability to psychopathology. 25 
Therefore, addressing emotion regulation difficulties might be crucial for mitigating the impact of negative life events on psychopathology. These findings underscore the role of emotion dysregulation as a potentially important mechanism linking early adversity with symptom severity and extend previous work, given that few studies have tested the mediating role of emotion regulation domains. 19 In addition, cognitive–behavioral interventions targeting family dynamics, such as conflict and stress, alongside emotion regulation strategies may help reduce the severity of child psychopathology. Understanding pathways that could lead to elevated risk of transdiagnostic symptoms in youth may be crucial for improving targeted interventions for child mental health conditions. 29 
Together, our findings are consistent with and complement prior work suggesting that emotion dysregulation is a transdiagnostic feature shared across symptom dimensions in youth. 3,5,6 This is notable, given the clinical overlap across symptom domains, where emotion regulation impairment contributes significantly to symptom severity. Regarding clinical implications, our findings emphasize the importance of assessing emotion regulation transdiagnostically, regardless of the primary diagnosis, which could inform treatment decisions. For instance, if moderate to severe emotion regulation impairments are reported, these impairments should be targeted during the course of a cognitive–behavioral intervention, regardless of the diagnostic classification. Targeting emotion regulation, 
in general, has been proposed as a tenable option to reduce the risk of behavioral problems, and supports the premise of transdiagnostic interventions addressing emotion dysregulation. 12 
Social Determinants and Health Equity Considerations Although we implemented best-practice recommendations for the use and analysis of ABCD data, 33,63,64 modest differences in socioeconomic and demographic variables were observed between included and excluded participants (Supplement 2, Supplemental Results, Table S19, available online). These differences underscore the importance of interpreting emotion regulation and psychopathology associations within the broader social and structural contexts that influence youth development. It is also important for future work to enhance accessibility and representativeness sampling in studies to ensure diverse samples reflecting heterogeneity necessary for identifying robust behaviorand brain-based biomarkers.

Beyond sample inclusion patterns, socioeconomic and demographic variables within the analytic ABCD cohort were associated with variability in symptom severity, emphasizing that emotion regulation and psychopathology unfold within broader social and structural contexts. So-cioeconomic disadvantage and systemic inequities can shape exposure to adversity, vulnerability to developing psychopathology, and the development of adaptive regulation skills, potentially contributing to disparities in child mental health outcomes. 65,66 These contextual factors underscore the importance of interpreting emotion regulation findings through an equity lens and promoting inclusive, socially informed research. Future work should explicitly examine how social determinants, such as neighborhood disadvantage, economic stress, and racialized or gendered experiences, intersect with emotion regulation processes in order to inform preventive and intervention strategies that advance mental health equity among diverse youth

populations.

There are study limitations that should be noted. First, measures of transdiagnostic symptom domains (CBCL) and emotion dysregulation (DERS) were assessed via parent-report, whereas emotion regulation strategies (ERQ) were assessed via youth self-report. The reliance on single informants may introduce shared method variance and informant bias, particularly given reported discrepancies between child and parent reports, 67 especially for internalizing symptoms among youth. 68,69 This limitation may result in underestimation of certain symptom domains and affect the magnitude of observed associations. However, we selected well-established and reliable measures of child psychopathology 70 (CBCL) and emotion regulation 52 (DERS and ERQ) that have been validated and used extensively in previous ABCD studies. 42,43,71 Second, the ABCD Study began collecting emotion regulation measures at the 3-year follow-up. Considering that emotion regulation begins to develop during early childhood and is shaped by caregivers, 28

Third, replication of findings is needed, particularly to assess the generalizability of current findings to clinical samples. Fourth, based on available measures of exposure to family conflict and negative life events, we were only to evaluate only the effects of overall lifetime exposures assessed in these measures, thus precluding conclusions regarding the effects of timing. Given that the effects of ACEs could vary depending on the timing of exposure, 29,72 it will be important for future work to elucidate the time course of ACEs and symptom onset. Fifth, state-based or direct performance-based measures of explicit emotion regulation, such as reappraisal, were not collected in the ABCD Study. Replication of findings leveraging performance-based measures of emotion regulation would allow direct testing of this hypothesis in future work. The current findings suggest that emotion regulation impairments, as well as the use of suppression, are linked to the severity of transdiagnostic symptoms in youth with sexspecific patterns. Emotion regulation impairments were found to mediate the link between childhood adversity and transdiagnostic symptoms, which emphasizes the critical need for progress in prevention and/or early-intervention approaches to help reduce the occurrence of adversity, such as family conflict.
