1. Introduction

The energy transition toward renewable sources shows important progress and is a critical element in promoting sustainability, but the substantial consumption of fossil fuels is still a constant \[1,2\]. The structural change needed to create a new energy system will demand multiple actions and take a long time to materialize; however, new technologies, less dependent on fossil fuels, will promote this new dynamic \[3\]. Due to its multidisciplinary nature, the definition of energy transition can include various perspectives involving different actors and elements of interest, from the local to the global, including regulation, distribution, and trade, among others \[4\]. The effects of climate change have adversely affected health and stressed the need to substitute fossil fuels. They have also accelerated actions conducive to a far-reaching energy transition through collaboration to achieve the required innovations. This scenario shows that the initial investments necessary for their development may be exceedingly high \[5–7\].

Although there are multiple perspectives to energy transition, the main idea is migrating the current system to other energy sources or alternative energy production technologies \[8\]. Migrating from traditional economic systems toward sustainable development involves sociotechnical transformations that explain how transitions toward sustainability are carried out \[9\]. These transitions can include different perspectives, ranging from the technical, institutional, and economic matters to those including ecology itself, as well as the social and cultural ones, which ultimately reflect in social change \[10\]. Energy demand will increase significantly in the near future as energy resources decrease; consequently, innovative solutions, both technological and non-technological, as well as clean energy production, are both necessary and desirable \[11,12\].

A significant number of studies in this regard focus on Europe, the United States, and OECD economies. However, it is also necessary to analyze the potential role of developing countries in this sector and the demand-based strategies being defined to promote sustainable development under favorable governance conditions and meet international demand \[13–15\]. Contemporary technology is an important starting point; however, it will be necessary to create conditions for promoting the development of new technologies and their introduction into the market, as well as political strategies to address social and financial matters \[16\]. Energy prices have increased significantly in recent years. However, certain products used to generate energy are cheaper than others, even water, which can become more expensive than these products, depending on the process and where it is acquired. In fact, the highest price paid for this energy bonanza has to do with its adverse effects on the environment, society, and the economy \[17\].

In this regard, energy transition is a term that has been gaining traction in energy policy development. Nevertheless, a consensus on its definition is still lacking; for example, in some cases, it can involve increased availability and the modernization of energy services, and there are possible undesired effects in certain situations (increased carbon intensity); under other approaches, it involves the liberalization of the energy sector, or even the sum of efforts to reduce the carbon footprint \[18\]. Although Latin America is not one of the world’s main greenhouse gas (GHG) emitters, its energy sector has an increasing role in producing these gases, and consequently, it tends to generate more significant effects on climate change \[19,20\]. Renewable energies are seen as a necessary vehicle for economic progress, which can help meet energy demands and address environmental problems, especially in emerging economies, although the technological dependence of these economies on advanced ones is considerable \[21–23\].

For its part, South America must develop more efficient and sustainable energy systems \[24\]. Thus, when considering the energy transition, Chile, and mainly Brazil, represent a relatively small market, albeit interesting in terms of investment in renewable energies \[25\]. As part of this transition, these two countries, along with Argentina and Colombia, have expressed their commitment to combat climate change by reducing CO2 emissions, although the proposed goals and achievements should be assessed \[26\] considering the weight of energy consumption as a source of CO2 emissions \[27\]. For example, the region has made progress in implementing its renewable energy development agenda to generate electricity, and globalization has positively impacted the promotion of technological change and provided the necessary stimuli in terms of trade and investment \[28\]. The region must also bear in mind that actions to mitigate climate change must be included in the design of policies within the context of good governance, especially if the prevailing conditions of poverty and inequality are considered \[29\].

Renewable sources include vast water resources, biomass, and others such as solar and wind, which can enhance the electricity sector; however, these renewable sources are heterogeneously distributed in the region \[30\]. Under this approach, concerned with the resources needed to meet water, energy, and food requirements in the future, Latin America and the Caribbean are committing more emphatically to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) related to poverty, the economy, sustainability, governance, and population increase \[31\]. Thus, discussing energy justice involves not just recognizing the availability of energy but also its accessibility and the guarantee of rights, transparency, and fairness concerning it \[32\]. Consequently, in order to drive the energy transition toward renewable sources, it is necessary to generate policies, design strategies, and establish a clear-cut vision based on specific scenarios, involving action plans and road maps supported by tools such as tax incentives and public financing to foster research and development (R&D), develop capabilities, and build regulatory frameworks \[33\].

This explains the lack of studies analyzing the problem from the reality facing different countries in Latin America and the Caribbean, particularly the problem of energy transition from a regional vision that allows for an increased understanding of this phenomenon, as has occurred in advanced economies. In this regard, the research questions of the present study are as follows: 1.- How can the energy supply in Latin America be characterized compared to the most innovative economies and the global context? 2.- How has policy design influenced the energy transition in Latin America? and 3.- Which public policy guidelines can promote the energy transition in Latin America? To answer these questions, the present study sought to characterize the energy supply, both at the global level and among the most innovative economies, and use this information as a point of comparison to analyze the situation in Latin America, and secondly, to evaluate the variables and identify energy supply patterns in different countries and selected years in order to deduce the energy management and policies that these economies have put in place. Based on such analysis, we propose public policy guidelines that could benefit the promotion of the energy transition toward renewable energies in Latin America.

## Policy and regulation for a sustainable energy transition

To move forward in complying with actions to mitigate global warming, the Paris Agreement signatory countries are required to include energy solutions that can coexist with the use of renewable energy. In some cases, these environmentally friendly technologies have reduced installation costs due to technological progress (for example, solar energy and wind energy), allowing both emerging and developing countries to implement these technologies efficiently. For its part, Europe has been a pioneer in generating renewable energies because it has included them in its political agenda. Latin America is a more recent actor, and studies on the impact of policies in this knowledge area are still scarce \[34–37\]. The SDGs promoted by the United Nations (UN) are a series of 17 goals focused on social, economic, and environmental matters. There are 169 interconnected targets associated with these goals. Some are compatible with other initiatives, such as the Paris Agreement, which is compatible with SDG 13 because both are geared to combating climate change \[38–40\].

These goals are also intended to address poverty and inequality, as well as other environmental problems. SDG 7 seeks not only to guarantee energy efficiency and universal access to quality energy services but also to renewable energies to play a central role in the global energy transition, which has not been as swift as expected, and technological innovation will play a strategic role in achieving this goal \[41,42\]. Renewable energies can be used to face climate change by meeting the increasing energy demand while reducing the use of fossil fuel sources. Thus, the policies aimed at producing electricity from renewable sources in Latin America and the Caribbean can include everything from legislative aspects to subsidies. However, heterogeneous results have been obtained under similar conditions, which may be influenced by the macroeconomic environment and other political, regulatory, and financial elements \[43\].

In addition, the purpose of policies aimed at implementing these technologies is to comply with international commitments, address the population increase, and support low-income populations; however, the market is an essential factor that cannot be ignored, and fiscal stimulus schemes have been used to materialize new technologies that can alleviate this situation \[44\]. Given the positive results that Latin America and the Caribbean have accomplished in the area of renewable energy, the potential of untapped resources and the reduced costs of certain technologies present the region as a potential market demanding increased investment is noteworthy \[45\]. Consequently, formulating policies to promote energy efficiency is a necessity for the economic development of any country \[46\]. In fact, energy efficiency is necessary to achieve the technical and economic viability required for a profitable energy transition \[47\]. Even in industrialized countries, experience shows that the energy transition process requires reorganizing the entire system in the long term due to the complexity of its operation. The present challenge is to make these technologies sustainable, efficient, and accepted by society \[48\].

These action plans must consider the context of each country or region. Actions can include elements ranging from energy efficiency to integrating renewable energies \[49\]. However, public policies and action plans should be based on models describing the energy systems to understand better the actions necessary for sustainable energy transition \[50\]. This structural change should consider actions for working on the capture, transport, and consumption of energy and, in general, on elements associated with energy resource management \[51\]. The techno-economic part of the process is known to play a central role in disseminating or scaling up innovations, but additional elements also need to be analyzed \[52\]. Given the current levels of production and consumption, the need to transform sociotechnical systems to protect the ecological environment is essential. In the case of energy, even those interested in fossil fuel industrialization and other actors recognize the pressing task of migrating toward energy systems less likely to affect the environment but that, at the same time, consider economic and social aspects to advance sustainability \[53–55\].

Different barriers make it challenging to achieve renewable energy, among them the need for significant investment and the generation of innovations to take advantage of these sources \[56\]. Energy is considered green or renewable when it comes from environmentally friendly sources, such as water resources, biomass, and wind, among others. Due to its features, Europe is a destination market par excellence \[57\]. Despite the critical role of fossil fuels in obtaining energy, Europe is undoubtedly committed to developing renewable energies and working on energy efficiency issues, although the necessary investments will be significant \[58\]. CO2 emissions increased from 2006 to 2017, which highlights the need to strengthen actions to mitigate this pollutant and move forward with the Paris Agreement \[59\].

In addition, geopolitical factors will have to be addressed as we move toward a green transition \[64\].

## Energy transition in Latin America and the Caribbean

Despite technological advances toward renewable energy sources, efforts must be multiplied. In Brazil, electricity is generated mainly using its water resources, although they use other methods such as nuclear, thermal, and wind \[112,113\]. Other important sources of renewable energy in Brazil are biodiesel and ethanol. In fact, this country is one of the most important ethanol producers in the world \[114\]. However, production is mainly based on first-generation ethanol, which represents a relevant challenge to move toward second-generation ethanol production \[115\]. In fact, the Brazilian industrial sector has an opportunity to mitigate CO2 emissions by implementing different actions, such as improving energy efficiency and using renewable fuels \[116\]. Additionally, the country needs to design climate policies that help to mitigate carbon emissions, not only by the industry but also by the energy sector \[117\]. When it comes to wind energy, progress is most clearly observed in Europe, although there are significant efforts in this field in countries such as Argentina, which has considerable potential; however, the current policies and regulations in the field hamper its progress in the general framework of renewable energies \[118\].

It can be argued that Argentina’s potential to produce renewable energy is second to Brazil’s, not only in terms of its wind capacity but also in terms of solar, hydroelectric, and biofuel prospects \[119,120\]. The case of Colombia is remarkable; its capacity to produce energy from photovoltaic generation at the domestic level is considerable, which demands policies that further commit the country to using renewable energies and thus mitigate greenhouse gases \[121,122\]. On the other hand, different Latin American countries have seen biofuels as an alternative market for the agricultural sector despite concerns about guaranteeing the sector’s sustainability, as shown by the case of bioethanol production in Brazil \[123\]. However, discussing sustainable energy and integral development has to do not only with technical matters but also with economic, societal, and, of course, political ones \[124\]. For example, considering the energy deficit prevailing in the region’s rural areas, photovoltaic technology can help to mitigate this problem \[125\].

Mexico is an important actor because of its petroleum and natural gas reserves, but it has a significant challenge in exploiting renewable sources, where multiple alternative solutions can also be found \[126–128\]. The energy and climate-related policies generated in Chile are a product of neoliberalism, and the efforts in this area focused on energy efficiency and renewable energy are even more recent \[129\]. The domestic energy supply in Chile is highly dependent on foreign energy. Of the total primary energy supply, petroleum is the most important source; however, firewood is a relevant source of biomass, among other alternatives \[130\]. Global interest in the production of biofuels has given rise to policies geared toward this goal. For example, Europe as a whole is a relevant actor in biodiesel production, but the role of the United States, Brazil, and Argentina is also noteworthy \[131\]. The role of the government in defining strategies for using renewable energies can be addressed in two ways: first, by defining short-term strategies, including subsidies, tax reductions, and CO2 emission penalties, and second, by outlining long-term strategies using price regulation (tariffs), quota systems (committed amount of energy from renewable sources), marketing of certificates, net metering, and auction (bidding for renewable energy production) \[132\].

## Geopolitics and global strategies for a new energy order

For its part, geopolitics represents the capacity of a country and the weight it can exert on another’s behavior. In the energy field, it refers to the power of a country to control its primary energy resources for the benefit of its economic development and other interests, such as national security. The international relationships between countries are influenced by the location of energy resources and the development of technologies to use them; therefore, technological change and an environmentally friendly energy transition will also represent a geopolitical transformation \[140\]. In this regard, Latin America and the Caribbean face significant challenges if they are to take advantage of the region’s abundant resources for the benefit of economic and social development. These challenges include policies to promote technological development, legislation, the regulations of each economy, and the review of trade agreements concerning energy with other countries.

Our literature review shows that the number of studies concerning energy and the energy transition is more significant in Europe and the United States, and they show the main advances and controversies around the sustainable energy transition in these territories; therefore, the discussion on these matters is more developed there. Although studies addressing the situation in different countries of Latin America and the Caribbean provide a general understanding of the scenario for the multiple economies that comprise the region, it is clear that, given the heterogeneous nature and the resources these countries have, it is necessary to conduct studies to characterize the energy supply at the global level and among the most innovative economies as a starting point to deepen the analysis of Latin America and the Caribbean; moreover, it is necessary to identify energy supply patterns in different countries and years to determine how energy supply policies and management are being approached and, based on this, propose guidelines for Latin America concerning the promotion of the energy transition towards renewable energies.

The proportion of clean and renewable energy is still considered low, but its growth speed has accelerated due to the push toward energy transition \[141\]. In recent years, South Asia’s economy has grown substantially. However, barriers to energy supply are constant because of characteristics particular to each region or country, which condition this supply to internal resources and ultimately reflect on a lack of attention to the energy demand \[142\]. Asia and Europe stand out as stand out as the main demanders of this energy resource. For example, Japan is at the top of the hydrogen economy, and China is the country that produces the most hydrogen globally; nevertheless, the need to move forward in the production of clean hydrogen has been emphasized, and despite advances in terms of technical feasibility, the production and economic viability challenges are still present \[143,144\].

The distribution of renewable energy sources is heterogeneous, and their availability is variable in each country. Thus, each region presents a different potential. In the case of Africa, the lack of electricity and other energy sources precedes the concern about migrating to renewable sources, although doing so would be a valuable alternative for this group of countries \[145\]. Sub-Saharan Africa shows significant development and potential in the use of economically viable photovoltaic technologies for producing electricity \[146\]. Interestingly, studies in African countries show that remittances have played a preponderant role in the modernization and accessibility of domestic energy services \[147\]. However, Africa and its industries are notorious for their negative environmental impact and their dependence on polluting energy sources. Although an important gap remains about the state of the energy transition in this continent, several countries can seize the different renewable energy sources available in the region, and some of them are already moving forward in this direction \[148,149\].

In this context, it is pertinent to talk about energy justice, given its current importance and transdisciplinary approach. The concept refers to the equity that energy systems must promote to decrease possible energy access inequalities \[150\]. However, other studies question the justice of the energy transition process between developed economies and developing countries \[151\]. 3. Methods and data

The study of sustainable energy transition has been approached by using multiple tools, such as indices based on multi-criteria analysis or designed scenarios, to evaluate not only the phenomenon of the energy transition but also the required technological change \[160,161\]. There are multiple tools capable of carrying out different levels of analysis. However, to reduce dimensionality and provide a better data visualization through two-dimensional scatter diagrams, unsupervised learning techniques, such as t-SNE, have been found to be extremely useful; it is based on the repulsion of the total number of points, followed by the attraction of the closest neighbors to each of these points \[162,163\]. On the other hand, PCA is a dynamic alternative widely applied in different fields of knowledge; its main purpose is to explore the data structure to obtain and restate the most significant information and use it in various applications \[164\]. This algorithm has the peculiarity of reducing the dimensionality of the data, which has the advantage of explaining most of the variance, which helps to simplify and visualize the study of a phenomenon \[165\].

The t-SNE algorithm was selected because of its usefulness for dimensionality reduction and visualization \[166,167\]. In certain technical applications, the combination of t-SNE and K-means is useful, first, to reduce dimensions, and second, to carry out groupings \[168\]. On the other hand, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) has many applications, for example, to build indices focused on topics ranging from economic policy to sustainability viewed from their environmental and socioeconomic aspects \[169,170\]. This method is based on transforming variables possibly correlated into smaller sets of non-correlated variables, making it an ideal vehicle for carrying out factor analysis, which includes classification and study aspects of decision-making units \[171\].

The first step consisted in carrying out a Machine Learning analysis using the unsupervised t-SNE method with R \[172\] to identify patterns through clusters based on total energy supply in all countries (a total of 49 because data for some regions and the world were excluded) and years (1971–2021) using the International Energy Agency (IEA) \[173\] database, considering the following criteria: 1.- A total of 2499 observations, of nine variables (coal, peat, and oil sale; crude, NGL and feedstocks; electricity; heat; natural gas; nuclear; oil products; renewables and waste; total) were loaded; these variables correspond to the flow given by the total supply of energy; and 2.- Different perplexity options and iterations (learning cycles) were evaluated for the application of the t-SNE method, and the best option was considered to be the one with the lowest error; thus, the algorithm was ran assigning 45 of perplexity and 500 iterations, which resulted in an error of 0.96. Consequently, the tests were stopped because, although we increased the iterations, only a slight variation in error reduction was obtained. Subsequently, a filter was created to graphically display results, considering data from 2014 to 2021 only.

The second stage consisted in carrying out a PCA to study the variables (excluding the total) associated with energy supply and their relationship with data associated with different countries and years. This was accomplished using the following three levels of analysis: 1.- As in the previous case, data on total energy supply for all countries and years were used. With this information, KMO was evaluated, obtaining an Overall MSA value = 0.6; 2.- To analyze the behavior of these variables in the most innovative countries (Switzerland, United States, Sweden, United Kingdom, Netherlands, Republic of Korea, Singapore, Germany, Finland, Denmark), according to the Global Innovation Index (IIG) - World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) \[174\], KMO was calculated and an Overall MSA value = 0.69 was obtained; and 3.- Finally, the same exercise was carried out for countries in Latin America and the Caribbean in the database (Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Mexico, and Argentina), obtaining an Overall MSA value = 0.77.

5. Conclusions

Globally, the generated energy supply varies significantly between the most innovative countries and the Latin American region. The pattern analysis shows economies as large and dynamic as the United States, China, and India positioned in group 1. However, the rest of the economies in the Americas are positioned in groups 2 and 3. This illustration shows that energy supply patterns are determined not only by whether the country is developed or developing but also by the fact that population size and energy demand play a fundamental role. This clearly reflects the need to carry out technical and economic feasibility studies to determine with greater certainty the type of energy supply required by each country and, thereby, identify the main obstacles to an energy transition in which the use of renewable energies and waste are the predominant model. In addition, actions aimed at reviewing and generating new proposals in the area of public policy and regulation must be taken to build the operating frameworks to make this transition possible. For its part, the PCA shows that the United States’s economy is more associated with energy sources such as natural gas, nuclear, petroleum, and intermediate products and that Asia has the most explicit association with the transition to renewable energy and waste, as well as to coal and mineral sources.

Regarding the most innovative economies, the study reveals that, when it comes to the energy transition to renewables and waste as sources, the United States makes the most considerable contribution in terms of an innovative country and that European and Asian countries are more associated with heat and petroleum products. Concerning Latin America, Brazil makes the largest contribution in this category. In contrast, Mexico has the highest energy supply based on petroleum products and natural gas. The increase in energy supply in the region was significant from 2000 to 2020, that is, it increased from 18,984 to 25,948 PJ in the selected countries, but the results in Brazil with respect to renewable energy and waste are a clear reflection of the policies that the country has implemented. These advances are certainly not problem-free; for example, the production of first-generation bioethanol represents a dilemma against using resources for energy or food. Consequently, it is necessary to formulate and design public policies to encourage research and development (R&D), especially those promoting endogenous technology to produce biofuels, such as second-generation bioethanol from lignocellulosic sources. Thus, when discussing renewable sources and the use of waste products, it is important to consider the region where the policy is to be implemented, as well as the laws and programs that will materialize the policy; to do so, aspects such as geopolitics, geography, and technological development must be taken into account, as well as the available resources.

For example, in the case of biofuels, certain economies in Latin America and the Caribbean can play a strategic role in the development of this area of knowledge, for example, Brazil, which has an important biomass production potential for this purpose. Other economies should guide their efforts toward developing other energy sources, such as solar and wind energy. Thus, incentives to promote a sustainable energy transition can include different elements, such as innovation plans, R&D subsidies, tax incentives, legal and regulatory frameworks, foreign direct investment (FDI), and others, such as infrastructure creation programs and stimulating new value chains. Latin American countries need to take stronger actions to accomplish the energy transition toward renewable energies and the use of waste to obtain solid results. However, this transition must be carried out in an orderly and planned way to avoid an energy supply crisis and its associated problems. Policies must be based on different knowledge fields, including the industry and its role in cleaner production, environmentally friendly infrastructure and technology, and, of course, building capacities to adopt present and future technological change.

The actions of each country in Latin America and the Caribbean will depend on the capacities of each economy; therefore, an analysis of the context, regulations, and economic, social, environmental, and cultural aspects is mandatory. However, regardless of the results in each case, the global trend is to reduce the use of fossil sources, although the adoption of renewable and environmentally friendly energy sources will require policies based on further studies to promote the technical, social, and economic viability of these new technologies. The main limitation of the present study is its general nature; although it allowed us to generate a first approach to the energy situation in these countries, it does not include the total number of economies due to a lack of data. Therefore, future research lines should, in the first place, focus on more economies as sufficient data become available, and in the second place, study each of these countries in greater detail precisely to learn more about their problems and the specific progress and challenges that each country faces in order to formulate policies that will effectively allow them to move toward a sustainable energy transition. In addition, economies should study their capacities to adopt and develop these new technologies and, consequently, create robust regulatory frameworks for their adequate operation.
