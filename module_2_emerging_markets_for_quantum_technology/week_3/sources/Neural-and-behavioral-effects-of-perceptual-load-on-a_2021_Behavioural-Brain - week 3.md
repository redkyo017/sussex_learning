1. Introduction

Everyday tasks requiring focal attention occur at varying levels of perceptual and cognitive demand. One may be reading a book in a noisy environment. For which type of reading will the noise be more distracting: light or dense? Theorists of attention have grappled with that question for tasks differing in perceptual load, that is, the perceptual demand required by the task \[1\]. The preponderance of research studies manipulating perceptual load has examined its effects on visual attention (e.g. \[2–4\]). The aim of the current study was to contrast two distinct views of the role of perceptual load on auditory attention. We manipulated auditory perceptual load in a single experiment and investigated its neural effects, using event-related potential (ERP) and source analysis, and its behavioral effects, using three distinct measures of attentional interference.

## Perceptual load theory

Lavie and her colleagues \[1,5–8\] proposed a link between the level of task-based perceptual load and the pool of processing resources available to perform mental analyses of task-irrelevant information.

According to perceptual load theory, a task that places strong demands on perceptual processing yields relatively few resources to process extraneous information, thereby keeping the distracting influences of task-irrelevant information at bay. Conversely, a task that places few demands on perceptual processing makes ample resources available to process both task-relevant and task-irrelevant information, leading to relatively greater attentional intrusion.

Extensive physiological and behavioral evidence has been amassed in support of perceptual load theory \[2–4,6,9,10,88\]. Pessoa et al. \[10\] found, for example, that the perceptual load manipulated in an orientation discrimination task modulated activity in the amygdala (measured as BOLD signal in fMRI) when viewing task-irrelevant emotional faces. Amygdala activity to the faces was significantly weaker when the discrimination task was made more difficult (i.e., high perceptual load). Evidence from ERPs indicates an early time course to the attentional effects of perceptual load. Handy et al. \[9\] found that the amplitude of the P1 ERP component to visual distractors (100 ms after stimulus onset) was significantly smaller during a more demanding visual identification task (see also \[11,12\]).

In addition to the flanker effect, we examined two other standard measures of attentional interference: Garner interference \[17\] and the Gratton effect \[18\].

## Tectonic theory of attention

Melara and Algom \[19\] proposed that the magnitude of attentional interference from task-irrelevant information during selection is proportional to the relative perceptual distinctiveness between values along the target and distractor dimensions. If perceptual change across trials is relatively greater to distractors, then observers will undergo greater attentional interference than if perceptual change is greater to targets. This principle has been observed over a wide variety of attentional tasks, including Stroop tasks \[20–22\], Garner tasks \[23\], Simon tasks \[24\], and go/no-go tasks \[25,26\], in both the visual and auditory modalities (see \[27\], for a review). According to their tectonic theory of attention, the processing basis of the effects of relative distinctiveness on attentional interference resides in a failure of frontal inhibitory processing when distractor change is relatively large, which allows distractor information to influence target decisions.

Melara et al. \[26\] studied the effects of relative distinctiveness in auditory perception. As the perceived salience of distractors increased from low to medium to high (across separate blocks of trials), participants’ behavioral performance progressively deteriorated: They were slower, committed more misidentifications of targets, and made more false alarms to distractors. These behavioral changes across conditions were accompanied by a monotonic reduction in an ERP component called Rejection Positivity or RP, occurring 600 ms after distractor onset. The authors concluded that as distractor salience sharpened, participants were steadily less able to exert inhibitory control over distraction, which consequently undermined their task performance. Chen and Melara \[28\] simulated the behavioral results of Melara et al. \[26\] in a formal model of inhibitory processing, as measured by RP. Their computational results were consistent with the hypothesis that RP activity influences the speed of responding to targets during selective attention through the inhibitory modulation of distractors.

## Distinct theoretical predictions

As in Melara et al. \[26\] study, most manipulations of relative distinctiveness compare performance in discriminating a constant perceptual difference between targets across two or more levels of distractor distinctiveness. Thus, perceptual load is usually held constant. However, if the perceptual distinctiveness of the target dimension (i.e., the demands on perceptual processing of the target) were varied instead, while the distractor dimension was held constant, it would be tantamount to manipulating perceptual load. Notably, the predictions of tectonic theory remain the same whether target or distractor distinctiveness is varied, namely, the less distinctive values along the target dimension relative to the distractor dimension the more the attentional interference.

Viewed in this way, perceptual load theory and tectonic theory make opposite predictions. According to perceptual load theory, if target values are perceptually less distinctive from each other, distractors should be less intrusive because relatively little capacity is available to process them. By contrast, according to tectonic theory, distractors should be more intrusive if target values are less distinctive because inhibitory processes are placed under strain from representational variability \[29\]. The goal of the current study was to pit these two predictions against each other in the context of an auditory selective attention task.

## Manipulations of auditory perceptual load

Investigators have explored alternative methods for varying auditory perceptual load (see \[15\], for a review). One approach is to manipulate set size, that is, the number of non-targets in the display. For example, Fairnie et al. \[30\] asked participants to identify an animal sound as either a dog or a lion while the number of other animal sounds appearing concurrently ranged from zero to five. As set size increased, participants were less sensitive in detecting a distinct distractor sound (car horn), in line with perceptual load theory. Yet increases in auditory set size also increase masking and/or dilution effects, perhaps creating a psychophysical rather than resource restriction on distractor detection \[14,15\]. A second approach is to vary the target task while keeping constant the number of distractor stimuli in the display. For example, Francis \[31\] asked participants to make target decisions on the basis of a single auditory feature (amplitude; low load) or a conjunction of features (amplitude and pitch; high load). Distractor congruity effects were relatively smaller under high perceptual load, again in concert with perceptual load theory. Yet changes in the cognitive task (feature vs. conjunction search) may draw on distinct cognitive processes, rather than simply depleting cognitive resources (as implied by perceptual load theory), thereby perhaps affecting the mechanisms of distractor processing. The approach applied in the current study – namely, varying target distinctiveness while leaving the cognitive task and number of stimuli the same between low load and high load conditions – was used to deplete perceptual resources while drawing on a single cognitive process, thus avoiding several of the pitfalls inherent in these alternative approaches.

## Three measures of attentional disruption

The current study implemented a multi-pronged approach to probe the effects of auditory perceptual load on disruptions to selective attention. Three attention measures were employed. First, Garner interference \[17\] is a measure of task-level disruption: Performance (speed and accuracy) in a filtering task, in which distractor values are varied randomly from trial to trial within a block, is compared with performance in a baseline task, in which distractor values are held constant \[23,32\]. Garner interference measures the disruptive effects of task-irrelevant variation on performance: The greater the difference in performance between filtering and baseline the greater the attentional interference. To rule out the influence of specific distractor values on task performance in low versus high load conditions, only neutral trials (i.e., when the flanker is a neutral tone) are used in analysis of Garner interference.

The flanker congruity effect (e.g., \[33–36\]) is a measure of stimulus-level disruption: Performance on congruent trials, in which target and distractor values match (e.g., high-pitched target, high-pitched distractor), is compared with performance on incongruent trials, in which target and distractor values mismatch (e.g., high-pitched target, low-pitched distractor) \[37,38\]. Whereas Garner interference assesses the influence of task-irrelevant variation on performance, the flanker effect assesses the influence of the kind of variation (i.e., corresponding or conflicting with the target value) (see \[27,39\]).

Finally, the Gratton effect \[18\] gauges how memory of the previous

## The present study

Participants were tested in a variation of the traditional flanker task \[48\], called the auditory flanker paradigm (cf \[49,50\]), which required them to identify the pitch of a target while ignoring the pitch of flanking distractors. Perceptual load was manipulated by varying the perceptual distance in pitch between the two possible targets: 1200-Hz separation (low perceptual load) or 800-Hz separation (high perceptual load), with distractors held constant at a perceptual distance of 1000 Hz. Thus, the perceptual difficulty in distinguishing different targets versus different distractors was relatively greater along the target dimension in the low perceptual load condition (1200 Hz \[targets\] vs. 1000 Hz \[distractors\]) and relatively greater along the distractor dimension in the high perceptual load condition (800 Hz \[targets\] vs. 1000 Hz \[distractors\]). We measured attentional interference in target classification using the flanker effect \[48\], the Garner effect \[32\], and the Gratton effect \[18\]. We also examined the load effects on early (P1) and late (RP) ERP components to the initial distractor, which was physically identical across the low load and high load conditions. Finally, we performed a dipole source analysis of the early ERP component to probe the neural sources of perceptual load effects. In sum, the present study employed a novel definition of auditory perceptual load, which circumvented problems of earlier definitions, to simultaneously assess its behavioral and electrophysiological consequences on attentional interference in a multi-pronged approach.

## Participants

Thirty-two participants (22 females, average age = 20.3 years), recruited from the City College of New York, were given course credit for participation in the study. The nature of the procedures was explained fully, and informed consent was obtained from each participant; the Institutional Review Board of the City University of New York approved the protocol. All participants had normal hearing with no history of neurological disorder (self-report).

## Stimulus, apparatus, and procedure

Stimuli were square-wave tones in the 1-kHz range digitized to 16 bits at a sampling rate of 48 kHz, created in Adobe Audition®, presented binaurally at 66 dB(A) on Sennheiser HD-280 headphones. Each participant was seated in a comfortable chair inside an electrically and acoustically shielded Industrial Acoustics Company (New York) chamber while EEG was recorded (see below for details). Each test session contained 16 blocks of 100 experimental trials (and four blocks of practice trials). Trials were presented in Presentation® (Neuro-behavioral Systems) on a Dimension 5150 Dell desktop computer. Each trial consisted of a fixation square (.67◦) (presented on a Dell Model P1130 RGB computer monitor with a refresh rate of 75 Hz) followed by three auditory stimuli presented sequentially: (1) First Flanker, (2) Target, and (3) Second Flanker (see Fig. 1). Each sound lasted 100 ms (10 ms rise/fall) separated by an inter-stimulus interval varying between 154 ms and 350 ms in random distribution (also varied randomly between First Flanker-Target interval and the Target-Second Flanker interval). The first and second flankers were identical on each trial: a low-

pitched tone (500 Hz), a high-pitched tone (1500 Hz), or a neutral tone (1000 Hz).

Test sessions were divided into 2 baseline and 2 filtering conditions \[32\], each repeated four times, with task order balanced across participants, and perceptual load (low vs. high) varied between the two baseline conditions or the two filtering conditions. In low perceptual load, targets differed in pitch by 1200 Hz (400 Hz vs. 1600 Hz), a relatively easy classification; in high perceptual load, targets differed by 800 Hz (600 Hz vs. 1400 Hz), a relatively difficult classification. Employing these target values in the presence of a constant distractor difference (i.e., 1000 Hz: 500 Hz vs. 1500 Hz), perceptual difficulty in distinguishing between different targets versus different distractors was relatively greater along the target dimension in the low perceptual load condition (1200 Hz \[targets\] vs 1000 Hz \[distractors\]) and relatively greater along the distractor dimension in the high perceptual load condition (800 Hz \[targets\] vs 1000 Hz \[distractors\]).

A pilot study was conducted to validate the load manipulation. Twenty-one participants (none from the experiment proper) were asked to discriminate target tones alone (i.e., without distractor tones) in the low load and high load conditions (100 trials in each condition; RSI varied randomly between 1000− 1200 ms; conditions counterbalanced between participants). In baseline tasks, flankers on each trial were neutral tones (see Fig. 1). Baseline tasks performed below 80 % accuracy were repeated immediately. In filtering tasks, the three types of flankers (low-pitch tone, high-pitch tone, or neutral tone) appeared randomly as a pair on each trial: On 40 % of trials (40 of 100) target and flanker tones matched in relative pitch (both low or both high; congruent trials), on 20 % (20 of 100) they mismatched (incongruent trials), and on 40 % flankers were neutral. We used different probabilities for congruent and incongruent trials to increase distractor salience (greater stimulus uncertainty) on incongruent trials, a standard procedure in conflict paradigms (e.g., Stroop and flanker paradigms; see \[51,52\]; and MacLeod \[38\] for a review of effects of trial type probabilities). Congruent, incongruent and neutral trials were randomly distributed within the task. Flankers always matched each other on each trial.

On each trial of each task participants were asked to respond by mouse key as quickly and accurately as possible to the pitch (low vs. high) of the target tone, ignoring the flanker tones. Assignment of target pitch to response keys was counterbalanced across participants. Partic-ipants were given short breaks throughout testing. The entire experiment, including EEG preparation, lasted approximately 3 h.

## Data recording and analysis

Participants performed the auditory flanker paradigm in baseline (only neutral flankers) and filtering (congruent, incongruent, and neutral flankers) tasks under both low and high perceptual load while behavioral and electrophysiological measures were made, allowing us to derive three separate indices of selective attention: Garner interference (difference between baseline and filtering tasks), flanker congruity (difference between congruent and incongruent trials), and the Gratton effect (magnitude of flanker congruity following congruent vs. incongruent trial sequence). Response accuracy (percent correct) and RTs to correct response trials were averaged for each participant in each condition.

We performed repeated measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs) on behavioral data using Statistica® software, with Load (2 levels: low and high) and Task (2 levels: baseline and filtering) as within-subject factors. 4. Discussion

The results of the present study depict a coherent picture of the effects of perceptual load on selective attention in an auditory conflict task. Perceptual load was created here by varying the difficulty of perceptual classification between two targets while holding the perceptual distinctiveness of distractors constant. Observers experienced greater attentional interference under high versus low perceptual load, whether analyses were performed between tasks (Garner interference; found in accuracy and RT), between stimuli (flanker congruity; found in accuracy), or between sequences (Gratton effect; found in accuracy). Electrophysiological recordings to the distractor, which was physically identical across load conditions, revealed load modulation of tasks effects in P1 (peak amplitude and latency), an early perceptual component of the ERP, peaking approximately 75 ms after distractor onset. ## Effectiveness of load manipulation

The current study used the perceived difference in the pitch of target sounds, identified in a pilot study conducted on target sounds alone, to define perceptual load: Load was low when observers were asked to identify tones that were relatively more distinct in pitch (1200 Hz: 400 Hz vs 1600 Hz) and high when observers were asked to identify tones that were relatively less distinct in pitch (800 Hz: 600 Hz vs. 1400 Hz). The difficulty of perceptual identification is a common definition of perceptual load. In the visual modality, for example, Pessoa et al. \[10\] varied the difficulty of line orientation, with relatively small change in orientation defining high perceptual load. We verified the effectiveness of our manipulation by examining the speed and accuracy of perceptual identification in a pilot study (i.e., distractor tones absent) and in accuracy at baseline (i.e., only neutral distractor tones: 94 % \[low load\] vs. 90 % \[high load\]). As expected, participants were significantly less accurate at target identification during the high versus low load condition.

## Theoretical implications

The current study adopted a multipronged methodology to probe the effects of load on auditory selective attention. We explored how load affects distractor processing physiologically and behaviorally at the level of stimuli (flanker congruity), task (Garner interference), and sequence (Gratton effect). The influence of perceptual load was seen in all three measures, at least in accuracy: Garner interference, flanker congruity, and the Gratton effect were all greatest under high perceptual load. Nevertheless, of the three attention measures, the task effects were the strongest. Here we found that Garner interference, in both speed and accuracy, was greatest under high perceptual load and, moreover, the difference in P1 amplitude and latency between the baseline and filtering tasks was greatest under high load.

The pattern hints to the processing source of auditory load effects. Algom and Fitousi \[27\] and others \[19,39\] have noted a conceptual relation between Garner interference and flanker congruity: The former betrays an influence of task-irrelevant variation on performance whereas the latter indicates an influence of the kind of variation (i.e., corresponding or conflicting with the target value). Moreover, the Gratton effect, a form of flanker congruity, gauges how memory of the previous trial in the sequence affects recognition of the kind of variation during the current trial. By demonstrating that high load magnifies the disruptive effects of task-irrelevant variation on performance our results suggest that perceptual load boosts stimulus uncertainty during auditory selective attention. As the perceptual classification increased in difficulty, observers were especially attuned to the average uncertainty or surprisingness \[64–67\] of trial-to-trial changes in distractor values. Distractors in the filtering task, which can take on one of three values across trials (i.e., neutral, low pitch, high pitch), are more uncertain on any particular trial, and hence more distracting, than distractors in the baseline task, which take on only one value during the task (i.e., neutral). Our results suggest that the disruptive effects of uncertainty are especially acute under high auditory perceptual load.

The results of the current study are new. Previous studies, primarily investigating the visual modality, have mainly shown an edge in attentional processing when perceptual load is high \[2–4,6,9,10,88\]. Even studies in the auditory modality have normally not reported an attentional advantage when perceptual load is low (e.g., Gomes et al. \[68\] and Murphy et al. \[16\] found no differences between low and high load conditions). Yet our results correspond well with those reported by Melara et al. \[26\]. In that study, as this, performance faltered as relative

distinctiveness between stimulus values favored the distractor dimension. Melara et al. varied the distinctiveness of distractors across conditions, keeping target distinctiveness constant. By contrast, we varied the distinctiveness of targets, keeping distractor differences constant. Thus, using the lens of relative distinctiveness, our findings fit with the view espoused in tectonic theory that distractor salience determines the degree of selective attention failure. This conclusion is consistent with a large literature in both visual and auditory attention (\[20,21,23,89,90; see \[27\], for a review).

What accounts for the difference between our results and those of other investigations exploring the effects of perceptual load on attention? One possibility considers the specific processing demands of the attention paradigm we employed. Kahneman and Treisman \[69\] divided the selection paradigms most commonly used in attention research into two categories: filtering and selective set. In filtering paradigms, including the auditory flanker paradigm used here, participants are asked to focus on signals from a task-relevant channel (e.g., pitch of the target) while disregarding signals from a task-irrelevant channel (e.g., pitch of the flankers). In selective-set paradigms, including visual search (e.g. \[70,71\],), a common procedure in many visual perceptual load studies \[5\], participants must locate and identify one stimulus among several other possible stimuli. The two paradigms impose different information processing burdens on attention. During selective set, a premium is placed on mechanisms of comparison, so attentional resources are drained as the number of items or dimensions of comparison increase. By contrast, during filtering, attention acts as a sorting center to separate signal (task relevant) from noise (task irrelevant). Here, a premium is placed on mechanisms of suppression, so distractors high in noise (variability) should impair processing efficiency. On this view, models of attentional resources, such as perceptual load theory, would emerge most naturally from results obtained with selective-set paradigms (e.g., visual search), whereas models of representational variability, such as tectonic theory, would emerge from results obtained with filtering paradigms (e.g., auditory flanker).

## Neural sources of attentional disruption

Results of the current study suggest that the effects of auditory load on distractor processing occurred early, modulating the P1 ERP component, 40− 90 ms after flanker onset. Indeed, the effect of load on P1 emerged even before target onset (i.e., to the initial flanker), indicating that attention was disrupted at the level of the task block, consistent with behavioral effects of our task-level measure, Garner interference (i.e., filtering task vs. baseline task). Dipole source analysis of the P1 component to the initial flanker revealed bilateral activation of the insula (Brodmann area 13), a region that has been associated with perceptual processing of pitch \[72–74\], in both the low and high load conditions. However, under low target load, but not high load, an additional dipole was found in the anterior prefrontal cortex (BA 10), an area involved in executive functioning \[75\], particularly with regard to working memory \[76–79\] and inhibitory control \[80–82\]. One explanation of prefrontal activation under low load suggests suppression of task-irrelevant information in working memory during filtering, which consequently dampened P1 amplitude to baseline levels, yielding insignificant levels of Garner interference in distractor P1.

By contrast, source analysis indicated the absence of prefrontal safeguards in the high load condition, suggesting a dearth of distractor inhibitory control during the P1 epoch. Perhaps as a consequence, P1 amplitude to distractors in the filtering condition was amplified re: baseline, which may explain the outsized role distractors played in undermining behavioral performance when perceptual load was high. Yet dipole source analysis also identified high perceptual load with activation of the posterior cingulate cortex (Brodmann area 23/31), a central component of the default mode network (DMN; \[83\]). Thus, when perceptual load is low, executive control mechanisms operate quickly to inhibit intrusive distractor activation. However, when perceptual load is high, later or sustained mechanisms are unavailable to prevent distractor interference.

5. Conclusions

The results of the present study are important in demonstrating an inverse relationship between distractor salience, varied here using perceptual load, and prefrontal control of distractor information. We found that low auditory perceptual load allowed strong prefrontal inhibitory control over the distractor P1 component, a transient index of early auditory distractor processing. Prefrontal control during the P1 epoch was accompanied by good selective attention under low load. By contrast, activation of a region of the DMN during P1 was accompanied by poor selective attention under high load. Murphy et al. \[16\] proposed that auditory attention is special in serving as an early alert system to unseen environmental change. Our results suggest that this system is sensitive to the relative degree of task-irrelevant change in the environment. The more salient the lion’s growl the more likely we are to notice him.
