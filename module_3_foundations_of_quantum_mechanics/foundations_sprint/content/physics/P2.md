## Moves

### Move P2.1 — Waves: amplitude, wavelength, frequency, wavenumber (10 min)

**Say it aloud.** A **wave** is a disturbance that travels: the medium (rope, air, field) wiggles in place while the pattern of wiggles moves along. Freeze time and you see a repeating shape in space; stand still and you see a repeating oscillation in time.

- **Amplitude** $A$: the largest displacement from rest. Units: those of the displacement (metres for a rope).
- **Wavelength** $\lambda$ ("lambda"): the distance over which the shape repeats. Units: m.
- **Frequency** $f$: complete oscillations per second at one point. Units: Hz $=$ s$^{-1}$.
- **Wave speed** $v$: how fast a crest travels. Since one wavelength passes per period,
$$v=f\lambda.$$
- **Wavenumber** $k=2\pi/\lambda$ ("k"): radians of phase per metre. Units: rad/m, written m$^{-1}$.
- **Angular frequency** $\omega=2\pi f$ ("omega"): radians of phase per second. Units: rad/s, written s$^{-1}$.

A right-moving wave is $y(x,t)=A\cos(kx-\omega t)$, and $v=\omega/k$ (because $\omega/k=2\pi f\cdot\lambda/2\pi=f\lambda$).

**Worked example (rope).** A rope wave has $f=5.0$ Hz and travels at $v=2.0$ m/s, amplitude $A=0.050$ m. Then
$$\lambda=\frac vf=\frac{2.0}{5.0}=0.40\ \text{m},\quad k=\frac{2\pi}{0.40}=5\pi\approx15.7\ \text{m}^{-1},\quad \omega=2\pi(5.0)=10\pi\approx31.4\ \text{s}^{-1}.$$
Check: $\omega/k=31.4/15.7=2.0$ m/s $=v$. Units check: m/s $\div$ s$^{-1}$ = m; $2\pi/$m $=$ m$^{-1}$; $2\pi\cdot$s$^{-1}$ = s$^{-1}$. So $y=0.050\cos(15.7x-31.4t)$ metres.

**Common mixing-up.** $f$ counts cycles, $\omega$ counts radians; $\omega=2\pi f$ always. $\lambda$ and $k$ are similarly related by $2\pi$. In quantum formulas ($E=\hbar\omega$, $p=\hbar k$) the reduced constant $\hbar=h/2\pi$ pairs with $\omega,k$; the unreduced $h$ pairs with $f,\lambda$.

**Quantum bridge.** The wave function of a free particle will be a wave with a definite $k$; "how wiggly is $\psi$ in space" is $k$ (or $\lambda$), and that turns out to be the momentum (Move P2.6).

### Move P2.2 — Superposition, interference, the double slit (10 min)

**Say it aloud.** When two waves meet, the displacements simply add point by point. This is **superposition**. Where crest meets crest the result is bigger (**constructive interference**); where crest meets trough the result shrinks, and equal ones cancel completely (**destructive interference**).

**Why amplitudes add, not intensities.** A wave's *intensity* (brightness, loudness, later: probability density) goes like the square of its amplitude. Amplitudes carry a sign; squares do not. Take two contributions $a_1,a_2$ arriving at the same point. Adding first, then squaring:
$$|a_1+a_2|^2=|a_1|^2+|a_2|^2+2\,\mathrm{Re}(a_1^*a_2).$$
The last term, the **cross term**, is the interference. It is positive when the signs agree, negative when they oppose, and it is exactly what "add probabilities first" throws away.

**Worked example (numbers).** Two real amplitudes $0.3$ and $0.4$ arrive at a point.

| Arrangement | Add amplitudes, then square | Square, then add |
|---|---|---|
| in phase | $(0.3+0.4)^2=0.49$ | $0.09+0.16=0.25$ |
| opposite signs | $(0.3-0.4)^2=0.01$ | $0.09+0.16=0.25$ |

Square-then-add cannot tell the two cases apart, and the wave experiment can. So amplitudes, not their squares, are what add.

**Two-path picture with signs.** Light from two narrow slits, separated by $d$, reaches a distant screen along two paths. Path lengths differ by $\Delta$. If $\Delta=m\lambda$ ($m=0,1,2,\dots$) the two waves arrive crest-to-crest with the same sign: bright. If $\Delta=(m+\tfrac12)\lambda$ they arrive with opposite signs: dark. For a screen at distance $L\gg d$ the bright fringes are evenly spaced, by the standard small-angle result
$$\Delta y=\frac{\lambda L}{d}.$$
(Sketch: bright when $d\sin\theta=m\lambda$; for small $\theta$, $y=L\tan\theta\approx L\theta$, so $y_m=m\lambda L/d$ and neighbours differ by $\lambda L/d$.)

**Worked example (fringes).** $\lambda=600$ nm, $d=0.20$ mm, $L=1.5$ m:
$$\Delta y=\frac{(600\times10^{-9})(1.5)}{0.20\times10^{-3}}=\frac{9.0\times10^{-7}}{2.0\times10^{-4}}=4.5\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}=4.5\ \text{mm}.$$
Units check: m $\cdot$ m $/$ m $=$ m.

**Quantum bridge.** Week 6 §6.4 writes $\psi=\psi_1+\psi_2$, one contribution per path, and takes $|\psi|^2$ as probability density. It is the same arithmetic with the same cross term. The rule "add amplitudes over indistinguishable paths, then square" is the physical content of the Week 2 rule that states are added as vectors.

### Move P2.3 — The complex plane wave $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ (5 min)

**Say it aloud.** A cosine has a peak and a zero-crossing; it forgets the "phase". Euler's formula (Day 1, Move 1.3) rolls the cosine and the sine into one object:
$$e^{i(kx-\omega t)}=\cos(kx-\omega t)+i\sin(kx-\omega t).$$
Its real part is the familiar wave with $A=1$. The imaginary part is the same wave shifted by a quarter of a cycle. As $x$ increases at fixed $t$ the value goes round the unit circle in the complex plane once per wavelength; it also goes round as $t$ increases. The pattern $kx-\omega t$ (constant means fixed crest position) moves right at speed $\omega/k$.

**Why physicists like it.** Differentiating gives a multiple of itself: $\partial_x e^{ikx}=ik\,e^{ikx}$. Sums of phases become products of exponentials.

**Modulus squared is constant.** $|e^{i\varphi}|^2=e^{-i\varphi}e^{i\varphi}=1$, or $\cos^2\varphi+\sin^2\varphi=1$. So the plane wave has the same $|\psi|^2$ everywhere and always: the particle is equally likely to be found anywhere. This is why it is not normalisable (W4 §4.2 says so directly) but is still the right tool for free particles.

**Worked example.** With the rope's $k=5\pi$ rad/m, $\omega=10\pi$ rad/s, at $t=0$: at $x=0.10$ m the phase is $5\pi(0.10)=\pi/2$ and the value is $e^{i\pi/2}=i$; at $x=0.20$ m the phase is $\pi$ and the value is $-1$. Both have modulus 1. At $t=0.050$ s and $x=0.10$ m the phase is $\pi/2-10\pi(0.050)=\pi/2-\pi/2=0$, so the value is 1: the crest that was at $x=0$ has moved to $0.10$ m $=v\,t=2.0\times0.050$. The wave is moving right at 2.0 m/s.

**Convention note.** Lecture Eq. 4.17 writes waves as $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$, so $k>0$ moves right and $k<0$ moves left. The sign is a convention; the lecture also uses $e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}$ (W1, Eq. 1.15), which corresponds to $\omega=E/\hbar$. The Week 4 lecture states this relation itself: §4.1 writes $E=\hbar\omega$ (after Eq. 4.3), and §4.2 writes $\omega=E/\hbar=\hbar k^2/2m$ for the free particle. It is also the Planck relation of Move P2.4 with $\hbar=h/2\pi$.

**Quantum bridge.** $\psi(x)=e^{ikx}$ (Eq. 4.10) is the $t=0$ slice. Moving to the full time dependence multiplies by $e^{-i\omega t}$, a pure phase.

### Move P2.4 — Planck, blackbody, photoelectric effect, photon momentum (10 min)

**Say it aloud.** (The history in this move, including the dates and the names Planck and Einstein, is standard background, not from the lecture.) Three facts around 1900-1905 broke the idea that light is only a continuous wave, and that energy is exchangeable in any amount.

- **Blackbody radiation.** A hot object glows, and the colour shifts from dull red to white as it heats. Classical waves-in-a-box reasoning predicted ever more radiation at short wavelengths, an absurd infinity. Planck fixed it by assuming that the radiating oscillators exchange energy with the field only in lumps of size
$$E=hf,$$
the **Planck relation**. In terms of $\omega$: $E=hf=\hbar\omega$, since $\hbar=h/2\pi$ and $\omega=2\pi f$.
- **Photoelectric effect.** Shine light on a metal and electrons come out, but only if the *frequency* exceeds a **threshold**, however bright the light. Below threshold, no electrons; above, they come out immediately, and brighter light gives more electrons, not faster ones. Einstein's reading: light arrives in packets, **photons**, each with energy $hf$; one photon gives all its energy to one electron. The minimum energy to free an electron from the metal is the **work function** $\phi$. Then
$$K_{\max}=hf-\phi,\qquad f_0=\frac{\phi}{h}\ \text{(threshold)}.$$
- **Photon momentum.** A photon has no rest mass, $E=pc$, so with $E=hf=hc/\lambda$:
$$p=\frac{h}{\lambda}.$$

**Convenient conversion (standard values).** $hc=(6.626\times10^{-34})(2.998\times10^{8})=1.986\times10^{-25}$ J m, and dividing by $1.602\times10^{-19}$ J/eV gives $hc=1.240\times10^{-6}$ eV m $=1240$ eV nm. So
$$E[\text{eV}]=\frac{1240}{\lambda[\text{nm}]}.$$

**Worked example (500 nm light).**
$f=c/\lambda=2.998\times10^{8}/5.00\times10^{-7}=5.996\times10^{14}$ Hz.
$E=hf=(6.626\times10^{-34})(5.996\times10^{14})=3.973\times10^{-19}$ J, and $3.973\times10^{-19}/1.602\times10^{-19}=2.480$ eV. Check: $1240/500=2.48$ eV.
$p=h/\lambda=6.626\times10^{-34}/5.00\times10^{-7}=1.325\times10^{-27}$ kg m/s; check $pc=(1.325\times10^{-27})(2.998\times10^{8})=3.973\times10^{-19}$ J. Units check: J s $/$ m $=$ kg m$^2$ s$^{-1}$ $/$ m $=$ kg m/s.

**Worked example (photoelectric threshold, $\phi=2.30$ eV).**
$\phi=2.30\times1.602\times10^{-19}=3.685\times10^{-19}$ J. Then $f_0=\phi/h=3.685\times10^{-19}/6.626\times10^{-34}=5.561\times10^{14}$ Hz, and $\lambda_0=c/f_0=2.998\times10^{8}/5.561\times10^{14}=5.391\times10^{-7}$ m $=539$ nm (check: $1240/2.30=539$ nm). Light at 500 nm (2.48 eV) is *above* threshold: $K_{\max}=2.48-2.30=0.18$ eV. Light at 600 nm ($1240/600=2.07$ eV) is below threshold, so nothing is ejected however intense.

**Quantum bridge.** $E=\hbar\omega$ is the first place the numbers $\hbar$ and $h$ appear. The photoelectric effect and $E=hf$ are why "energy comes in units" is more than a slogan, and they set up the lecture's word *quantum* (W3 §3.1).

### Move P2.5 — de Broglie, wave-particle duality, amplitudes add (10 min)

**Say it aloud.** If light waves carry momentum $h/\lambda$, de Broglie proposed the reverse for matter: a particle of momentum $p$ has an associated wave of wavelength
$$\lambda_{\mathrm{dB}}=\frac hp.$$
The lecture reaches the same relation from the Schrödinger equation (Move P2.6). Note $p=\sqrt{2mK}$ for kinetic energy $K$ (non-relativistic), so $\lambda=h/\sqrt{2mK}$.

**Worked example (electron at 150 eV).**
$K=150\times1.602\times10^{-19}=2.403\times10^{-17}$ J.
$p=\sqrt{2m_eK}=\sqrt{2(9.109\times10^{-31})(2.403\times10^{-17})}=\sqrt{4.378\times10^{-47}}=6.616\times10^{-24}$ kg m/s.
$\lambda=h/p=6.626\times10^{-34}/6.616\times10^{-24}=1.001\times10^{-10}$ m $=0.100$ nm.
Units check: $\sqrt{\text{kg}\cdot\text{J}}=\sqrt{\text{kg}^2\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2}}=$ kg m/s, so $h/p$ is J s $\div$ (kg m/s) $=$ m. The speed is $p/m_e=7.26\times10^{6}$ m/s, about 2.4% of $c$, so the non-relativistic formula is fine.
A shortcut: $h/\sqrt{2m_e\,(1\text{ eV})}=1.226$ nm, so $\lambda_e\approx1.226\ \text{nm}/\sqrt{K[\text{eV}]}$. For 150 eV: $1.226/12.25=0.100$ nm. That is about the size of an atom, which is why electrons diffract from crystals.

**Worked example (baseball, for contrast).** Take $m=0.145$ kg and $v=40$ m/s (illustrative values, not constants). $p=(0.145)(40)=5.8$ kg m/s and $\lambda=6.626\times10^{-34}/5.8=1.1\times10^{-34}$ m. Compare a nucleus, about $10^{-15}$ m: the wavelength is nineteen orders of magnitude smaller. No slit could show it, so a baseball never shows interference.

**The double-slit experiment with electrons, in words.** Fire electrons at a screen with two narrow slits, one at a time, so that there is never more than one electron in the apparatus. Each electron lands as a single localised dot, at a place that cannot be predicted. Over thousands of electrons the dots pile up into bands: many electrons in some places, none in others, with the spacing predicted by $\Delta y=\lambda L/d$ using $\lambda=h/p$. Block one slit and the bands vanish, and electrons now do land in the former dark places. Try to find out which slit each electron took, and again the bands vanish (this is the lecture's own description, W6 §6.4). So "single electrons build up an interference pattern one dot at a time".

**What this means.** Each electron behaves as one thing which is *detected* as one dot, but which travels with a complex **probability amplitude** $\psi$ (the "physical" amplitude, a number for each place on the screen). Contributions from the two slits add as amplitudes; the probability density for a dot is $|\psi_1+\psi_2|^2$; and the cross term makes the bands. The dark places are where the two amplitudes cancel.

**Wave-particle duality is not "sometimes a wave, sometimes a particle".** The electron is one kind of object. Its *propagation* (where it might arrive) is governed by an amplitude with wave-like adding rules, and its *detection* is a single event. The lecture calls this the origin of "wave-particle duality" (W4 §4.2), and leaves the deeper interpretation to Week 3 §3.4.

**Quantum bridge.** $\psi$ is a probability amplitude, $|\psi|^2$ a probability density (W6 §6.4), and amplitudes for indistinguishable routes add. That is the physics under the Week 2 rules on states, and under the Week 6 spin "interference".

### Move P2.6 — Bridge: $e^{ikx}$ has $p=\hbar k$; discrete lines (10 min)

**The derivation (Eqs. 4.9-4.13 of W4 §4.2).** The free-particle time-independent equation ($V=0$) is
$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\mathrm d^2\psi}{\mathrm dx^2}=E\psi.\tag{4.9}$$
Substitute $\psi=e^{ikx}$ (4.10). Then $\psi''=(ik)^2e^{ikx}=-k^2\psi$, so the left side is $+\dfrac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\psi$, giving
$$E=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}.\tag{4.11}$$
Classical free motion has $E=p^2/2m$. Compare: $p^2=\hbar^2k^2$, so
$$p=\hbar k.\tag{4.12}$$
The wave $e^{ikx}$ has wavelength $\lambda=2\pi/|k|$ (4.13), so $|p|=\hbar\cdot2\pi/\lambda=h/\lambda$. That *is* the de Broglie relation of Move P2.5, now derived rather than proposed. The sign of $k$ gives the direction of motion; $|p|$ needs $|k|$, which is why the lecture writes $|p|=2\pi\hbar/\lambda_{\mathrm{dB}}$.

**Consistency with the operator.** In Day 4 Move 4.8, $\hat p=-i\hbar\,\mathrm d/\mathrm dx$ acting on $e^{ikx}$ gives $\hbar k\,e^{ikx}$, the same $p=\hbar k$. Three routes (energy comparison, wavelength, operator) all agree.

**Discrete spectral lines (W3 §3.1).** Heat hydrogen gas and it emits light at only certain sharp wavelengths, not a rainbow. Combine this with $E=hf$: if an atom drops from one energy level to another and the difference is emitted as one photon, then $hf=E_{\text{upper}}-E_{\text{lower}}$, and sharp lines mean sharp, discrete energy *differences*. That is the physical evidence for a *discrete spectrum of the Hamiltonian* that the W3 lecture cites when it calls discrete eigenvalues "shocking". (The lecture itself does not spell out the photon-emission step; it is standard physics.) Example with a made-up gap: a 2.00 eV gap emits $1240/2.00=620$ nm. The red hydrogen line near 656 nm corresponds to a gap of about $1240/656=1.89$ eV (standard fact, not from the lecture).

**Free versus bound.** Free-particle energies $\hbar^2k^2/2m$ take any value, because $k$ is any real number. Discreteness appears only when the particle is confined (bound); P3 shows why.

**Quantum bridge.** The two lines "$p=\hbar k$" and "discrete lines mean discrete eigenvalues" are the two pieces of physics W4 §4.2 and W3 §3.1 assume; you now have both.

So the probability density is the same at every place and time: the particle is equally likely to be anywhere, and the plane wave is not normalisable.
9. A free electron is described by $\psi=e^{ikx}$ with $k=5.0\times10^{9}$ m$^{-1}$. Use $p=\hbar k$ to find $p$, the speed, the wavelength $2\pi/k$ and the energy in eV. Cross-check $\lambda$ with $h/p$. — **Hint:** $p=(1.055\times10^{-34})(5.0\times10^{9})$; $v=p/m_e$; $E=p^2/2m_e$. — **Solution sketch:** $p=5.275\times10^{-25}$ kg m/s; $v=5.275\times10^{-25}/9.109\times10^{-31}=5.79\times10^{5}$ m/s (well below $c$); $\lambda=2\pi/k=1.257\times10^{-9}$ m; cross-check $h/p=6.626\times10^{-34}/5.275\times10^{-25}=1.256\times10^{-9}$ m, agreeing to 0.04% (the rounding gap between $\hbar$ and $h/2\pi$). $E=p^2/2m_e=(5.275\times10^{-25})^2/(2\times9.109\times10^{-31})=1.527\times10^{-19}$ J $=0.953$ eV. Units: J s $\cdot$ m$^{-1}$ = kg m/s; (kg m/s)$^2$/kg = J.
10. Short answer: electrons are fired one at a time, so they cannot interfere with each other. Why does the screen still show an interference pattern? — **Hint:** think about what is added, and what is detected. — **Solution sketch:** Each electron has its own amplitude with a contribution from each slit; those contributions add as amplitudes, and the probability of a dot at a given place is $|\psi_1+\psi_2|^2$, which has bands. The electron is detected once, as one dot, but the *chances* of where it lands follow that pattern, so the pattern emerges as dots accumulate. Nothing needs two electrons to talk to each other. If you learn which slit was used, the paths become distinguishable and the pattern disappears (W6 §6.4).

## Anti-patterns / Common mistakes

- **Adding probabilities instead of amplitudes** when the routes are indistinguishable. Add the amplitudes, then take the modulus squared.
- **Mixing $f$ and $\omega$** (or $\lambda$ and $k$): $\omega=2\pi f$, $k=2\pi/\lambda$. Pair $h$ with $f,\lambda$ and $\hbar$ with $\omega,k$. A stray factor of $2\pi$ is the usual symptom.
- **Treating "wave-particle duality" as "sometimes a wave, sometimes a particle".** One object; wave-like amplitudes for where it may go, one localised event when it is detected.
- **Forgetting the eV-to-joule conversion** before using $h$ or $m_e$ in SI formulas.
