## Moves

### Move P5.1 — Photon polarisation as a two-state system; Malus's law is the Born rule (10 min)

**Picture first.** Light is a wave whose electric field wobbles sideways to its direction of travel. If the wobble always lies along one line, the light is **linearly polarised** along that line. A **polariser** is a sheet with a preferred line, its *axis*. It lets through the part of the wobble along its axis and absorbs the part at right angles.

**Classical rule (Malus's law).** Polarised light of intensity $I_0$ meets a polariser whose axis makes angle $\theta$ with the light's polarisation. The transmitted intensity is

$$I = I_0\cos^2\theta .$$

*Units.* Intensity is power per area, $\mathrm{W/m^2}$; $\cos^2\theta$ is a pure number, so $I$ and $I_0$ have the same units. Check the extremes: $\theta = 0$ gives $I = I_0$ (all through), $\theta = 90^\circ$ gives $I = 0$ (all blocked).

**Worked example (three polarisers).** A beam polarised along $0^\circ$ with $I_0 = 100\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$ meets a polariser at $0^\circ$, then one at $35^\circ$, then one at $90^\circ$. (The first one changes nothing: relative angle $0^\circ$.)

- Second polariser: relative angle $35^\circ - 0^\circ = 35^\circ$. $\cos35^\circ = 0.8192$, so $\cos^2 35^\circ = 0.6710$ (check: $\tfrac12(1+\cos70^\circ) = \tfrac12(1+0.3420) = 0.6710$). Intensity $= 100\times0.6710 = 67.10\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$.
- Third polariser: relative angle $90^\circ - 35^\circ = 55^\circ$ (the light now points along $35^\circ$, *not* along $0^\circ$). $\cos^2 55^\circ = 0.3290$. Intensity $= 67.10\times0.3290 = 22.08\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$.
- Remove the middle polariser: relative angle $90^\circ$ between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, so $I = 0$.

Adding a filter in the middle *increased* the light. Units check: $\mathrm{W/m^2}\times$ (pure number) $= \mathrm{W/m^2}$ at every step. This only looks paradoxical if you forget that each polariser *re-prepares* the polarisation.

**The quantum version: one photon.** A single photon has two independent linear polarisations, horizontal and vertical. Write them as an orthonormal basis $|H\rangle$ and $|V\rangle$ (say "ket H", "ket V"). That is a **photon polarisation state** living in a two-dimensional space: a physical **qubit**, the same mathematics as spin-½ (Day 3, Day 7).

A photon polarised at angle $\alpha$ from horizontal is

$$|\alpha\rangle = \cos\alpha\,|H\rangle + \sin\alpha\,|V\rangle .$$

A polariser with axis at angle $\theta$ asks the question "is the photon in $|\theta\rangle$ or in the perpendicular state?". Passing means the photon is found in $|\theta\rangle$. The amplitude is the inner product

$$\langle\theta|\alpha\rangle = \cos\theta\cos\alpha + \sin\theta\sin\alpha = \cos(\alpha-\theta),$$

so by the Born rule (Week 3, Section 3.2) the probability of passing is $|c|^2 = \cos^2(\alpha - \theta)$.

Intensity is the number of photons per second times $hf$ (P2.4), so a fraction $\cos^2\theta$ of photons passing *is* a fraction $\cos^2\theta$ of the intensity. Malus's law and the Born rule are one statement, seen from the beam and from the single photon. After a photon passes, it *is* in $|\theta\rangle$ (collapse, Week 3, Section 3.3), which is why the third polariser in the example sees the $35^\circ$ direction and not the original one.

**Quantum bridge.** Classical: intensity fractions, a smooth $\cos^2$. Quantum: each photon passes or is absorbed, at random, with probability $\cos^2\theta$; only the *average* over many photons reproduces Malus. One caution: for spin-½ the state pointing at angle $\theta$ in space has amplitudes $\cos(\theta/2)$ and $\sin(\theta/2)$, half the angle. Do not carry polarisation angles into spin formulas without checking the convention.

### Move P5.2 — Entanglement, EPR and Bell at concept level; correlated is not communicating (10 min)

**What the course says.** Week 7, Section 7.2 defines entangled states as two-particle states that cannot be factorised, and names the singlet and the three triplet states as examples. Section 7.4 then describes a source sending one of two entangled photons to Alice and one to Bob, each with a polariser giving $+1$ or $-1$. Two facts from it are all we need here:

1. At each polariser alone, $+1$ and $-1$ occur *randomly with equal probability*.
2. If the polarisers are aligned along the same direction, the two results are *totally correlated*: both $+1$ or both $-1$; the crossed cases never happen.

**EPR in one paragraph.** *Standard background, not from the lecture (apart from the Week 3 Section 3.4 wording noted here).* Einstein, and the "realist" position quoted in Week 3, Section 3.4, said that if two distant results always agree, the simplest explanation is that each photon carried its answer with it from the source. Those pre-set answers would be the **hidden variables** the lecture mentions there: extra information beyond the wave function $|\psi\rangle$. "Local" means that nothing done to one photon can change the other instantly. The argument that quantum mechanics must then be incomplete is called the **EPR paradox**, after Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen.

**Bell in one paragraph.** *Standard background; the lecture does not present Bell's inequality.* Perfect agreement at *one* alignment can be produced by pre-set answers, so it cannot decide the question. Bell showed that the statistics at *several different* alignments obey an inequality if local hidden variables are true, and that quantum mechanics predicts a violation for entangled pairs. Experiments of this kind have found results in line with quantum mechanics, which rules out local hidden-variable explanations. The lecture's Figure 7.1 credit names Aspect's article on this debate. *Standard background, not the lecture's reasoning:* this is why several alignments matter in a Bell test. The lecture's own reason for Section 7.4's random polariser angles is different: a smart Eve who knows Bob's direction could insert a polariser along it and copy the result, so Alice and Bob choose orientations randomly and use the non-aligned runs to check that the correlations match those expected of entangled pairs (Move P5.4).

**Correlated is not communicating.** Look at fact 1: Bob's results look like fair coin tosses whatever Alice does. The agreement only appears when the two lists are *compared*, and comparing needs an ordinary channel (a phone call). So entanglement carries no message and allows no faster-than-light signalling. See Day 7, Move 7.5 and the Week 7 companion ("Section 7.4, one link only") for the maths of the same statement. Do not redo those calculations here.

**Quantum bridge.** Classical correlation: two sealed envelopes with matching cards. Quantum: matching results with no pre-set cards, at the price of the joint state carrying "information about the correlation, not about specific values" (Section 7.4).

### Move P5.3 — Interpretations in neutral language (5 min)

Week 3, Section 3.4 asks: you measure a particle at point C; where was it just before? It names **three positions**. Below, each gets two sentences. Say them in your own words in the discussion, without ranking them.

- **Realist.** The particle was at C, and the wave function is an incomplete description, so some extra information (a hidden variable) is needed for a full account. Einstein is named as advocating this.
- **Orthodox (the Copenhagen interpretation).** The particle was not definitely anywhere until the measurement, which produces the definite value. The lecture associates this with Bohr and says it has been the most widely accepted view among physicists, while noting that "the act of measurement" remains puzzling.
- **Agnostic.** Decline to answer, since a statement about the state *before* a measurement cannot be tested by any measurement. The lecture quotes Pauli on this.

The lecture also opens the section with a quotation from Tong that the classical world emerges from the quantum one and not the reverse. **Many-worlds** and **decoherence** are *not* named in this lecture section, so this file does not describe them. The optional reading `exteral_resources/(2007) Collins, G. - The Many Interpretations of Quantum Mechanics (Scientific American).pdf` is listed by name only; I have not summarised it.

**What they share.** The three positions in Section 3.4 answer a question about the status of the particle *before* measurement. None is put forward as changing the outcome probabilities that this module calculates (Week 3, Sections 3.2 and 3.3). *Standard background:* Bell-type results (Move P5.2) add that any hidden-variable account matching those probabilities cannot be local. Stay with the wording of Section 3.4 when you write about it.

**Quantum bridge.** Classical physics never had to ask "what was it before I looked", because looking changes nothing. Section 3.4 is about what to say now that looking does matter.

### Move P5.4 — Quantum technology at lecture level: key distribution and spin sensing (10 min)

**What Week 7, Section 7.4 says.**

- A secret key must be a long random bit string; the hard part is passing it from Alice (sender) to Bob (receiver) when an **eavesdropper**, Eve, may spy.
- The no-cloning theorem (stated in the lecture, cited from outside sources) says an unknown quantum state cannot be copied, so any attempt to eavesdrop causes a disturbance that Alice and Bob can detect.
- Ekert (1992) proposed a scheme with polarisation-entangled photon pairs. At aligned polarisers the two results agree, so both get identical random sequences, and $-1$ is relabelled $0$.
- The bit values are "decided only at the last moment" of measurement, so between the source and the polarisers there is nothing to spy on.
- A smart Eve who knows Bob's direction $b$ could insert a polariser along $b$ and get a copy. To beat that, Alice and Bob *choose their polariser orientations randomly* from a set such as $0,\pi/8,2\pi/8,3\pi/8$, then compare on a public channel which angle each used. The runs at relative angle $0$ give the identical **key** (the part they keep is called the **sifted key**, standard terminology, not a term used in the lecture); the other runs are used to check that correlations match the entangled-pair expectation. If they do not, a spy is on the line and they refrain from using the key.

The lecture states no numbers for error rates or for what fraction of runs is kept, and says an eavesdropper measuring along another direction makes the correlations "different from the ones expected". A specific error rate is **outside this module**.

**Plain-English recap (simplified two-choice picture; a teaching model, not the lecture's exact protocol).** Suppose each side independently picks, at random, one of two measurement settings for every photon. Afterwards they announce *which setting* (never the result) and keep only the rounds where the settings matched. Matching happens with probability $\tfrac12$, so about half the rounds survive **sifting**. If Eve has to measure in the middle, she does not know which setting to use, so she disturbs some rounds, and the checks reveal it.

*Numerical example (new numbers).* Alice and Bob run $N = 600$ rounds. Expected survivors: $600\times\tfrac12 = 300$ bits. Spread: for a coin-toss count the standard deviation is $\sqrt{N p(1-p)} = \sqrt{600\times0.25} = \sqrt{150} \approx 12$, so $300\pm12$ is typical, about $288$ to $312$ bits. Units check: bits and rounds are counts; probability has no units, so bits $=$ rounds $\times$ probability.

**Spin sensing (background, not in the lecture; one paragraph).** Week 6, Section 6.1 gives the force on a magnetic dipole as $F = \langle\mu_z\rangle\,\mathrm{d}B_z/\mathrm{d}z$: what a spin does depends on the field it sits in. A dipole in a field also has an energy that depends on $B$ (P4), so a spin-½ with moment of size $\mu$ has two energy levels split by about $2\mu B$, and the frequency $f = \Delta E/h$ at which it responds is a *reading of $B$*. That idea is **quantum sensing**: use a quantum two-level system as a very sensitive meter. Example with $\mu = \mu_B = 9.274\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T}$ (standard value) and $B = 1.0\ \mathrm{mT}$: $\Delta E = 2\times9.274\times10^{-24}\times1.0\times10^{-3} = 1.855\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{J}$, and $f = 1.855\times10^{-26}/6.626\times10^{-34} = 2.80\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{Hz}$, about $28\ \mathrm{MHz}$. Units: $(\mathrm{J/T})\times\mathrm{T} = \mathrm{J}$; $\mathrm{J}/(\mathrm{J\,s}) = \mathrm{s^{-1}} = \mathrm{Hz}$.

**Quantum bridge.** Classical security depends on the eavesdropper lacking computing power; the quantum argument depends on measurement disturbing the state. Classical field sensors measure a field by its push; a spin measures it through a level splitting.

### Move P5.5 — The physical-reasoning kit: units, order of magnitude, limiting case, symmetry (10 min)

**The four-step recipe.** Run it on any result *after* you calculate it.

1. **Units.** Write the SI units of each side. They must match, and each added term must have the same units.
2. **Order of magnitude.** Round to one significant figure with powers of ten and ask whether the size is plausible for the physical situation. (An **order of magnitude** is the nearest power of ten.)
3. **Limiting case.** Push a parameter to an extreme ($0$ or $\infty$) where you already know the answer. This is a **limiting case**.
4. **Symmetry.** Ask what should stay unchanged if you relabel or swap something. This is a **symmetry check**.

**Worked check 1: units of the box energy (Week 4, Eq. 4.20).** $E_n = \dfrac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}$. Here $n$, $\pi$ and $2$ are pure numbers. Units: $\dfrac{(\mathrm{J\,s})^2}{\mathrm{kg}\,\mathrm{m}^2}$. Use $\mathrm{J} = \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}}$ once: $\dfrac{\mathrm{J}\cdot\mathrm{J}\,\mathrm{s}^2}{\mathrm{kg\,m^2}} = \dfrac{\mathrm{J}\cdot(\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}})\,\mathrm{s}^2}{\mathrm{kg\,m^2}} = \mathrm{J}$. Correct: an energy.

**Worked check 2: limiting case $L\to\infty$.** $E_n \propto 1/L^2$, so $E_n\to0$ as the box grows. The gap $E_{n+1}-E_n = (2n+1)\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$ also tends to $0$: the levels crowd into a continuum, and the particle acts like a free one whose energy can take any value. Consistent with Week 4, Section 4.2, where $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ with any $k$ (here $k = n\pi/L$, spacing $\pi/L\to0$).

**Worked check 3: symmetry of a two-qubit state (new state).** Let $|\chi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\big(|00\rangle + 2|01\rangle + 2|10\rangle - |11\rangle\big)$ (norm: $1+4+4+1 = 10$, good). Swap the two qubits (write the first label second and vice versa): $|01\rangle\leftrightarrow|10\rangle$, $|00\rangle$ and $|11\rangle$ unchanged. The coefficients of $|01\rangle$ and $|10\rangle$ are equal, so the state is unchanged: swap-*symmetric*. Meaning: a set-up that treats Alice and Bob identically can be described by such a state, and the two one-qubit statistics agree. Check: probability the first qubit reads $0$ is $\tfrac{1+4}{10} = \tfrac12$, and the second qubit reading $0$ has $\tfrac{1+4}{10} = \tfrac12$. Equal, as symmetry demands. A state with different coefficients on $|01\rangle$ and $|10\rangle$ would fail this test.

**Worked check 4: order of magnitude of the de Broglie wavelength of a room-temperature electron.** Take a thermal kinetic energy $E = \tfrac32 k_B T$ with $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}$ (standard value) and $T = 300\ \mathrm{K}$. Then $E = 1.5\times1.381\times10^{-23}\times300 = 6.215\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{J}$ (about $0.039\ \mathrm{eV}$, using $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$, standard value). With $m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$ (standard value), $p = \sqrt{2m_eE} = \sqrt{2\times9.109\times10^{-31}\times6.215\times10^{-21}} = \sqrt{1.132\times10^{-50}} = 1.064\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$. Then
$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{1.064\times10^{-25}} = 6.23\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m}\approx 6\ \mathrm{nm}.$$
(Using $\lambda = 2\pi\hbar/p$ with the lecture's $\hbar$ gives $6.23\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m}$ too, Week 4, Section 4.2.) Units: $p$: $\sqrt{\mathrm{kg}\cdot\mathrm{J}} = \sqrt{\mathrm{kg}^2\,\mathrm{m}^2\mathrm{s}^{-2}} = \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$; $\lambda$: $\mathrm{J\,s}/(\mathrm{kg\,m/s}) = \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}/(\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}) = \mathrm{m}$. Order of magnitude: a few nanometres, many times larger than an atom (roughly $10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m}$), so wave effects of thermal electrons are not negligible on atomic scales.

**Quantum bridge.** Classical physics has these checks too; what changes is *which* limit to test. For a quantum result the useful limits are "large box", "large mass", "many particles", where you know the classical answer; that is where Week 3–5 formulas should reduce to it.

## Exercises

1. Three polarisers with axes at $0^\circ$, $30^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ (in that order) stand in a beam already polarised along $0^\circ$ with $I_0 = 80\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$. Find the intensity leaving the last polariser. Then remove the $30^\circ$ polariser and find the intensity again. — **Hint:** apply $I = I_0\cos^2\theta$ one polariser at a time, always with the angle *between the current polarisation and the next axis*; $\cos 30^\circ = \sqrt3/2$, $\cos60^\circ = \tfrac12$. — **Solution sketch:** first polariser: relative $0^\circ$, $80\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$. Second: relative $30^\circ$, $\cos^2 = \tfrac34$, $60\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$. Third: relative $60^\circ$ (from $30^\circ$ to $90^\circ$), $\cos^2=\tfrac14$, $15\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$. With the middle one removed: relative $90^\circ$, $I = 0$. Units check: $\mathrm{W/m^2}\times$ pure number $= \mathrm{W/m^2}$.

2. A polariser lets $10\%$ of the intensity of polarised light through: $I = 4.0\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$ from $I_0 = 40\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$. Find the angle $\theta$ (between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$) between polarisation and axis. — **Hint:** $\cos^2\theta = I/I_0$; take the square root before the inverse cosine. — **Solution sketch:** $\cos^2\theta = 0.10$, $\cos\theta = 1/\sqrt{10} = 0.3162$; equivalently $\tan\theta = 3$, so $\theta = 71.57^\circ\approx71.6^\circ$. Check: $\cos71.57^\circ\approx0.3162$, squared $0.100$. Units check: $I/I_0$ is dimensionless.

3. A photon is prepared in $|\psi\rangle = \cos\alpha\,|H\rangle + \sin\alpha\,|V\rangle$ with $\alpha = 20^\circ$. Give the probabilities that a horizontal/vertical measurement returns $H$ and $V$, check they sum to $1$, and give the probability that it passes a polariser at $45^\circ$. — **Hint:** probabilities are the squared magnitudes of the coefficients; for the $45^\circ$ polariser use the Move P5.1 amplitude $\cos(\alpha-\theta)$. — **Solution sketch:** $P(H) = \cos^220^\circ = \tfrac12(1+\cos40^\circ) = \tfrac12(1+0.7660) = 0.883$; $P(V) = \sin^220^\circ = 0.117$; sum $=1.000$. For $\theta=45^\circ$: $\cos^2(25^\circ) = \tfrac12(1+\cos50^\circ) = \tfrac12(1+0.6428) = 0.821$. No units: probabilities.

4. Alice and Bob run $400$ rounds of the simplified two-setting picture from Move P5.4 (each picks one of two settings at random, independently). (a) How many bits are expected to survive sifting, and roughly how much does the count typically vary? (b) According to Week 7, Section 7.4, what does an eavesdropper who measures along a direction other than Bob's do, and what error rate does the lecture give? — **Hint:** (a) matching has probability $\tfrac12$; the spread for $N$ tosses of a fair coin is $\sqrt{Np(1-p)}$. (b) Re-read the last two paragraphs of Section 7.4 and quote only what is there. — **Solution sketch:** (a) $400\times\tfrac12 = 200$ expected; standard deviation $\sqrt{400\times0.25} = 10$, so usually $180$ to $220$ (within two standard deviations, standard statistics). (b) The lecture says the measured correlations then differ from those expected for entangled pairs, so Alice and Bob detect the spy and refrain from using the key; it states no error rate. A number is outside this module. Units: counts and probabilities only.

5. A student proposes the box energy $E_n = \dfrac{n^2\pi^2\hbar}{2mL^2}$ (one $\hbar$, not $\hbar^2$). Use the units check from Move P5.5 to show it cannot be an energy, and say what it is instead. — **Hint:** reduce $\dfrac{\hbar}{mL^2}$ to base units with $\mathrm{J\,s} = \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}$. — **Solution sketch:** $\dfrac{\mathrm{J\,s}}{\mathrm{kg\,m^2}} = \dfrac{\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}}{\mathrm{kg\,m^2}} = \mathrm{s^{-1}}$: a frequency, not joules. So it is $E_n/\hbar$; multiply by $\hbar$ to get the correct Eq. 4.20. Units are the only tool needed to catch the missing $\hbar$.

6. Limiting cases. (a) State Malus's law at $\theta = 0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ and say what each means physically. (b) For an electron ($m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$, standard value) in a box of width $L = 1.0\ \mathrm{nm}$, compute $E_1$ in joules and in eV (use $\hbar = 1.055\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$ and $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$, standard value), then find $E_1$ for $L = 10\ \mathrm{nm}$ without recomputing. — **Hint:** (a) evaluate $\cos^2$ at the two angles. (b) $E_1 = \pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$; $E_1\propto L^{-2}$. — **Solution sketch:** (a) $\theta=0^\circ$: $I=I_0$, light aligned with the axis passes entirely; $\theta=90^\circ$: $I=0$, light at right angles is blocked. (b) $E_1 = \dfrac{9.870\times1.113\times10^{-68}}{2\times9.109\times10^{-31}\times10^{-18}} = \dfrac{1.0985\times10^{-67}}{1.8218\times10^{-48}} = 6.03\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{J}$, i.e. $6.03\times10^{-20}/1.602\times10^{-19} = 0.376\ \mathrm{eV}$. For $L=10\ \mathrm{nm}$, a factor $10^{2}$ smaller: $3.76\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}$ ($3.76\ \mathrm{meV}$). The trend $E_1\to0$ as $L\to\infty$ is the limiting case. Units check: $(\mathrm{J\,s})^2/(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}) = \mathrm{J}$ (Move P5.5).

7. Symmetry check. For each state, swap the two qubit labels and say whether the state is unchanged (symmetric) or not, and confirm by comparing the probability that the first qubit reads $0$ with the probability that the second reads $0$. (a) $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{23}}\big(|00\rangle + 3|01\rangle + 3|10\rangle + 2|11\rangle\big)$. (b) $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}\big(|01\rangle + 2|10\rangle\big)$. — **Hint:** compare the coefficients of $|01\rangle$ and $|10\rangle$; the probability that qubit 1 reads $0$ adds $|\text{coefficient}|^2$ for kets with first label $0$. — **Solution sketch:** (a) norm $1+9+9+4 = 23$, good. Coefficients of $|01\rangle$ and $|10\rangle$ are both $3$: symmetric. First qubit $0$: $\tfrac{1+9}{23} = \tfrac{10}{23}$; second qubit $0$ (kets $|00\rangle,|10\rangle$): $\tfrac{1+9}{23} = \tfrac{10}{23}$. Equal. (b) norm $1+4 = 5$, good. Swapping gives $\tfrac1{\sqrt5}(|10\rangle + 2|01\rangle)$, which differs (coefficients $1,2$ exchange), so it is not symmetric. First qubit $0$: $\tfrac15$; second qubit $0$: $\tfrac45$; unequal, consistent with the failed swap test.

8. Order of magnitude. Estimate the de Broglie wavelength of a proton ($m_p = 1.673\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$, standard value) at the same thermal energy $E = \tfrac32k_BT$ at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ ($k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}$, standard value; $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$, standard value). Compare with the electron of Move P5.5 using a ratio, as a check. — **Hint:** $E = 6.215\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{J}$ as in Move P5.5; $p = \sqrt{2mE}$; $\lambda = h/p$; for equal $E$, $\lambda\propto m^{-1/2}$. — **Solution sketch:** $p = \sqrt{2\times1.673\times10^{-27}\times6.215\times10^{-21}} = \sqrt{2.079\times10^{-47}} = 4.560\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$; $\lambda = 6.626\times10^{-34}/4.560\times10^{-24} = 1.45\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m}$, about $0.15\ \mathrm{nm}$. Ratio check: $\sqrt{m_e/m_p} = \sqrt{5.445\times10^{-4}} = 0.0233$, and $6.23\ \mathrm{nm}\times0.0233 = 0.145\ \mathrm{nm}$. Agrees. Units: as in Move P5.5, $\mathrm{J\,s}/(\mathrm{kg\,m/s}) = \mathrm{m}$.

9. Short answer: correlated versus communicates. Alice and Bob each hold one photon of many entangled pairs, and their polarisers are aligned, so their result lists match exactly (Week 7, Section 7.4).
