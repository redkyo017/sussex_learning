## Why this matters

Week 5 opens with a mass on a spring: the potential $V=\tfrac12kx^2=\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$ and the frequency $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$ (lecture §5.1, Eqs. 5.1 and 5.2, in `week_5/week_5_lecture.md`) are stated in three lines, as if you remembered them. Today you get the physics behind them: what a classical spring does, why a wave squeezed into a region can only have certain wavelengths, and why "squeezed" always means "not at rest". Each move ends with a **Quantum bridge** line. This file is the Day 4 physics pair, so it comes before Day 5. Day 5 (Move 5.5) will re-derive the box levels properly from the Schrödinger equation, and Day 6 does the maths of the oscillator; this file is the pictures and the physical reasoning, not a second derivation. Nothing here assumes you have done Day 5 or Day 6.

**Constants.** Only $\hbar=1.055\times10^{-34}$ J s comes from the lecture (Week 1 §1.1). Every other constant is a **(standard value)**: $h=6.626\times10^{-34}$ J s, $c=2.998\times10^{8}$ m/s, $m_e=9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg, $m_p=1.673\times10^{-27}$ kg, $1\ \mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}$ J. ## Moves

### Move P3.1 — The classical spring (10 min)

**Say it aloud.** A spring pulls back harder the further you stretch it. For a stretch $x$ from the relaxed position, Hooke's law says the force is

$$F=-kx,$$

where $k$ is the **spring constant** (units N/m, "newtons per metre": a stiff spring has a large $k$). The minus sign means "pulls back towards $x=0$". This force comes from the potential $V=\tfrac12kx^2$ through $F=-\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}x$ (P1.2), which is exactly the lecture's Eq. 5.1.

Newton's second law, $m\,\mathrm{d}^2x/\mathrm{d}t^2=-kx$, has the solution

$$x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\varphi),\qquad \omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$

This motion is **simple harmonic motion**. $A$ is the **amplitude** (the largest stretch), $\varphi$ is a starting phase, $\omega$ is the angular frequency in rad/s (the lecture's $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$, §5.1), and the **period** (time for one full cycle) is $T=2\pi/\omega$.

*Check that it solves the law.* Differentiating twice gives $\mathrm{d}^2x/\mathrm{d}t^2=-\omega^2A\cos(\omega t+\varphi)=-\omega^2x$. Setting $m(-\omega^2x)=-kx$ gives $\omega^2=k/m$. Good.

**Energy sloshes.** The velocity is $v=-A\omega\sin(\omega t+\varphi)$. The total energy is

$$E=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12kx^2=\tfrac12mA^2\omega^2\sin^2(\cdot)+\tfrac12kA^2\cos^2(\cdot)=\tfrac12kA^2,$$

using $m\omega^2=k$ and $\sin^2+\cos^2=1$. Energy is constant; it only moves back and forth between kinetic (biggest at $x=0$) and potential (biggest at the **turning points** $x=\pm A$, where the mass stops for an instant and reverses). So the turning points are where $E=\tfrac12kx^2$, i.e. $x=\pm\sqrt{2E/k}$.

**Worked example (a new object).** A block of mass $m=0.50$ kg on a spring of constant $k=200$ N/m, released at rest from a stretch $A=0.10$ m.

- $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}=\sqrt{200/0.50}=\sqrt{400}=20$ rad/s.
- $T=2\pi/\omega=6.283/20=0.314$ s.
- $E=\tfrac12kA^2=\tfrac12(200)(0.10)^2=\tfrac12(200)(0.010)=1.00$ J.
- Maximum speed (at $x=0$, where all the energy is kinetic): $v_{\max}=A\omega=0.10\times20=2.0$ m/s. Check: $\tfrac12mv_{\max}^2=\tfrac12(0.50)(4.0)=1.00$ J. Consistent.

*Units check.* $k/m$ has units $(\mathrm{N/m})/\mathrm{kg}=(\mathrm{kg\,s^{-2}})/\mathrm{kg}=\mathrm{s^{-2}}$, so $\omega$ is in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ (rad/s, the radian is dimensionless). $\tfrac12kA^2$ has units $\mathrm{N/m}\cdot\mathrm m^2=\mathrm{N\,m}=\mathrm J$. Good.

**Quantum bridge.** The lecture's Eq. 5.3, $H=p^2/2m+\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$, is this same energy written with $p=mv$ (P1.3). The whole quantum problem in Week 5 is: what happens when you promote this $H$ to an operator (Eq. 5.4)? The classical $\omega$ survives untouched and becomes the frequency that sets the level spacing $\hbar\omega$ (Move P3.5).

### Move P3.2 — Where does the classical mass spend its time? (8 min)

**Picture first.** Film the block and ask: at a random moment, where is it? It moves fastest through the middle and slows to a stop at each turning point. Slow places collect more time. So the block is *most likely to be found near the turning points* and *least likely near the centre*.

**The result (stated, not derived).** The time spent in a small strip $\mathrm{d}x$ is $\mathrm{d}x/|v|$, so the classical probability density is proportional to $1/|v(x)|$. With $v=\pm\omega\sqrt{A^2-x^2}$ from energy conservation, the normalised classical probability density is

$$P_{\text{cl}}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{A^2-x^2}},\qquad -A<x<A.$$

It is smallest at $x=0$ (equal to $1/(\pi A)$), rises without limit as $x\to\pm A$ (the mass is nearly stationary there; the total is still finite), and is zero outside $[-A,A]$, because the block never goes there. No derivation of this "arcsine law" is needed; keep the shape: a shallow dip in the middle, tall spikes at the edges.

**Quantum bridge.** A quantum state has its own $|\psi(x)|^2$ and it can be non-zero outside the classical turning points. Comparing the classical $1/|v|$ shape with the quantum shape is the standard way to see how quantum mechanics differs. (Which one is a bump in the middle, and when the two agree, is a question you will meet again in Exercise 11.)

### Move P3.3 — Standing waves and confinement (12 min)

**Picture first.** Pluck a guitar string of length $L$ that is pinned at both ends. The pinned ends cannot move, so the wave must have a **node** (a point that never moves) at each end. Only certain wave shapes fit: half a wavelength, one wavelength, one and a half, and so on. These are **standing waves**. In words, a whole number $n$ of half-wavelengths fits into the length:

$$n\,\frac{\lambda_n}{2}=L\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lambda_n=\frac{2L}{n},\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots$$

The string cannot vibrate with $\lambda=1.3L$: the ends would not be pinned. Confinement forces a discrete list. That is the whole physical idea of **confinement** giving **quantisation** (in the physical, "only certain values allowed" sense).

**Now apply de Broglie.** From P2.5, a particle with wavelength $\lambda$ has momentum $p=h/\lambda$, and a free particle has energy $E=p^2/2m$. A particle confined to $0\le x\le L$ (the infinite well of lecture §4.3 and Day 5 Move 5.5) has to fit a standing wave with nodes at the walls, so $\lambda_n=2L/n$ and

$$p_n=\frac{h}{\lambda_n}=\frac{nh}{2L},\qquad E_n=\frac{p_n^2}{2m}=\frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}.$$

The last step uses $h=2\pi\hbar$, so $h^2=4\pi^2\hbar^2$ and $h^2/8=\pi^2\hbar^2/2$. This is the lecture's Eq. 4.20, $E_n=\hbar^2\pi^2n^2/(2mL^2)$. It also matches Eq. 4.19: $k_n=2\pi/\lambda_n=n\pi/L$. **Relationship to Day 5.** Day 5 Move 5.5 derives the same result properly from the Schrödinger equation with $\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0$. The standing-wave argument here is a picture that gets the answer with no calculus; it is not a replacement for the derivation. **A convention to note:** the lecture (Eq. 4.19) starts the box at $n=1$ (its text says $n=0$ would mean "no particle"); the oscillator of Week 5 starts at $n=0$. Keep the two labelling schemes apart (Anti-patterns).

The key scaling: $E_n\propto n^2$ (levels spread out as you go up) and $E_n\propto1/L^2$ (squeeze the box, energies shoot up) and $E_n\propto1/m$ (heavy particles have closely spaced levels).

**Worked example.** An electron ($m_e=9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg, standard value) in a box of width $L=0.80$ nm $=0.80\times10^{-9}$ m. Use $E_1=h^2/(8m_eL^2)$ with $h=6.626\times10^{-34}$ J s (standard value).

- $h^2=(6.626\times10^{-34})^2=4.390\times10^{-67}\ \mathrm{J^2s^2}$.
- $8m_eL^2=8(9.109\times10^{-31})(6.4\times10^{-19})=4.664\times10^{-48}$ kg m$^2$.
- $E_1=4.390\times10^{-67}/4.664\times10^{-48}=9.414\times10^{-20}$ J.
- In eV: $9.414\times10^{-20}/1.602\times10^{-19}=0.588$ eV (standard value for the conversion).
- The next levels scale as $n^2$: $E_2=4E_1=2.35$ eV and $E_3=9E_1=5.29$ eV.

*Units check.* $h^2/(mL^2)=\mathrm{(J\,s)^2/(kg\,m^2)}$. Since $\mathrm J=\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}}$, this is $\mathrm{J\cdot(kg\,m^2\,s^{-2})\,s^2/(kg\,m^2)}=\mathrm J$. Good. Cross-check with the lecture's form: $\pi^2\hbar^2/(2m_eL^2)$ with $\hbar=1.055\times10^{-34}$ J s gives $0.588$ eV as well ($\pi^2\hbar^2=1.0985\times10^{-67}$; divided by $2m_eL^2=1.166\times10^{-48}$ gives $9.42\times10^{-20}$ J).

**Quantum bridge.** The energies of a bound quantum particle are quantised for the same reason a guitar string has a harmonic series: boundary conditions select allowed waves. The Schrödinger equation is the machinery; the standing-wave picture is why you should expect the answer.

### Move P3.4 — Zero-point energy and the uncertainty relation (10 min)

**Why $n=0$ is not allowed in the box.** The lowest standing wave has $n=1$, so $E_1=\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)>0$. A particle in a box cannot have zero energy. Classically it could sit at rest in the middle, but a wave with zero wave number is a flat line and cannot meet the boundary condition $\psi=0$ at both walls. The smallest energy allowed by confinement is called the **zero-point energy**.

**The wave form of the uncertainty principle.** A wave that lives in a small region cannot have a single sharp wavelength: to make a bump of width $\Delta x$ you must add waves of different wavenumbers. The spread of wavenumbers $\Delta k$ obeys the standard result

$$\Delta x\,\Delta k\gtrsim\tfrac12.$$

State it as a standard result here (no proof needed). Multiply by $\hbar$ and use $p=\hbar k$ (lecture §4.2): $\Delta x\,\Delta p\gtrsim\hbar/2$. This is **Heisenberg's relation**, also called the **uncertainty principle**, the same inequality $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$ that is quoted in the Week 5 task statement. It says squeezing $\Delta x$ forces a large $\Delta p$: a narrow bump is a mixture of many wavelengths.

**A rough energy estimate.** Confine a particle to a region of size $L$. Then $\Delta x\sim L$ and $\Delta p\sim\hbar/L$ (the lecture's version, §4.3: it takes $\Delta x=\pm L/2$ and $\Delta p=\hbar/L$, so that $\Delta x\Delta p=\hbar/2$). The kinetic energy is at least of order

$$E\sim\frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m(\Delta x)^2}\to\frac{\hbar^2}{2mL^2}.$$

This is the lecture's estimate. The exact box result $\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$ is bigger by the factor $\pi^2\approx9.87$: an estimate gives the *scale* and the *dependence on $m$ and $L$*, not the coefficient.

**Worked example (order of magnitude).** An electron squeezed into $L=0.80$ nm: $\hbar^2=(1.055\times10^{-34})^2=1.113\times10^{-68}$; $2m_eL^2=2(9.109\times10^{-31})(6.4\times10^{-19})=1.166\times10^{-48}$; the ratio is $9.55\times10^{-21}$ J $=0.060$ eV. The exact ground energy in the worked example above, $0.588$ eV, is about $9.9$ times bigger, as the $\pi^2$ says. Same order of magnitude in the units of the problem, which is all an estimate promises.

*Units check.* $\hbar^2/(mL^2)$ is energy by the same arithmetic as before.

**Quantum bridge.** In quantum mechanics "lowest energy" means the minimum of kinetic plus potential energy, and squeezing the particle to reduce the potential energy raises the kinetic energy. Balance gives a finite floor: the zero-point energy (lecture §4.3 last paragraphs, and §5.4 for the oscillator).

### Move P3.5 — Bridge: the oscillator ladder $E_n=\hbar\omega(n+\tfrac12)$ (5 min)

The lecture's result for the quantum oscillator is (§5.4, Eq. 5.20, and repeated unnumbered just below the proof that $n=0,1,2,\dots$):

$$E_n=\hbar\omega\left(n+\tfrac12\right),\qquad n=0,1,2,3,\dots$$

Three physical features, each with a classical contrast:

1. **Equally spaced.** Neighbouring levels differ by exactly $\hbar\omega$ (Figure 5.2). Compare the box, where the spacing $(2n+1)E_1$ grows with $n$. The $\omega$ is the classical $\sqrt{k/m}$ of Move P3.1.
2. **Not at rest.** The lowest level is $E_0=\tfrac12\hbar\omega\neq0$. Classically zero energy means "sitting at $x=0$ at rest"; the lecture says the quantum particle "can never be fully at rest". Same message as Move P3.4: confining a particle in a parabola raises its kinetic energy.
3. **Discrete versus continuous.** A classical block can have any energy $\tfrac12kA^2$ (choose any $A$); the quantum oscillator can only have the values on the ladder.

**Worked example (why you never see the quantisation of a block).** For the 0.50 kg block on the 200 N/m spring, $\omega=20$ rad/s. The level spacing is $\hbar\omega=(1.055\times10^{-34})(20)=2.11\times10^{-33}$ J. Its energy is $E=1.00$ J, so it sits at about $E/\hbar\omega=4.7\times10^{32}$ rungs up. A spacing of $2\times10^{-33}$ J out of $1$ J is beyond any measurement: the ladder looks like a smooth continuum, and the zero-point energy $\tfrac12\hbar\omega\approx1\times10^{-33}$ J is unobservable. This is the classical limit (limiting-case reasoning; P5.5).

**Scope note.** Week 5's tasks ask you to compute averages such as $\langle x^2\rangle$, the spreads $\sigma_x,\sigma_p$ and the uncertainty product for the ground state. That is deliberately **not** done here; use the Day 6 moves and your own working for that. What you should take from here is only the *physical* picture: equally spaced, not at rest, and classically continuous.

**Quantum bridge (summary).** Classical: a continuum of energies and a $1/|v|$ time-spent shape. Quantum: a ladder of energies with spacing $\hbar\omega$ and a floor at $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$.

## Exercises

Attempt each one cold for 5 to 10 minutes before opening the hint. Use the constants in the header (standard values); show the units check on every numerical one.

1. Find $\omega$ and $T$ for (a) $m=2.0$ kg on a spring with $k=50$ N/m and (b) $m=0.10$ kg on $k=3.6$ N/m. What happens to $\omega$ if you quadruple $k$? — **Hint:** $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$, then $T=2\pi/\omega$. Take the square root of the ratio first. — **Solution sketch:** (a) $\omega=\sqrt{50/2.0}=\sqrt{25}=5.0$ rad/s; $T=2\pi/5=1.26$ s. (b) $\omega=\sqrt{3.6/0.10}=\sqrt{36}=6.0$ rad/s; $T=2\pi/6=1.05$ s. Quadrupling $k$ multiplies $\omega$ by $\sqrt4=2$ (and halves $T$). Units: $\mathrm{(N/m)/kg}=\mathrm{s^{-2}}$, so $\omega$ is in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $T$ in seconds.

2. The $m=2.0$ kg, $k=50$ N/m system is released at rest from $A=0.30$ m. Find the total energy and the maximum speed. — **Hint:** $E=\tfrac12kA^2$; the maximum speed is at $x=0$ where all the energy is kinetic. Check the answer with $\tfrac12mv^2$. — **Solution sketch:** $E=\tfrac12(50)(0.30)^2=\tfrac12(50)(0.090)=2.25$ J. $v_{\max}=A\omega=0.30\times5.0=1.5$ m/s. Check: $\tfrac12(2.0)(1.5)^2=2.25$ J. Units: $\mathrm{(N/m)\,m^2=N\,m=J}$; $\mathrm{m\cdot s^{-1}=m/s}$.

3. The same system (m = 2.0 kg, k = 50 N/m) is given total energy $E=0.64$ J. Find the turning points, the speed at the centre, and the speed at $x=0.10$ m. — **Hint:** turning points where all energy is potential: $E=\tfrac12kx^2$. At any $x$: $\tfrac12mv^2=E-\tfrac12kx^2$. — **Solution sketch:** $x=\pm\sqrt{2E/k}=\pm\sqrt{1.28/50}=\pm\sqrt{0.0256}=\pm0.16$ m. Centre: $\tfrac12mv^2=0.64$ so $v=\sqrt{2(0.64)/2.0}=\sqrt{0.64}=0.80$ m/s. At $0.10$ m: $\tfrac12kx^2=\tfrac12(50)(0.0100)=0.25$ J, so $\tfrac12mv^2=0.39$ J and $v=\sqrt{0.39}=0.62$ m/s (with $m=2.0$, $v^2=0.39$). Units: $\mathrm{J=kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}}$, so $\sqrt{\mathrm{J/kg}}=\mathrm{m/s}$.

4. For the oscillator in Exercise 3, at which $x$ is the mass moving fastest, at which slowest, and where is it most likely to be found in a photograph taken at a random time? Explain in one sentence. — **Hint:** probability of being in a strip is proportional to the time spent there, which is proportional to $1/|v|$. — **Solution sketch:** Fastest at $x=0$ ($0.80$ m/s), slowest (momentarily stopped) at $x=\pm0.16$ m. The photo is most likely to catch it near the turning points, least likely near $x=0$ (though the probability there is not zero), because slow regions take more time.

5. A string of length $L=0.80$ m is fixed at both ends. Find the wavelengths $\lambda_n$ for $n=1,2,3$ and how many nodes (excluding the ends) each has. — **Hint:** $\lambda_n=2L/n$; the mode with $n$ half-wavelengths has $n-1$ interior nodes. — **Solution sketch:** $\lambda_1=1.60$ m, $\lambda_2=0.80$ m, $\lambda_3=1.6/3=0.533$ m. Interior nodes: 0, 1 and 2. Units: metres.

6. An electron in an infinite box of width $L=0.60$ nm. (a) Compute $E_1$, $E_2$, $E_3$ in eV. (b) What is the wavelength of a photon emitted in the $2\to1$ transition? — **Hint:** $E_n=n^2h^2/(8m_eL^2)$; energy conserved: $\Delta E=hc/\lambda$. Convert J to eV by dividing by $1.602\times10^{-19}$. — **Solution sketch:** $h^2=4.390\times10^{-67}$; $8m_eL^2=8(9.109\times10^{-31})(3.6\times10^{-19})=2.623\times10^{-48}$; $E_1=1.674\times10^{-19}$ J $=1.045$ eV. So $E_2=4E_1=4.18$ eV and $E_3=9E_1=9.40$ eV. (b) $\Delta E=E_2-E_1=3E_1=5.02\times10^{-19}$ J ($3.13$ eV). $\lambda=hc/\Delta E=(6.626\times10^{-34})(2.998\times10^{8})/(5.02\times10^{-19})=1.986\times10^{-25}/5.02\times10^{-19}=3.96\times10^{-7}$ m $=396$ nm (near-ultraviolet edge of the visible). Units: $\mathrm{J\,s\cdot m/s\div J=m}$.

7. By what factor does the ground energy of a box change if its width is increased by a factor $1.5$? If the particle's mass is reduced to one third at the same width? — **Hint:** $E_1\propto1/(mL^2)$: substitute directly; no constants needed. — **Solution sketch:** $L\to1.5L$: $1/(1.5L)^2=1/(2.25L^2)$, so $E_1$ multiplies by $1/2.25=0.444$. $m\to m/3$: $E_1$ multiplies by 3. (With Exercise 6: the same electron in a $0.90$ nm box would have $1.045/2.25=0.464$ eV.) Units: pure ratios, no units.

8. A proton ($m_p=1.673\times10^{-27}$ kg) is confined to a region of width $L=1.0\times10^{-15}$ m (1 fm). Estimate $E_1$ in MeV (1 MeV $=1.602\times10^{-13}$ J, standard value). — **Hint:** $E_1=h^2/(8m_pL^2)$; compute the denominator first. Then divide by $1.602\times10^{-13}$. — **Solution sketch:** $8m_pL^2=8(1.673\times10^{-27})(1.0\times10^{-30})=1.338\times10^{-56}$; $E_1=4.390\times10^{-67}/1.338\times10^{-56}=3.28\times10^{-11}$ J $\approx205$ MeV, i.e. of order $10^{2}$ MeV. Units: joules, then MeV. This is a crude order-of-magnitude figure (real nuclei are three-dimensional with finite walls); the useful point is that nuclear energies are millions of times bigger than atomic ones, because $L$ is smaller by about $10^{5}$ and the energy goes as $1/L^2$ (with the heavier proton partly compensating). **Caveat:** $\sim205$ MeV is not small next to the proton's rest energy scale (about $938$ MeV, standard value), so the non-relativistic formula $E=p^2/2m$ behind $h^2/(8mL^2)$ is only a crude order-of-magnitude guide here; no relativistic answer is attempted.

9. Estimate the zero-point energy of an electron confined to an atom-sized region $\Delta x=0.10$ nm using $E\sim\hbar^2/(2m\,\Delta x^2)$, in eV. Then say how the exact box formula would compare. — **Hint:** $\hbar=1.055\times10^{-34}$ J s; square it, divide by $2m_e(\Delta x)^2$ with $\Delta x=1.0\times10^{-10}$ m. Then multiply by $\pi^2$ for the box. — **Solution sketch:** $\hbar^2=1.113\times10^{-68}$; $2m_e\Delta x^2=2(9.109\times10^{-31})(1.0\times10^{-20})=1.822\times10^{-50}$; $E\sim6.11\times10^{-19}$ J $=3.8$ eV. The exact box formula would be $\pi^2\times$ larger, about $38$ eV. Either way the answer is an eV-scale energy, which is why atomic spectra involve energies of a few to tens of eV (for example hydrogen's ionisation energy is 13.6 eV, standard value). Units: $\mathrm{(J\,s)^2/(kg\,m^2)=J}$.

10. Limiting case: for the infinite box show that $E_{n+1}-E_n=(2n+1)E_1$. What happens to the spacing as $L\to\infty$, and what is $E_1$ for an electron in a box of width $1.00$ cm (compare with $0.588$ eV at $0.80$ nm)? — **Hint:** expand $(n+1)^2-n^2$. $E_1\propto L^{-2}$, so the width ratio gives the answer without new constants. — **Solution sketch:** $(n+1)^2-n^2=2n+1$, so the spacing is $(2n+1)E_1$. As $L\to\infty$, $E_1\propto1/L^2\to0$ so all spacings vanish: the levels merge into a continuum, the classical (free particle) limit. For $L=1.00\times10^{-2}$ m: $L$ is $1.25\times10^{7}$ times larger than $0.80$ nm, so $E_1$ is $1.5625\times10^{14}$ times smaller: $0.588/1.5625\times10^{14}=3.76\times10^{-15}$ eV ($6.02\times10^{-34}$ J). No experiment could resolve steps that small. Units: eV, joules.

11. Short answer. State two ways in which the classical probability distribution of a mass on a spring differs from what you expect for a quantum oscillator in its lowest state. Do not calculate anything. — **Hint:** compare (i) where the density is largest, and (ii) whether it can leak beyond the turning points; also recall the lowest energy. — **Solution sketch:** (i) Classical density is smallest at the centre and biggest at the turning points ($\propto1/|v|$); the lowest quantum state is a single bump centred on $x=0$, biggest in the middle. (ii) Classical density is exactly zero beyond $x=\pm A$; the quantum $|\psi|^2$ is not zero beyond the classical turning points. Also (iii) the classical energy is any positive value, including zero, while the quantum lowest energy is $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$. ((i) and (ii) are standard qualitative facts about the ground state; they are stated here, not derived.) Large-$n$ agreement (the case promised in Move P3.2): for highly excited states the quantum $|\psi|^2$ wiggles rapidly, and its average over those wiggles approaches the classical $1/|v|$ shape. This is the standard correspondence principle, stated and not derived here.

## Anti-patterns / Common mistakes

- **"The ground state has zero energy."** Not for a confined particle and not for the oscillator: $E_1=\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$ in the box and $E_0=\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ for the oscillator. Zero would mean $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ both zero.
- **Using $\hbar$ where $h$ is needed (or the reverse).** $\lambda=h/p$ but $p=\hbar k$; $E_n=n^2h^2/(8mL^2)$ but $E_n=n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$. If your energy comes out $4\pi^2$ off, check which one you used.
- **Mixing up the labels.** The box starts at $n=1$ (Eq. 4.19); the oscillator starts at $n=0$ (Eq. 5.20). "$n=1$ is the ground state" is true for the box and false for the oscillator (where it is the first excited state).
- **Thinking the oscillator's steps are the box's steps.** The oscillator's levels are equally spaced ($\hbar\omega$); the box's spread like $n^2$.
