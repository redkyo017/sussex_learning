# P1 — Energy and Classical State

**Time box (about 1.5 h):** 10 min warm-up · 40 min moves · 30 min exercises · 10 min apply and confusion log.

## Why this matters

The Week 1 lecture (`week_1/week_1_lecture.md`, §1.1) opens by comparing quantum mechanics with the classical mechanics you may half remember: Newton's second law, force from a potential, "the state determines the future". Then the Hamiltonian $\hat H$ and the momentum operator $\hat p$ arrive, and every later week treats $\hat H$ as "the energy operator" (Week 4 §4.1 turns the Schrödinger equation into $\hat H\psi=E\psi$). The Week 1 lecture states $\hat H$ without deriving it from the classical energy, and Week 5 (Eqs. 5.3 and 5.4) writes the oscillator Hamiltonian as the classical $p^2/2m+\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$ with $x,p$ replaced by operators. The general recipe is the same: $\hat H$ comes from the classical energy $E=p^2/2m+V$, with one substitution. If that classical energy is fuzzy, $\hat H$ looks like magic. If it is sharp, $\hat H$ is a translation.

This file also fixes the units: joules, electron-volts, and the size of $\hbar$. Without them, no number in Module 3 can be sanity-checked. Nothing here needs more than school algebra and one derivative.

## Moves

### Move P1.1 — Newtonian state: $x$, $v$, $a$, $p=mv$; determinism (8 min)

**Say it aloud.** *Position* $x$ says where the particle is (in metres). *Velocity* $v = \mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t$ says how fast $x$ changes (metres per second). *Acceleration* $a = \mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}t = \mathrm{d}^2x/\mathrm{d}t^2$ says how fast $v$ changes (metres per second squared). *Momentum* $p = mv$ ("p equals m v") is mass times velocity (kilogram metres per second). Momentum, not velocity, is the quantity quantum mechanics uses.

**What "state" means.** In classical mechanics the *state* of a particle is the minimum information that fixes its future. For one particle in one dimension the lecture (§1.1) says: give the initial position $x(0)$ and the initial velocity $v(0)$, and the future $x(t)$ is *unique*. Knowing $x$ and $v$ is the same as knowing $x$ and $p$ (because $p = mv$). That pair is the **classical state**. (Note: the lecture itself says the classical state is described by the position $x$, with velocity and momentum recoverable from $x(t)$. This file uses the pair $(x,p)$ because $p=mv$ makes the two descriptions equivalent; it does not contradict the lecture.) The lecture calls this **determinism**: the present state fixes the entire future.

**Worked example.** A trolley of mass $2\ \mathrm{kg}$ rolls at $v = 3\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Its momentum is $p = mv = 2\times3 = 6\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$. Units check: kg times m/s gives kg m/s. Nothing else is needed to know where it goes next if no force acts: $x(t) = x(0) + 3t$.

**Why two numbers, not one?** Two trolleys at the same place with different velocities end up in different places. A photograph (position only) does not fix the future; position plus velocity does.

**Quantum bridge:** The classical state is two numbers, $(x, p)$. The quantum state is a whole function $\psi(x)$ (Move P1.5). The lecture says both theories are deterministic, but they mean different things by "state".

### Move P1.2 — Force from a potential; Newton's second law as a differential equation (8 min)

**Say it aloud.** A *force* $F$ (in newtons, $1\ \mathrm{N} = 1\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s^2}$) is a push or pull. **Newton's second law** (lecture Eq. 1.1) says $F = ma$: the acceleration is force divided by mass. A **potential energy** $V(x)$ (in joules) is stored energy that depends on position. The lecture (Eq. 1.2) gives the link between them:

$$F = -\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}x}\qquad(\text{the lecture writes a partial derivative, }-\partial V/\partial x).$$

Read it as "force points downhill": the force is minus the slope of the potential. Combining $F=ma$ with $F=-\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}x$ gives the lecture's second-order differential equation (Eq. 1.3):

$$m\frac{\mathrm{d}^2x}{\mathrm{d}t^2} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}x}.$$

"Second order" means two derivatives in time, so solving needs two initial conditions: $x(0)$ and $v(0)$. That is why the state is two numbers.

**Worked example (a mass in a linear potential).** A $1\ \mathrm{kg}$ mass sits in $V = \alpha x$ with $\alpha = 4\ \mathrm{J/m}$. (Units: $1\ \mathrm{J/m} = 1\ \mathrm{N}$.)

1. Force: $F = -\mathrm{d}(\alpha x)/\mathrm{d}x = -\alpha = -4\ \mathrm{N}$, the same everywhere. It pushes towards negative $x$.
2. Acceleration: $a = F/m = -4\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
3. Integrate once: $v(t) = v(0) - 4t$. Integrate twice: $x(t) = x(0) + v(0)t - 2t^2$.
4. Take $x(0) = 0$, $v(0) = +6\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Then $x(t) = 6t - 2t^2$, which turns round when $v = 6 - 4t = 0$, at $t = 1.5\ \mathrm{s}$, where $x = 9 - 4.5 = 4.5\ \mathrm{m}$.

**Recipe.** (i) Differentiate $V$ to get $F$. (ii) Divide by $m$ to get $a$. (iii) Integrate twice, fixing the two constants with $x(0)$ and $v(0)$.

**Quantum bridge:** The Schrödinger equation (lecture Eq. 1.4) has the *same* ingredients, $m$ and $V$, but $V$ enters as a multiplier of $\psi$, not as a force. The force never appears; the potential does.

### Move P1.3 — Energy: $p^2/2m + V$, the classical Hamiltonian (8 min)

**Say it aloud.** **Kinetic energy** is the energy of motion, $\tfrac12 mv^2$. Since $p = mv$, that is

$$T = \tfrac12 mv^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m}.$$

Check: $\frac{p^2}{2m} = \frac{m^2v^2}{2m} = \tfrac12mv^2$. **Potential energy** is $V(x)$. The **total energy** is the sum, written as a function of position and momentum:

$$E = H(x, p) = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V(x).$$

This function $H$ is called the **classical Hamiltonian**. With no friction, the total energy is constant in time: kinetic energy converts to potential energy and back, but the sum does not change. (Conservation of energy follows from $F = -\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}x$; you can take it as the working rule.)

**Worked example (same linear-potential mass).** For the $1\ \mathrm{kg}$ mass in $V = 4x$ (joules, with $x$ in metres) launched from $x = 0$ at $6\ \mathrm{m/s}$:

- Total energy: $E = \tfrac12(1)(6)^2 + 4\times0 = 18\ \mathrm{J}$.
- At the turning point, all energy is potential: $4x = 18$, so $x = 4.5\ \mathrm{m}$. This matches the value found from the motion in Move P1.2, without solving any differential equation.

**Why it works.** A conserved total lets you skip the time dependence. "How high?" or "how fast at this place?" questions become one line of algebra. Near the Earth's surface, gravity is a linear potential, $V = mgh$ (with $g$ the gravitational acceleration, $9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, standard value), which is why Exercise 4 uses the same trick.

**Quantum bridge:** In quantum mechanics, "the energy" is what $\hat H$ returns: $\hat H|\psi\rangle = E|\psi\rangle$ (lecture Eq. 1.11). The operator $\hat H$ is the classical $H(x,p)$ with $p$ replaced by an operator (Move P1.5).

### Move P1.4 — SI units, eV, sizes of $\hbar$ and $h$; dimensional analysis (8 min)

**SI base units used in QM.** Length: metre (m). Mass: kilogram (kg). Time: second (s). (Charge uses the coulomb, C; magnetic quantities appear in P4.) Everything else is built from these:

| Quantity | Unit | In base units |
|---|---|---|
| force | newton, N | kg m s$^{-2}$ |
| energy | joule, J | kg m$^2$ s$^{-2}$ ($=$ N m) |
| momentum | | kg m s$^{-1}$ |
| action ($\hbar$, $h$) | J s | kg m$^2$ s$^{-1}$ |

**The joule and the electron-volt.** One **joule** is the energy of a force of $1\ \mathrm{N}$ acting over $1\ \mathrm{m}$. It is huge on atomic scales, so atomic physics uses the **electron-volt (eV)**: the energy an electron gains crossing a potential difference of one volt. The conversion is

$$1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}\quad(\text{standard value}).$$

To go from joules to eV, *divide* by $1.602\times10^{-19}$; to go from eV to joules, *multiply*. Sanity check: an atomic energy of about $10^{-18}\ \mathrm{J}$ is about $6\ \mathrm{eV}$, a modest number, which is why eV is the natural unit.

**Sizes of $\hbar$ and $h$.** The lecture (§1.1) gives $\hbar = h/2\pi = 1.055\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$, the **reduced Planck's constant**. **Planck's constant** itself is $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$ (standard value); indeed $6.626/(2\pi) = 6.626/6.283 = 1.0546$, consistent with the lecture's $1.055$. Both have units of J s, an energy times a time (called *action*), and both are tiny compared with everyday actions (a $1\ \mathrm{kg}$ mass moving $1\ \mathrm{m/s}$ over $1\ \mathrm{m}$ has action of order $1\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$, that is $\sim10^{34}\hbar$). "Tiny" does not mean "ignorable": it means quantum effects show up when the relevant momentum times distance is comparable to $\hbar$. Exercise 7 makes that comparison.

**Dimensional analysis: a 4-step recipe.** Use it to check a formula without knowing any numbers.

1. **Write** each symbol's units in base units (kg, m, s).
2. **Substitute** them into both sides of the formula.
3. **Simplify** and compare: both sides must have identical base units. Sums must add like with like.
4. **If they differ,** the formula is wrong (or a constant is missing). Find the missing factor by asking what would repair the units.

**Worked example.** Check the wave-function relation $p = \hbar k$ (Week 4 §4.2), where $k$ is a wavenumber in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Step 1: $\hbar$ is J s $=$ kg m$^2$ s$^{-1}$, and $k$ is m$^{-1}$. Step 2 and 3: $\hbar k = \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}\times m^{-1}} = \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$. That is the unit of momentum. The formula passes.

Dimensional analysis cannot catch a missing pure number (a factor of 2 or $\pi$), but it catches most slips, fast.

**Quantum bridge:** Every formula in this module can be checked this way. $\hbar$ having units of J s is what makes $\hbar\omega$ an energy ($\mathrm{J\,s\times s^{-1} = J}$, used in Week 4 for $E=\hbar\omega$).

### Move P1.5 — Bridge: classical state $(x,p)$ versus quantum $\psi$; $\hat H=\hat p^2/2m+V$ (8 min)

**The translation rule.** Start from the classical energy:

$$E = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V(x).$$

Quantum mechanics keeps this recipe and replaces the number $p$ by an **operator** $\hat p$, one that acts on wave functions. In one dimension the lecture's momentum operator (Eq. 1.14, $\hat P|\psi\rangle = -i\hbar\boldsymbol\nabla|\psi\rangle$; Eq. 1.23 is its eigenvalue form, $-i\hbar\boldsymbol\nabla|\psi\rangle=\mathbf p|\psi\rangle$) reads, for the $x$ component,

$$\hat p = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}.$$

Because $1/i = -i$, this is the same operator as $(\hbar/i)\,\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}x$, the form used in Day 4 Move 4.8. The two forms in the path are the same thing written two ways. Squaring it (apply it twice):

$$\hat p^2 = \left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right) = (-i)^2\hbar^2\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} = -\hbar^2\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}.$$

So

$$\hat H = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} + V = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x),$$

which is exactly the operator in the lecture's Schrödinger equation (Eq. 1.4, right-hand side) and in Week 4 §4.1, where it is labelled $\hat H$ under the brace. The potential $V(x)$ stays as "multiply by $V(x)$". That is why the Hamiltonian is called the *energy operator*: it is the classical energy expression, translated.

**What changes about "state".** Classically, the state is $(x(0), v(0))$, two numbers, and the lecture's law (Eq. 1.3) gives $x(t)$. Quantum mechanically, the state is a function $\psi(x)$ at $t=0$, and the Schrödinger equation gives $\psi(x,t)$ for all later $t$ (lecture §1.1: "given initial conditions at time $t=0$ [quantum: $\psi(x,0)$; classical: $x(0)$]"). Both are deterministic in how the state evolves. The difference is what the state *means*: $|\psi(x,t)|^2$ is a probability density (the Born rule), not a position. This file does not solve any Schrödinger equation.

**Say it in one line.** Classical physics: state is where and how fast; quantum physics: state is a function whose squared modulus gives probabilities.

**Quantum bridge (the point of the whole file):** Classical $E = p^2/2m + V$ becomes $\hat H = \hat p^2/2m + V$ with $\hat p = -i\hbar\,\partial/\partial x$. Every later use of $\hat H$ is this translation.

## Exercises

Constants: $\hbar = 1.055\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$ (lecture). Standard values: $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$, $m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$, $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. All speeds here are far below light speed, so the ordinary $\tfrac12mv^2$ applies.

1. **Electron kinetic energy.** An electron moves at $2.0\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find its kinetic energy in joules and in eV. — **Hint:** use $T=\tfrac12 m_e v^2$ with $v^2 = 4.0\times10^{12}$; then divide by $1.602\times10^{-19}$ to convert to eV. — **Solution sketch:** $v^2 = (2.0\times10^6)^2 = 4.0\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}$. $m_ev^2 = 9.109\times10^{-31}\times4.0\times10^{12} = 3.6436\times10^{-18}$. Half of it: $T = 1.822\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm{J}$. In eV: $1.8218\times10^{-18}/1.602\times10^{-19} = 11.37\ \mathrm{eV}\approx11\ \mathrm{eV}$. Units check: kg $\times$ m$^2$/s$^2$ $=$ kg m$^2$ s$^{-2}$ $=$ J. Plausible: atomic-scale energies are some eV.

2. **Force from a potential.** Find $F=-\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}x$ for (a) $V=\alpha x$ with $\alpha = 2.5\ \mathrm{J/m}$, and (b) $V=\tfrac12kx^2$ with $k=40\ \mathrm{N/m}$. State the force on the mass in each case, including its direction at $x = +0.05\ \mathrm{m}$ for (b). — **Hint:** differentiate; in (b) the derivative of $x^2$ is $2x$, so the $\tfrac12$ cancels. — **Solution sketch:** (a) $F=-\alpha=-2.5\ \mathrm{N}$, constant, pointing towards $-x$ wherever the mass is. (b) $F=-kx=-40x$. At $x=+0.05\ \mathrm{m}$, $F=-40\times0.05=-2.0\ \mathrm{N}$: the force points back towards $x=0$ (a restoring force). Units check: $\mathrm{J/m}=\mathrm{N}$; $(\mathrm{N/m})\times\mathrm{m}=\mathrm{N}$.

3. **Constant force, integrated twice.** A cart of mass $0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$ feels a constant force $F=2.0\ \mathrm{N}$ (in the $+x$ direction), with $x(0)=-1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ and $v(0)=+3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find $x(t)$, and its position and velocity at $t=2.0\ \mathrm{s}$. — **Hint:** $a=F/m$ is constant; integrate $a$ to get $v(t)$, integrate again to get $x(t)$, fixing each constant with the initial values. — **Solution sketch:** $a=2.0/0.50=4.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. $v(t)=3.0+4.0t$. $x(t)=-1.0+3.0t+2.0t^2$ (the $2.0=\tfrac12\times4.0$). At $t=2.0$: $v=3.0+8.0=11\ \mathrm{m/s}$; $x=-1.0+6.0+8.0=13\ \mathrm{m}$. Units check: $\mathrm{N/kg}=\mathrm{m/s^2}$; $\mathrm{(m/s^2)\,s^2}=\mathrm{m}$. Cross-check: $x(0)$ from the formula is $-1.0$ and $v(0)$ (the derivative at $t=0$) is $3.0$, as required.

4. **Ball thrown upward.** A ball is thrown straight up at $12\ \mathrm{m/s}$ from ground level (take $V=mgy$, $g=9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, ignore air). (a) Find the maximum height. (b) Find its speed when it is $3.0\ \mathrm{m}$ above the ground. — **Hint:** total energy $\tfrac12mv^2+mgy$ is constant; the mass cancels. At the top $v=0$. — **Solution sketch:** (a) $\tfrac12mv_0^2=mgh\Rightarrow h=v_0^2/2g=144/19.62=7.34\ \mathrm{m}$. (b) $v^2=v_0^2-2gy=144-2(9.81)(3.0)=144-58.86=85.14$, so $v=9.23\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (less than $12$, as expected). Units check: $(\mathrm{m/s})^2/(\mathrm{m/s^2})=\mathrm{m}$. Mass never appeared, so any ball gives the same answer.

5. **Unit check of two formulas.** Use the 4-step recipe to check (a) $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$, where $k$ is a spring constant in $\mathrm{N/m}$, and (b) $E=p^2/2m$. — **Hint:** first turn N into base units, kg m s$^{-2}$. Then substitute. — **Solution sketch:** (a) $k=\mathrm{N/m}=\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}/m}=\mathrm{kg\,s^{-2}}$; $k/m=\mathrm{s^{-2}}$; the square root is $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, the unit of an angular frequency (radians are dimensionless). Passes. (b) $p^2=(\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}})^2=\mathrm{kg^2m^2s^{-2}}$; divided by $m$ (kg) this is $\mathrm{kg\,m^2s^{-2}}=\mathrm{J}$. Passes. As a failed check: $E=p^2/m$ would give the same units, so units cannot fix the $\tfrac12$.

6. **Units of $\hbar$.** From $\hbar=h/2\pi$ find the units of $\hbar$, show that $\mathrm{J\,s}=\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$, and check numerically that $h=6.626\times10^{-34}$ gives the lecture's $\hbar$. — **Hint:** $2\pi$ is a pure number, so it does not change the units. Replace J by kg m$^2$ s$^{-2}$. — **Solution sketch:** $\hbar$ has the units of $h$, J s. $\mathrm{J\,s}=\mathrm{kg\,m^2s^{-2}\times s}=\mathrm{kg\,m^2s^{-1}}=\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$. Numerics: $6.626\times10^{-34}/6.2832=1.0546\times10^{-34}\approx1.055\times10^{-34}$, matching the lecture. Units check: identical to the symbolic result above; also the same as (momentum $\times$ length).

7. **How quantum is it?** Compare a $1.0\ \mathrm{g}$ bullet at $300\ \mathrm{m/s}$ with an electron at $1.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Find each momentum, then the ratio $\hbar/(p\cdot L)$ with $L=1\ \mathrm{nm}=10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m}$. Which one is "quantum" at that length scale? — **Hint:** convert $1.0\ \mathrm{g}$ to kg first. $pL$ has units kg m$^2$/s, the same as $\hbar$, so the ratio has no units. — **Solution sketch:** Bullet: $p=1.0\times10^{-3}\times300=0.30\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$; $pL=3.0\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$; $\hbar/pL=1.055\times10^{-34}/3.0\times10^{-10}=3.5\times10^{-25}$. Electron: $p=9.109\times10^{-31}\times1.0\times10^6=9.109\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$; $pL=9.109\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$; $\hbar/pL=1.055/9.109=0.116\approx0.12$. Units check: $\mathrm{J\,s}/\mathrm{(kg\,m/s\times m)}=\mathrm{(kg\,m^2/s)/(kg\,m^2/s)}=1$. For the bullet the ratio is about $10^{-25}$: quantum effects are hopelessly small. For the electron it is order $0.1$: quantum effects matter. Order-of-magnitude comparison only.

8. **From $H$ to $\hat H$.** Write the classical Hamiltonian for (a) a free particle (no force) and (b) a particle with $V=\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$. Then write the corresponding $\hat H$ by replacing $p\to\hat p=-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial x$. Do not solve anything. — **Hint:** free means $V=0$. Compute $\hat p^2$ as "apply $\hat p$ twice", using $(-i)^2=-1$. — **Solution sketch:** (a) $H=p^2/2m$; $\hat H=\hat p^2/2m=-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}$. (b) $H=\dfrac{p^2}{2m}+\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$; $\hat H=-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}+\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$. The potential term is unchanged: "multiply by $\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$". Units check: $\hbar^2/m\cdot\partial_x^2=\mathrm{(kg^2m^4s^{-2})/(kg)\times m^{-2}}=\mathrm{kg\,m^2s^{-2}}=\mathrm{J}$, acting on $\psi$ to give J times $\psi$. Both agree with the operator form in the lecture (Eq. 1.4, and §4.1).

9. **State: classical versus quantum.** In two sentences, say what changes between "state $=(x,p)$" and "state $=\psi(x)$". — **Hint:** Think of how many numbers each needs, and what you can predict from each. — **Solution sketch:** Checklist for a good answer: (i) the classical state is two numbers, $x$ and $p$, that give exact values of every observable; the quantum state is a function $\psi(x)$ (infinitely many numbers) from which $|\psi|^2$ gives only probabilities for measurement outcomes (Born rule). (ii) Both evolve deterministically from the initial state (lecture §1.1: $x(0),v(0)$ versus $\psi(x,0)$). Full marks if you name both what the state is and what it predicts.

10. **Limiting-case check.** Does $E=p^2/2m+V$ reduce correctly when $V=0$? Check that it gives $\tfrac12mv^2$, that the energy is constant, and what happens as $p\to0$. — **Hint:** put $V=0$ and $p=mv$. A free particle feels $F=-\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}x=0$. — **Solution sketch:** $E=p^2/2m=m^2v^2/2m=\tfrac12mv^2$, purely kinetic. With $F=0$, $a=0$ so $v$, hence $E$, is constant: consistent. As $p\to0$, $E\to0$: a particle at rest with $V=0$ has no energy. (Notice for later: the quantum version of this, $\hat H=\hat p^2/2m$, also gives $E\to0$ for a zero-momentum state.) Units check: $\mathrm{kg\,(m/s)^2}=\mathrm{J}$.

## Anti-patterns / Common mistakes

- **Mixing $p$ and $v$.** $p=mv$. In $E=p^2/2m$ use $p$; in $\tfrac12mv^2$ use $v$. Writing $\tfrac12 p^2$ or $p^2/m$ is a slip that dimensional analysis cannot always catch.
- **Forgetting eV$\leftrightarrow$J.** Energies in atomic problems are in eV; $\hbar$ and $m$ are in SI. Convert to joules for the arithmetic, then back to eV to judge whether the answer is sensible.
- **Treating $\hbar$ as small, so "ignore it".** The number $10^{-34}$ is tiny only in SI units. What matters is $\hbar$ compared with $p\times L$ for the system (Exercise 7), which for atoms is order one.
