## Moves

### Move P4.1 — Magnetic field, magnetic moment, torque, dipole energy (10 min)

**Say it aloud.** A **magnetic field** $\mathbf{B}$ ("B") is the thing magnets push against. Its size is measured in **tesla** (T). A **magnetic moment** $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ ("mu") is how strongly an object acts like a tiny bar magnet, and which way its north end points. Its units are joules per tesla (J/T). A compass needle is a magnetic moment; a bar magnet is a bigger one.

A magnetic moment in a field feels two separate things.

1. **A twist (torque).** The field tries to swing the moment round until it lines up with $\mathbf{B}$. With $\theta$ the angle between $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\mathbf{B}$,

$$\boldsymbol{\tau} = \boldsymbol{\mu}\times\mathbf{B}, \qquad |\boldsymbol{\tau}| = \mu B\sin\theta.$$

2. **An energy that depends on the angle.** Aligned is the lowest energy, anti-aligned the highest:

$$U = -\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{B} = -\mu B\cos\theta.$$

**Units check.** $\mu B$ has units $(\mathrm{J/T})\cdot\mathrm{T} = \mathrm{J}$, so $U$ is an energy. Torque has units $\mathrm{N\,m}$, which is also joules dimensionally, so $\mu B\sin\theta$ ($\sin$ has no units) fits.

**Worked example.** Take a moment of size $\mu_B = 9.274\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T}$ (the Bohr magneton, **standard value**; it is not given in the lecture) in a field $B = 1.00\ \mathrm{T}$.

- Aligned ($\theta = 0$): $U = -\mu_B B = -9.274\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J}$. In electron-volts, with $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$ (**standard value**): $9.274\times10^{-24}/1.602\times10^{-19} = 5.789\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$. So $U = -5.79\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$.
- Anti-aligned ($\theta = 180^\circ$): $U = +5.79\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$. The gap between the two is $2\mu_B B = 1.855\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J} = 1.158\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}$.
- At $\theta = 60^\circ$: $\sin 60^\circ = 0.8660$ and $\cos 60^\circ = 0.5$, so $|\tau| = 9.274\times10^{-24}\times0.8660 = 8.032\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ and $U = -4.637\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J}$.

For scale: $10^{-5}$ eV is tiny next to atomic energies of a few eV, which is why a beam of atoms is only nudged, not wrecked, by a laboratory magnet.

**Why it works.** A twist is not a push. A torque turns the moment; it does not move the atom's centre of mass sideways. Keep that in mind for the next move. (Standard background, not in the lecture: a moment with its own angular momentum does not simply flop into line; it circles, or *precesses*, around the field direction, like a tilted spinning top around the vertical, while the component along $\mathbf{B}$ stays constant.)

**Quantum bridge.** In P1 the total energy became the operator $\hat{H}$. For a spin in a field the energy is $-\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ with $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ turned into an operator proportional to spin (lecture: $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \tfrac{gq}{2m}\mathbf{S}$). The Week 6 lecture uses only the force from this energy, not the Hamiltonian, so treat this sentence as background.

### Move P4.2 — A force on a dipole needs a field gradient (10 min)

In P1 you met $F = -\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}x$: force is minus the slope of the potential energy. Apply it to the dipole energy. Take a field pointing along $z$ whose strength varies with height, $\mathbf{B} = B(z)\,\hat{\mathbf{e}}_z$, and a moment whose $z$-component is $\mu_z$. Then $U = -\mu_z B(z)$, so

$$F_z = -\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}z} = \mu_z\,\frac{\mathrm{d}B_z}{\mathrm{d}z}.$$

This is the lecture's force law (§6.1), which writes $\langle\mu_z\rangle$ for the average $z$-component. Say it as "force equals the $z$-part of the moment times the field gradient".

**Units check.** $\mathrm{J/T}\times\mathrm{T/m} = \mathrm{J/m} = \mathrm{N}$. Also $\mathrm{J/T} = \mathrm{A\,m^2}$ (using $\mathrm{T} = \mathrm{kg\,s^{-2}A^{-1}}$ and $\mathrm{J} = \mathrm{kg\,m^2 s^{-2}}$), and $\mathrm{A\,m^2}\cdot\mathrm{T/m} = \mathrm{A\,m\,T} = \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}} = \mathrm{N}$.

**The lesson in one line.** The force depends on the *slope* $\mathrm{d}B_z/\mathrm{d}z$, not on how strong the field is. In a uniform field the slope is zero, so there is a torque but **no net force** and the beam is not split. Physically, the north end and south end of the tiny magnet are pulled equally, so the pulls cancel. In a non-uniform field one end sits in a stronger field than the other, and a net force is left over. That is why the apparatus of §6.1 has magnet poles shaped to make an **inhomogeneous** field.

**Worked example.** Take $\mu_z = \mu_B = 9.274\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T}$ and a gradient of $25\ \mathrm{T/m}$. Then $F_z = 9.274\times10^{-24}\times25 = 2.319\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{N}$ (more precisely $2.3185\times10^{-22}$). Reverse the sign of $\mu_z$ and the force reverses. The size of the push is set by $\mu_z$, so the screen pattern is a map of the allowed $\mu_z$ values.

The lecture adds: if $\boldsymbol{\mu}\perp\mathbf{B}$ there is no deflection, and aligned or anti-aligned gives the largest. That is the $\mu_z = \mu\cos\theta$ picture: $\theta = 90^\circ$ gives $\mu_z = 0$.

**Quantum bridge.** $F_z\propto\mu_z\propto S_z$ (through $\boldsymbol{\mu}\propto\mathbf{S}$). So the deflection reads out the spin component along the gradient. The magnet is a *measuring device for $S_z$*, and the two outcomes are the two beams.

### Move P4.3 — Classical angular momentum and the classical prediction (10 min)

**Say it aloud.** **Angular momentum** is the "amount of turning" something has. For a point mass at position $\mathbf{r}$ (measured from a chosen centre) with momentum $\mathbf{p}$,

$$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}, \qquad |\mathbf{L}| = m v r \ \text{ for circular motion.}$$

Its units are $\mathrm{m}\cdot\mathrm{kg\,m/s} = \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s} = \mathrm{J\,s}$, the same units as $\hbar$ (see the check below).

**Worked example.** A $0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$ ball whirled on a circle of radius $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ at $2.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ has $L = 0.50\times2.0\times0.30 = 0.30\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s} = 0.30\ \mathrm{J\,s}$. Compare with $\hbar/2 = 5.275\times10^{-35}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$ (using the lecture's $\hbar = 1.055\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$): the ratio is $0.30/5.275\times10^{-35} = 5.7\times10^{33}$. Everyday spin is astronomically many times $\hbar$, which is why turning looks smooth and continuous to us.

**Moment and angular momentum go together.** For a charge $q$ of mass $m$ circulating (a tiny current loop), classical physics gives $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \tfrac{q}{2m}\mathbf{L}$ (standard background). The lecture's $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \tfrac{gq}{2m}\mathbf{S}$ has the same shape with the $g$-factor added; the classical loop is the case $g = 1$.

**What classical physics predicts for the beam.** The oven gives atoms whose moments point in *arbitrary* directions (lecture §6.1: "randomly oriented"). If the angle $\theta$ between $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and the $z$-axis can be anything, then $\mu_z = \mu\cos\theta$ can be anything from $-\mu$ to $+\mu$. Each value gives a different force $F_z = \mu_z\,\mathrm{d}B_z/\mathrm{d}z$, so each atom lands in a different place. The prediction is a **continuous smear**, filled in the middle, symmetric about the undeflected line. This is the top panel of the lecture's Figure 6.2.

**Quantum bridge.** The classical picture says *every* orientation, hence every $\mu_z$, is allowed. The experiment (next move) will say otherwise.

### Move P4.4 — Spin, $S_z = \pm\hbar/2$, and two beams (10 min)

**What is observed.** Silver atoms (lecture: 47 electrons, 46 in a spherically symmetric cloud, the 47th in a $5s$ orbital) are sent through the inhomogeneous field. The screen shows **two separate spots**, one up and one down, and nothing in between (lecture §6.1, Figure 6.2 lower panel).

**What that means.** Two spots mean $\mu_z$ takes exactly two values. Because $\boldsymbol{\mu}\propto\mathbf{S}$, so does the spin component $S_z$: it is **quantised**. The lecture calls spin the "intrinsic angular momentum" of the particle: angular momentum the particle has by nature, not because a part of it circles a centre. The moment is carried by the unpaired outer electron; the other 46 cancel out (lecture, standard background: a $5s$ electron has no orbital angular momentum, so the moment is pure spin).

**The value $\hbar/2$ is a convention, not in the lecture.** The lecture writes only $S_z = +$ and $S_z = -$. The standard convention is $S_z = \pm\hbar/2$, i.e. $\hat{S}_z = \tfrac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_z$ with the Pauli matrix of Day 3. Numerically $\hbar/2 = 0.5275\times10^{-34} = 5.275\times10^{-35}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$ with the lecture's $\hbar$. Two values (for spin one-half) is the "2" in "2 beams".

**A consistency check with the lecture's formula.** Take $\mu_z = \tfrac{gq}{2m}S_z$ with $q = e$ (in size), $g = 2$ (**standard value** for the electron), $m = m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$ (**standard value**), $S_z = \hbar/2$, $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$ (**standard value**):

$$\mu_z = \frac{2\,e}{2m_e}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{2} = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e} = \frac{1.602\times10^{-19}\times1.055\times10^{-34}}{2\times9.109\times10^{-31}} = \frac{1.690\times10^{-53}}{1.8218\times10^{-30}} = 9.277\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T}.$$

This is the Bohr magneton, agreeing with $9.274\times10^{-24}$ to $0.03\%$ (the small gap comes from rounding $\hbar$). *Units:* $\mathrm{C\cdot J\,s/kg} = \mathrm{A\,s\cdot kg\,m^2 s^{-1}/kg} = \mathrm{A\,m^2}$, which is $\mathrm{J/T}$, as it must be.

Two warnings. First, the $m$ in $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \tfrac{gq}{2m}\mathbf{S}$ is the mass of the particle carrying the moment, the **electron**. The silver atom's mass ($1.79\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$, **standard value**; about $2\times10^{5}$ times $m_e$) enters only Newton's second law, when you ask how far the atom is pushed. (The lecture's phrase "a heavy electron" refers to the atom's spin behaviour, not its moment.) Second, for a negative charge the moment points opposite to the spin; that only flips which beam is which.

**Why not a spinning ball.** A spinning ball can be tilted to *any* angle, and that freedom is exactly the classical smear. The lecture's warning (§6.1) is that the little-arrow image "will deceive you sooner or later". Use "spin" as the name of a quantity with its own rules, not as a description of motion.

**One atom, one beam.** For a single atom the result is one spot; many atoms build up both (lecture §6.3: "either but not simultaneously both"). See the companion's retrieval question 4, which flags a wording issue in the quiz key.

**Quantum bridge.** The classical band becomes two allowed values, the eigenvalues of $\hat{S}_z$. The apparatus is the operator, and the beams are its eigenstates $\lvert\psi_z^+\rangle$, $\lvert\psi_z^-\rangle$.

### Move P4.5 — Sequential magnets, states and interference (10 min)

The lecture's notation: $\lvert\psi_z^\pm\rangle$ for the two $\hat{S}_z$ beams, $\lvert\psi_x^\pm\rangle$ for the two $\hat{S}_x$ beams, and $\langle b\mid a\rangle$ for the amplitude of state $a$ to be found in $b$, with $P(b\mid a) = \lvert\langle b\mid a\rangle\rvert^2$. Read the operator chain right to left: the lecture (§6.2) notes that the left-most operator in its chain corresponds to the last apparatus and the right-most to the first.

**Same axis twice (Eq. 6.1).** Feed one beam of a $z$-magnet into another $z$-magnet: it comes out in the same beam every time. So a magnet *prepares* a state as well as measuring one.

**Different axes (Eq. 6.2).** Feed a $z$-beam into an $x$-magnet: 50/50 between the two $x$ beams. Eq. 6.2 lists $1/\sqrt2$ for all four amplitudes. This is a **table of magnitudes**; the phase (sign) of each amplitude is a convention that the lecture does not fix. In the standard column convention (from Day 3, an assumption not in the lecture) $\lvert\psi_z^+\rangle = (1,0)^{\mathsf T}$, $\lvert\psi_z^-\rangle = (0,1)^{\mathsf T}$, $\lvert\psi_x^\pm\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,\pm1)^{\mathsf T}$, which gives $\langle\psi_z^-\mid\psi_x^-\rangle = -1/\sqrt2$. The companion's sign note explains why an all-plus table would not be a consistent orthonormal set. Do not treat the matrix of moduli printed under Eq. 6.1 as the spin operator: $\hat{S}_z$ has eigenvalues $\pm\hbar/2$.

**Any angle (Eq. 6.3, 6.4).** Prepared in $\lvert\psi_m^+\rangle$ and sent into $\hat{S}_n$, the probability of $S_n = +$ is $(1+\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{n})/2 = (1+\cos\theta)/2$. Read it as a limiting-case check: $\theta = 0$ gives $1$; $\theta = 180^\circ$ gives $0$.

**A physical description of the recombining experiment (§6.4).** Start with atoms prepared in $S_z = +$. Split them by an $x$-magnet into an upper beam ($S_x = +$) and a lower beam ($S_x = -$). Recombine the beams with a second, reversed, magnet so both paths end in the same beam. Send it into a final $z$-magnet.

- **Both paths open and nobody records which path an atom took.** The final result is the same as if the splitter and recombiner were not there: every atom exits in the $S_z = +$ beam and none in $S_z = -$.
- **One path blocked, or the path recorded.** Then an atom is known to have been in an $x$ state, and the final $z$-magnet gives $+$ and $-$ with probability $1/2$ each (Eq. 6.2). An outcome that was *impossible* with both paths open now happens.

The mechanism is the double-slit one of P2: each path contributes an **amplitude**, and with both paths open unobserved you **add the amplitudes** *before* squaring. For the outcome $S_z = -$,

$$\text{amplitude} = \langle\psi_z^-\mid\psi_x^+\rangle\langle\psi_x^+\mid\psi_z^+\rangle + \langle\psi_z^-\mid\psi_x^-\rangle\langle\psi_x^-\mid\psi_z^+\rangle = \langle\psi_z^-\mid\psi_z^+\rangle = 0.$$

The middle equality is the completeness relation of an orthonormal basis, $\lvert\psi_x^+\rangle\langle\psi_x^+\rvert + \lvert\psi_x^-\rangle\langle\psi_x^-\rvert = 1$ (the identity), sandwiched between $\langle\psi_z^-\rvert$ and $\lvert\psi_z^+\rangle$. Day 2, Move 2.4 has the orthonormal expansion $\lvert\psi\rangle=\sum_j\lvert e_j\rangle\langle e_j\vert\psi\rangle$ from which this identity follows; the relation itself is standard and stated here. The two path terms cancel exactly, which is what the "dark fringe" of the double slit is. Blocking one path removes one term, so there is nothing to cancel it, and the outcome appears. If the path is recorded, you add *probabilities* instead and the cross-term never appears. This is why removing a block can *restore* the original beam: it restores the cancellation. (The same cancellation works if the middle magnet points along any axis $\mathbf{n}$ in the $xz$-plane, because the completeness relation is basis-independent; Exercise 8 uses that.)

**Quantum bridge.** Definite $S_z$ and definite $S_x$ cannot hold together (the operators do not commute, Day 3, Move 3.5); a state prepared by one magnet is a superposition in the basis of the next, and probabilities come from squared amplitudes. That is the whole content of §6.2 to §6.4 in the language of Days 3 and 5.

## Exercises

Constants: $\hbar = 1.055\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$ (lecture). Standard values: $\mu_B = 9.274\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T}$, silver atom mass $1.79\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$, $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$.

1. A made-up moment $\mu = 3.0\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/T}$ sits in a uniform field $B = 0.50\ \mathrm{T}$ at $\theta = 30^\circ$ to the field. Find the torque, and the dipole energy in joules and in eV. Show the units check. — **Hint:** $\tau = \mu B\sin\theta$ and $U = -\mu B\cos\theta$; $\sin30^\circ = 0.5$, $\cos30^\circ = 0.8660$. Divide joules by $1.602\times10^{-19}$ for eV. — **Solution sketch:** $\mu B = 3.0\times10^{-23}\times0.50 = 1.5\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J}$. Torque: $1.5\times10^{-23}\times0.5 = 7.5\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{N\,m}$. Energy: $U = -1.5\times10^{-23}\times0.8660 = -1.299\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J}$, and $-1.299\times10^{-23}/1.602\times10^{-19} = -8.109\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$. Units: $(\mathrm{J/T})\cdot\mathrm{T} = \mathrm{J}$, and $\mathrm{N\,m}$ has the dimensions of $\mathrm{J}$. Sanity check: the energy is negative because $\theta < 90^\circ$ is closer to aligned than to anti-aligned.

2. Silver atoms carry $\mu_z = +\mu_B$ through a region where $\mathrm{d}B_z/\mathrm{d}z = 10\ \mathrm{T/m}$. (a) Find the force. (b) Find the acceleration of one silver atom of mass $1.79\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$. (c) With toy numbers (an invented $0.10\ \mathrm{m}$ magnet length and $500\ \mathrm{m/s}$ speed, both made up), how far is the atom deflected on leaving the magnet, taking the acceleration as constant and neglecting any initial $z$-velocity? Give the units check. — **Hint:** (a) $F_z = \mu_z\,\mathrm{d}B_z/\mathrm{d}z$; (b) $a = F/m$; (c) time in the magnet is $t = L/v$, then $\Delta z = \tfrac12 a t^2$. — **Solution sketch:** (a) $F_z = 9.274\times10^{-24}\times10 = 9.274\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{N}$. (b) $a = 9.274\times10^{-23}/1.79\times10^{-25} = 518.1\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. (c) $t = 0.10/500 = 2.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s}$, $t^2 = 4.0\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s^2}$, $\Delta z = \tfrac12\times518.1\times4.0\times10^{-8} = 1.036\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m}\approx10\ \mu\mathrm{m}$; the two beams end up about $21\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ apart. Units: $\mathrm{(J/T)(T/m)} = \mathrm{J/m} = \mathrm{N}$; $\mathrm{N/kg} = \mathrm{m/s^2}$; $\mathrm{m/s^2}\cdot\mathrm{s^2} = \mathrm{m}$. Sanity check: a tiny nudge, which is why real experiments use very strong gradients and sensitive detection.

3. Explain in two or three sentences why a strong but *uniform* field along $z$ does not split a beam of silver atoms into two, even though it exerts a torque. — **Hint:** Start from $F_z = \mu_z\,\mathrm{d}B_z/\mathrm{d}z$ and put in a uniform field. — **Solution sketch:** A uniform field has $\mathrm{d}B_z/\mathrm{d}z = 0$, so $F_z = 0$ for every $\mu_z$: no net force, no deflection. The torque only turns the moment (it precesses); the north and south "ends" are pulled equally, so the pulls cancel. Limiting-case check: gradient $\to0$ gives force $\to0$ however large $B$ is.

4. A classical atom whose moment has magnitude $\mu_B$ enters the gradient of Exercise 2 with its moment at $75^\circ$ to the $z$-axis. What force does the classical model give, and how does it compare with the maximum force? Then say what the classical model predicts for the whole beam and what is observed. — **Hint:** $\mu_z = \mu\cos\theta$, $\cos75^\circ = 0.2588$. The maximum is at $\theta = 0$. — **Solution sketch:** $\mu_z = 0.2588\times9.274\times10^{-24} = 2.400\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T}$, so $F = 2.400\times10^{-24}\times10 = 2.400\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{N}$, which is $0.259$ of the maximum $9.274\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{N}$. Units: $(\mathrm{J/T})(\mathrm{T/m}) = \mathrm{N}$. Since every angle occurs, the classical prediction is a continuous filled band running between the two extreme deflections. What is seen is only two spots at the extremes; no atoms land at the $0.259$ position. (The observed spots correspond to two values of $\mu_z$, not to a continuum.)

5. Take $\hat{S}_z = \tfrac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_z$ and the state $\lvert\psi\rangle = \tfrac{5}{13}\lvert{\uparrow}\rangle + \tfrac{12}{13}\lvert{\downarrow}\rangle$. (a) Check normalisation. (b) State the possible results of an $\hat{S}_z$ measurement in $\mathrm{J\,s}$ and their probabilities. (c) Find $\langle\hat{S}_z\rangle$. (d) Find the probabilities of $S_x = +$ and $S_x = -$, using $\lvert\psi_x^\pm\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,\pm1)^{\mathsf T}$. — **Hint:** (a) $\sum\lvert c\rvert^2 = 1$. (b, c) Probabilities are $\lvert c_n\rvert^2$ and $\langle\hat{S}_z\rangle = \sum p_n a_n$. (d) $c_\pm = \langle\psi_x^\pm\mid\psi\rangle$; the coefficients are real, so conjugation changes nothing here. — **Solution sketch:** (a) $25/169 + 144/169 = 1$. (b) Eigenvalues $\pm\hbar/2 = \pm5.275\times10^{-35}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$; $P(+) = 25/169 = 0.148$, $P(-) = 144/169 = 0.852$. (c) $\langle\hat{S}_z\rangle = (\hbar/2)(25-144)/169 = -119\hbar/338 = -0.352\hbar = -3.714\times10^{-35}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$. (d) $\langle\psi_x^+\mid\psi\rangle = (5/13+12/13)/\sqrt2 = 17/(13\sqrt2)$, so $P(x{+}) = 289/338 = 0.855$; $\langle\psi_x^-\mid\psi\rangle = (5/13-12/13)/\sqrt2 = -7/(13\sqrt2)$, so $P(x{-}) = 49/338 = 0.145$. Check: $289/338 + 49/338 = 1$. Units: $\hbar$ is in $\mathrm{J\,s}$ and probabilities have no units.

6. A beam is prepared in $S_z = -$. It then enters a box $\hat{S}_n$ whose axis $\mathbf{n}$ lies at $30^\circ$ to the $z$-axis. (a) Probability of $S_n = +$ and of $S_n = -$? (b) The $S_n = +$ atoms alone go into a final $\hat{S}_z$ box. Probability of $S_z = +$ and of $S_z = -$? (c) What fraction of all atoms leaving the first box finish in $S_z = +$? (d) Without the middle box, what would the answer to (c) be? — **Hint:** Use Eq. 6.4 for the first box (prepared in a minus state), Eq. 6.3 for the last (prepared in $\lvert\psi_n^+\rangle$), with $\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{z} = \cos30^\circ = 0.8660$. Multiply along the path. — **Solution sketch:** (a) Eq. 6.4: $P(n{+}) = (1 - 0.8660)/2 = 0.0670$ and $P(n{-}) = (1+0.8660)/2 = 0.9330$; sum $1$. (b) Eq. 6.3: $P(z{+}) = (1+0.8660)/2 = 0.9330$ and $P(z{-}) = 0.0670$. (c) $0.0670\times0.9330 = 0.0625 = 1/16$ (exactly $(1-c)(1+c)/4 = (1-3/4)/4 = 1/16$). (d) Prepared in $z{-}$ and re-measured, Eq. 6.1 gives $P(z{+}) = 0$. The middle box makes a forbidden result happen $6.25\%$ of the time. Limiting check: at $\theta\to0$ the answer to (c) would tend to $0$.

7. Three magnets follow an unpolarised source, in this order: $\hat{S}_x$ (keep the $+$ beam only), $\hat{S}_x$ again (keep $+$ only), then $\hat{S}_z$, then a screen. (a) Does the second magnet change anything? (b) Which beams appear after the $\hat{S}_z$ magnet, and how do their sizes compare? (c) Now insert one more $\hat{S}_x$ magnet after the $\hat{S}_z$ magnet and take only its $S_z = +$ output into it. Which beams appear, and how do their sizes compare? — **Hint:** Same axis twice is Eq. 6.1; different axes is Eq. 6.2, 50/50. Track what state each magnet prepares. — **Solution sketch:** (a) The second $\hat{S}_x$ box passes the whole beam in $S_x = +$ and nothing in $S_x = -$ (Eq. 6.1 with $x$ in place of $z$); it does nothing new to this beam. (b) The $\hat{S}_z$ magnet gives two beams, $S_z = +$ and $S_z = -$, of equal size (Eq. 6.2, $1/2$ each), even though the beam was perfectly sharp in $S_x$. (c) The $S_z = +$ atoms are now in $\lvert\psi_z^+\rangle$, so the last $\hat{S}_x$ magnet gives two equal beams, $S_x = +$ and $S_x = -$. The earlier $S_x = +$ selection has been erased, which is the point of §6.3. Sanity check: at each different-axis step the two output beams are equal; at each same-axis step one beam is empty.

8. A spin-one-half state prepared in $S_z = +$ goes through a split-and-recombine device along a direction $\mathbf{n}$ in the $xz$-plane at $45^\circ$ to $z$, then a final $\hat{S}_z$ box. Use $\cos45^\circ = 0.7071$. (a) If the path is unobserved, what is $P(S_z = -)$, and which identity explains it? (b) If the path is recorded, what is $P(S_z = -)$? — **Hint:** (a) Add the two path amplitudes, then use completeness, $\lvert\psi_n^+\rangle\langle\psi_n^+\rvert+\lvert\psi_n^-\rangle\langle\psi_n^-\rvert=1$. (b) Add the two path *probabilities*: $P(n{\pm}\mid z{+})\,P(z{-}\mid n{\pm})$, using Eqs. 6.3 and 6.4 with $\cos45^\circ$. — **Solution sketch:** (a) $\sum_j\langle\psi_z^-\mid\psi_n^j\rangle\langle\psi_n^j\mid\psi_z^+\rangle = \langle\psi_z^-\mid\psi_z^+\rangle = 0$ by completeness and orthogonality, so $P(z{-}) = 0$. (b) Via $n{+}$: $\tfrac{1+0.7071}{2}\times\tfrac{1-0.7071}{2} = 0.8536\times0.1464 = 0.1250$. Via $n{-}$: $\tfrac{1-0.7071}{2}\times\tfrac{1+0.7071}{2} = 0.1464\times0.8536 = 0.1250$. Sum: $0.2500 = 1/4$ (exactly $2\times(1-\tfrac12)/4$). The difference between $0$ and $1/4$ is the interference term.

9. Predict the pattern: on a screen after a *single* $\hat{S}_z$ magnet, silver atoms from an oven show two spots. If the magnet is replaced by an identical one with its poles swapped (north below, south above), what changes? What happens if it is turned to make a field with **no** gradient? — **Hint:** Use $F_z = \mu_z\,\mathrm{d}B_z/\mathrm{d}z$ and think about what flips sign. — **Solution sketch:** Swapping the poles reverses the sign of $\mathrm{d}B_z/\mathrm{d}z$, so each atom is pushed the opposite way: the two spots are still there, but the labels ($S_z = +$ or $-$) and the up/down positions are interchanged. Zero gradient means zero force: a single unsplit spot, since nothing separates the two values of $\mu_z$.

10. Units check: (a) show that $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}$ has units of $\mathrm{J\,s}$ and say what that tells you about $\hbar/2$ as a spin value; (b) show that $\mu_B = e\hbar/2m_e$ has units $\mathrm{J/T}$. — **Hint:** (a) $\mathrm{m}\cdot\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$. (b) Coulomb is $\mathrm{A\,s}$ and $\mathrm{T} = \mathrm{kg\,s^{-2}A^{-1}}$. — **Solution sketch:** (a) $\mathrm{m\cdot kg\,m\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{kg\,m^2 s^{-1}} = (\mathrm{kg\,m^2 s^{-2}})\,\mathrm{s} = \mathrm{J\,s}$. Since $\hbar$ is also in $\mathrm{J\,s}$, $\hbar/2$ has the units of an angular momentum, which is why $S_z = \pm\hbar/2$ is the same kind of quantity as a classical $L$. (b) $\mathrm{C\cdot J\,s/kg} = \mathrm{A\,s\cdot kg\,m^2 s^{-1}/kg} = \mathrm{A\,m^2}$, and $\mathrm{J/T} = \mathrm{kg\,m^2 s^{-2}}/(\mathrm{kg\,s^{-2}A^{-1}}) = \mathrm{A\,m^2}$. The two agree.

## Anti-patterns / Common mistakes

- **Treating spin as a literal spinning ball.** A ball can be tilted to any angle, so the classical picture predicts the smear, not two spots. The lecture warns against the picture (§6.1).
- **Forgetting the gradient.** "Strong field, so big force" is wrong: $F_z = \mu_z\,\mathrm{d}B_z/\mathrm{d}z$ depends on the slope. Uniform field means a twist but no net force.
- **Assuming a second magnet "does nothing" to a beam that already came out of one.** It does nothing only if its axis is the *same*. Along a different axis it reshuffles the beam into two equal or unequal beams, and it can erase what the first magnet prepared.
- **Mixing the mass in the force law and in the moment formula.** The atom's mass is for $a = F/m$; the electron's mass is for $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \tfrac{gq}{2m}\mathbf{S}$.
