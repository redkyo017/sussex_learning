### Five steps to problem solving: How to gain marks
These are five recommended steps to problem solving to help you to gain maximum marks in this module, each of which are aimed to make your answers easy to read, and to demonstrate how well you understand the problem that you are trying to solve.
1. Read the question carefully.
Before you begin answering the question, make sure you understand what you are being asked to do.
The questions are hopefully clearly written. Don't jump to conclusions about what they might secretly be asking you to do, but instead answer which is being asked.
2. Prepare your answer.
If you are not certain how to begin answering, you can make notes to yourself to try to organise your thoughts. Some things which can be helpful to do are: 
a. Write a list of what you know, e.g., variables mentioned specifically in the question, or variables whose values you can infer (e.g., the probabilities of the outcomes of a uniformly random bit).
b. Write the definitions of the functions, matrices, or equations on computational basis states for the operations involved. — But don't write a matrix if it will be too large: think of whether there is a better way of writing the same thing, e.g., using ket notation.
c. Write to yourself, what method or algorithmic concept that you intend to use, and the formuas that involves. Explain the key elements, e.g., whether it is important that some vector is an eigenvector, whether a process produces a uniform superposition, and so on.
d. Work out your answer in advance — do the work that you need to do to find the answer, before you start writing it for others to see. This is to get past the pressure of getting right the first time, but it also gives you a chance to think later about how you might present those workings when it comes to submitting your work. 
Do these things for yourself — don't write all of these things in your submitted work, but do it to help you to prepare and to organise what you will submit. 
3. Explain your work.
This module is heavy with mathematics, but even mathematical work should be presented clearly, with descriptions of what is being presented or what the meaning of a variable is.
Credit is given for how well your work is explained. In those rare occasions where you do not need to write any supporting text, this will be made clear in the statement of the question.
4. Show your work.
Often, you will be expected to explain your answer by presenting calculations. This does not mean writing out multiplications of large matrices, but rather presenting your answer with enough detail that it is clear how you have obtained your answer.
Don’t write a matrix if it is going to be large: try to find the way to express an operation as simply as possible, that relates to the way that you will try to use it in your analysis. 
Credit is given for how well your work is demonstrated. On those rare occasions in which you do not need to write any supporting calculations, this will be made clear in the statement of the question.
5. Structure your work.
Any serious answer should be well-organised, to make it easy to understand and appreciate by others. This is for similar reasons why complete sentences are good to present with mathematics, but it also applies to making sure that the answers to each part of a question are clearly labeled, that arguments are presented in a logical sequence, and so forth.
Credit is given for how well your work is organised. On those occasions in which a correct analysis is so short that there is not any 'organisation' to speak of, the conciseness of your answer will be rewarded.
***Scientists and mathematicians are lazy people: do not make extra work for yourself. But at the same time, we work best together when we can clearly read and understand each other's work.***
