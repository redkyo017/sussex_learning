## 1.1 An introduction to quantum technologies

Since its inception in the early 20th century, quantum physics has continuously led to disruptive thoughts and inventions [\[1\]](#sect7). Even the protagonists of the scientific revolution that the arrival of quantum physics represented struggled with the radical implications their new conceptual framework posed. It was, in fact, that deep scepticism that led experimental scientists to test the implications of quantum physics more thoroughly in the laboratory than arguably is true for any other scientific theory. A possibly initially unexpected pay-out of the intensity of the laboratory research, with ever newer and more precise tools to prepare and measure quantum systems, has been the emergence of a whole new technology sector that combines dramatically new concepts of physics with superb experimental methodology.

Quantum technology today is an interdisciplinary field that leverages the principles of quantum mechanics to develop new technologies with a wide range of applications [\[2\]](#sect8). The counter-intuitive and peculiar nature of quantum mechanics enables these technologies to perform tasks that are impossible with classical technologies.

The field of quantum technologies is rapidly expanding and has the potential to revolutionise many areas of science and technology.

The key types of quantum technologies are quantum sensing, quantum communication, quantum simulation and quantum computing. We will explore each of these technologies in turn in the following sections, and then in greater depth in subsequent weeks of this module.

## 1.2 Quantum sensing

Quantum sensing uses the principles of quantum mechanics to measure physical quantities with unprecedented accuracy. Quantum sensors can measure many quantities including magnetic fields, electric fields and forces with high precision, providing new insights into physical systems [\[3\]](#sect9). This has applications in areas such as geology, medical imaging and materials science [\[4, 5, 6\]](#sect10), for example in non-invasively exploring the local underground tectonic composition, detecting neurophysiological activities of the human brain or quality control in conductive transparent materials used for solar cells. The arguably most advanced quantum technology devices are quantum sensors. They exploit the fact that quantum systems respond in well-defined ways to their environment, so that they can be used as a reliable probe.

The range of quantities that can be measured with quantum sensors is large. It spans time, magnetic and electric fields, inertial forces including gravity, light and non-visible electromagnetic radiation, material properties and many others. All or at least most of these quantities can also be measured with conventional tools, such as clocks, compasses, springs and microscopes. Quantum sensors can outperform their classical counterparts in many respects, including key performance parameters, usage range and practicality. The specific advantages of quantum sensing depend on the quantity in question, the type of quantum sensor that is used and the requirements of the application it is used for. The content covered in Weeks 2 and 3 of this module is dedicated to quantum sensing, specifically: magnetic field measurements, clocks, inertial sensors for gravity measurements and navigation. Additionally, we will explore specific cases to develop a good understanding of where and how quantum solutions are meaningful technological developments.

## 1.3 Quantum communication

Quantum communication is a promising application of quantum technologies that provides secure communication channels [\[7\]](#sect11). It is based on the concept of quantum key distribution, where two parties can generate a shared secret key that is guaranteed to be secure against any eavesdropping attempts. Any attempt to intercept the key would disturb the quantum state of the system, which can be detected by the legitimate parties. This provides a high level of security, even against advanced eavesdropping techniques.

With ever increasing volumes of stored, processed and transmitted information, secure communication is of paramount interest across many sectors of business and public life. Consequently, a lot of effort has always been made to encrypt sensitive messages in ways that mean intercepting messages is difficult. Quantum physics enables a step change in the technology to do this, in that it provides tools that prevent unnoticed eavesdropping on a communication line on fundamental grounds.

The possibilities afforded by quantum information encoding, encryption and transfer are rooted in the fundamental Quantum No-Cloning theorem that states that identical copies of a physical system in a specific quantum state cannot be made without alteration of the initial state [\[8\]](#sect12). This means that even if a message or an encryption key was secretly intercepted on a communication channel, reading the message or key and sending it on to the legitimate recipient is not possible.

With the rapid growth of digital information exchange and storage, the need for secure communication has become more important than ever. As an example of a failure of classical encryption, the 2017 Equifax data breach is a well-known instance. In this breach, hackers were able to gain access to sensitive personal information of millions of individuals by exploiting a vulnerability in a web application. The stolen data included names, Social Security numbers, birth dates, and other sensitive information, all of which were presumably encrypted using classical encryption techniques. The Equifax breach underscores the need for stronger, more secure methods of encryption, such as quantum cryptography [\[9\]](#sect13).

Traditional encryption methods rely on mathematical algorithms to scramble information into a format that can only be decoded with a secret key. However, these methods have limitations as there is always the possibility that the key can be stolen or the algorithm can be broken.

Quantum technology, on the other hand, offers a new approach to secure communication that is based on the principles of quantum mechanics. The fundamental properties of quantum mechanics, such as superposition and entanglement, can be harnessed to create encryption protocols that are impossible to break. These principles are related to the fact that in quantum physics, particles are also waves and waves are also particles (wave-particle duality). Particles/waves of one type are also fundamentally indistinguishable. Together, these peculiar properties lead to the possibility of a single particle simultaneously being in two states (superposition) and multiple particles being in joint correlated states that cannot be described as a combination of individual particle states (entanglement).

One of the key features of quantum encryption is that it relies on the fact that the act of measuring a quantum system disturbs it, meaning that any attempt to intercept a message will be immediately noticed. This is known as the 'no-cloning' theorem, which states that it is impossible to make identical copies of a quantum system without altering its initial state.

The most widely known example of quantum encryption is known as Quantum Key Distribution (QKD). In QKD, two parties use entangled particles, such as photons, to create a shared secret key that can be used to encrypt and decrypt messages. Any attempt to intercept the photons during transmission will change their state, alerting the legitimate users to the presence of an eavesdropper.

While the technology is still in its infancy and there are many challenges to overcome, quantum encryption offers the potential for truly secure communication that is not possible with classical methods. As such, it has the potential to transform many sectors of business and public life, including finance, healthcare and national security.

## 1.4 Quantum simulation

Quantum simulation is a rapidly growing field that uses quantum technologies to simulate complex quantum systems that are difficult to study with classical methods. Quantum simulators can model the behaviour of molecules, materials, and even entire quantum systems, providing new insights into the behaviour of these systems [\[10\]](#sect14). Simulations are widespread tools in science, engineering and technology. A physical process, often of considerable complexity, is mapped onto a controllable situation. Most common are numerical simulations run on powerful computers or computer clusters. The benefit is that the physical representation of the process of interest can then be implemented on a very well-known system based on the large degree of control of classical information processing in electronic computing devices. Underlying physical phenomena and potential occurrences can be switched on and off to analyse their impact on the ‘real’ process in detail. The cost in resources and time is much reduced in a computer test-run over the actual system.

This classical and very successful process fails when quantum phenomena increase the complexity to a point where they are not just an effect to consider but matter for the core functionality of the system, as is the case across the spectrum of quantum technologies, from sensors to communication devices and computers. Furthermore, there is considerable interest in quantum simulation in areas that are also under investigation with classical laboratory and computing tools. Those areas span broad areas, ranging from fundamental science to applied technology with immediate industrial and commercial interest. Examples include cases as diverse as an improved understanding of the formation of matter in the early universe, a better understanding of superconducting materials at high temperatures with the potential to result in lossless long-distance power lines, and targeted drug design with substantial relevance for the pharmaceutical industry [\[11\]](#sect15).

In some ways, functional quantum simulators can be viewed as versions of quantum computers, with the distinct difference that, in contrast to universal quantum computers, quantum simulators are designed to solve specific scientific or technological problems or classes of such problems.

## 1.5 Quantum computing

Quantum computing is another promising application of quantum technologies that has the potential to solve problems that are currently intractable with classical computers, such as factoring large numbers and other problems whose computational effort grows so rapidly with input size that the solving time becomes too large. Quantum computers are able to perform multiple calculations simultaneously, thanks to the superposition of quantum states, which provides a significant speed-up over classical computers for some applications. The development of practical quantum computers is still in its early stages, but progress has been made in recent years, with several companies and research groups building small-scale quantum computers [\[12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19\]](#sect16). The journey from the first room-filling computers less than a century ago to modern super-computing farms is an incredible success story, in which the devices have become ever more powerful in their quantitative and qualitative capabilities in ways that were never imagined in the beginning [\[20\]](#sect17). The driver of this development has been a steady course to increasing density of electronic elements on chips and circuit boards. This is based on an empirical exponential law of decreasing structure sizes with a halving time of only a few years, often dubbed as Moore’s Law [\[21\]](#sect18). On such a pathway, it is natural to extrapolate to the size scales of the constituent most basic electronic elements. One finds that the regime governed by quantum physics is inevitably reached very soon if Moore’s Law continues to hold as it has done for decades already.

The realisation that quantum phenomena can be exploited rather than having to be avoided in computing is an ingenious concept that forms the basis of quantum computing. The core idea is that in quantum systems, binary encoding of information in bits can now occur in so called quantum bits or qubits [\[22\]](#sect19) for short. While a bit is either on or off, set or not set, zero or one, a qubit can be in (infinitely) many states that contain both basis states to a varying degree. This is known as superposition. As a second key novelty in quantum computing, two qubits can be linked to each other in a peculiar way that is only possible in quantum physics, known as entanglement. These two properties, superposition and entanglement, together with a basic set of operations performed on one or two qubits, so called quantum gates, are the fundamental ingredients of universal quantum computing.

The interest in quantum computing started to grow quickly when its potential power became apparent through quantum algorithms that could exclusively be run on a quantum computer and would make it possible to solve tasks that a classical computer could not solve in a reasonable time. Key early examples concern database searches and cracking of encryption codes [\[23, 24\]](#sect20). Quantum computing is now expected to have an impact in large sectors across technologies ranging from the pharmaceutical industry to artificial intelligence.

Despite tremendous advances, the physical implementation of quantum computers remains difficult due to the fragility of quantum bits and the fidelity of operations on them. Quantum error correction will be a central tool, which enables implementation of quantum algorithms with inevitably imperfect implementations, albeit with a large overhead of physical qubits needed in order to implement a smaller set of logical qubits on an actual quantum computer.

## 1.6 Capabilities and limitations of quantum technology

When it comes to learning about quantum technology, it is important to gain an understanding of the various capabilities and limitations of each type of technology.

**Capabilities of quantum technology**

-   **Quantum computing:** This has the potential to solve problems that are currently intractable with classical computers, such as factoring large numbers and simulating complex systems. Quantum computers are able to leverage the principles of quantum mechanics, such as superposition and entanglement, to perform calculations much faster than classical computers.
-   **Quantum communication:** This can provide an unprecedented level of security for transmitting information. Quantum cryptography, such as quantum key distribution, uses the properties of quantum mechanics to ensure that any interception or eavesdropping on the communication can be detected.
-   **Quantum sensing:** This can measure physical properties with unparalleled accuracy. Quantum sensors are able to detect tiny changes in magnetic or electric fields, and can be used in a wide range of applications, such as medical diagnostics and geological surveying.

**Limitations of quantum technology**

-   **Quantum computers:** They cannot solve all problems faster than classical computers. There are some problems that do not have a known quantum algorithm, and for which quantum computers would not provide any speedup.
-   **Quantum communication:** This cannot completely eliminate the possibility of interception. While quantum cryptography can detect any interception, it cannot prevent it entirely. There are still ways in which an eavesdropper can interfere with the communication.
-   **Quantum sensing:** As quantum sensor development continues, challenges around the sensitivity to environmental noise, the fragility of quantum state preparation, evolution and readout, as well as issues around scalability, complex data interpretation, restricted measurement range and, in some cases sensing speed, need to be tackled.

In conclusion, quantum technologies have the potential to transform many fields, but they also have some limitations. As the field continues to develop, it is important to understand both the potential and the limitations of quantum technologies in order to make the most of their capabilities.
