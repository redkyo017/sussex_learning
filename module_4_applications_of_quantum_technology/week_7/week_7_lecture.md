### Introduction

Over the previous weeks of this module, you have covered the main types of quantum technology that are currently being developed around the world. It is apparent that each case has a multitude of application opportunities, many of which have the potential for commercial exploitation. However, there are substantial differences in the state of development of the various types of technology and their adaptation to meet the needs of the respective application sectors. This is typically characterised by the technology readiness level (TRL), where the scale starts with 1 for early proof-of-principle research and extends to 9 for a fully mature commercial product on the market (see Table [7.1](#tab7.1) for definitions of each level according to the commonly used scheme introduced by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)).

Table 7.1: Technology readiness level (TRL) definitions. Adapted from Tzinis, I. 2021 [\[1\]](#sect7).
| TRL | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| TRL 1 | Basic principles observed and reported. |
| TRL 2 | Technology concept and/or application formulated. |
| TRL 3 | Analytical and experimental critical function and/or characteristic proof-of-concept. |
| TRL 4 | Component and/or breadboard validation in laboratory environment. |
| TRL 5 | Component and/or breadboard validation in relevant environment. |
| TRL 6 | System/subsystem model or prototype demonstration in a relevant environment. |
| TRL 7 | System prototype demonstration in an operational environment. |
| TRL 8 | Actual system completed and qualified through test and demonstration. |
| TRL 9 | Actual system proven through successful mission operations. |

The TRL for each technology and component is different and often is subject to interpretation. For instance, years ago a company sold a system to a Swiss bank. Since this commercial sale is evidence for TRL 9, one could argue that this would be classified as TRL 9. However, the system is not routinely used because it doesn’t fulfil all the requirements of the market. Specifically, it does not fully guaranteed security advantage over conventional systems and is too slow – so clearly it is still at the field test level. Thus, one could classify this as TRL 6 or 7. Any numbers quoted here or elsewhere should therefore be read as a guideline rather than factual quantitative information [\[11\]](#sect71). Typically, the constituent components of a quantum device are (or need to be) developed to a much higher TRL before they can be integrated into a functional device.

In the following section, we will explore each of the previously introduced branches of quantum technologies in the context of their individual states of development to facilitate comparisons between them. This section is meant to illustrate how the different technologies vary greatly in their technical maturity and to provide a broad picture of what it takes for a new technology to climb the TRL ladder from conception to full practical and commercial deployment. Each step of that journey is intellectually exciting and can offer academic and economic opportunities well before TRL 9 is reached.

This week, we will summarise the types of quantum technologies we covered over the previous weeks. We will compare the various states of development of these quantum technologies and look at the challenges and barriers within each type. Additionally, we will discuss some of the early successes of quantum technologies.

## 7.1 State of development of various types of quantum technologies

In this section, we will explore the state of development for each of the quantum technologies discussed in the previous weeks and compare the state of development for each.

**Atomic clocks**

Chip-scale atomic clocks (CSACs) became commercially available in 2011 after more than two decades of continuous support from the USA Defence Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) [\[12\]](#sect72). CSACs find applications in battery-operated backpack equipment used in the defence sector, such as man-portable military radios, jammers and global navigation satellite system (GNSS) receivers. More recently, the commercialisation of radiation-hardened devices has opened up possibilities for deploying CSACs in space, including synchronising constellations of low Earth orbit (LEO) nano- or micro-satellites [\[13\]](#sect73). CSACs utilise microwave transitions in warm vapours of alkali metals and leverage a phenomenon called coherent population trapping (CPT), which eliminates the need for a microwave cavity and results in a compact size, weight and power (SWaP) footprint. However, CSACs have relatively poor long-term stability, which limits their use as primary frequency standards. Additionally, they are more expensive compared with conventional compact rubidium clocks. As a result, CSACs are primarily used in applications that require extremely small, lightweight and battery-operated devices.

To address the limitations of current-generation CSACs, DARPA initiated a targeted effort to develop next-generation CSACs (NG-CSACs) with significantly improved long-term stability by approximately three orders of magnitude [\[14\]](#sect74). NG-CSACs must rely on different physical phenomena since technological improvements alone cannot achieve the desired performance level. Various physical platforms are being explored, each at different levels of maturity. One approach, that has been the subject of research for over 30 years, involves miniaturising mercury-ion clocks and utilising microwave transitions in electromagnetically confined ions [\[15\]](#sect75). Another approach employs laser-cooled atoms to exploit the same CPT effect as used in current-generation devices, thereby mitigating the long-term instabilities of atomic cells. The most challenging efforts involve utilising optical transitions in warm atomic or molecular vapours, which suppresses Doppler broadening through nonlinear optical phenomena such as two-photon absorption or coherent modulation transfer. The difficulty here is that while more practical and in principle allowing for better precision through larger atom numbers, warm vapours suffer the problem of random movement of the atoms in the vapour. This leads to the so-called Doppler broadening, or less precise spectroscopy. A workaround is to not use standard atomic transitions to probe the spectroscopy, but to build on more complex phenomena involving multiple phontons and nonlinear optical effects. Essentially, this means more demands on a laser system in terms of control of power and frequency.

A survey of the available literature reveals the TRLs achieved by these research lines and notes that the USA is leading in the development of these technologies. In particular, a chip-scale clock derived from the miniaturisation of the mercury-ion Deep Space Atomic Clock, which was launched into space in 2019 [\[16\]](#sect76), could reach the fully integrated prototype stage within the next five years. However, despite its exceptional research capabilities, Europe currently lacks a focused and coordinated effort in NG-CSAC development [\[17\]](#sect77). This puts Europe, including the UK, at risk of relying on imported devices, which is similar to the situation with CSACs, despite having developed promising prototypes. The industrial production and commercialisation of CSACs involve significant investment risks due to the high cost of technologies and the specificities of the defence market.

**Magnetometry**

Quantum magnetometry techniques have reached various TRLs in their development and application. As an overview, here are some current estimates on the TRL that has been reached for some key ones.

1.  **Optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs):** Optically pumped (or atomic) magnetometers have reached relatively high TRLs and have been deployed in practical applications. They have been extensively used in geophysical surveys [\[18\]](#sect78), medical imaging [\[19\]](#sect79) and navigation systems [\[20\]](#sect720). Some atomic magnetometers have achieved TRL 9, which represents their successful deployment and proven performance in real-world environments. At the same time, there is substantial ongoing work in improved schemes with higher sensitivity, higher bandwidth, larger dynamic range and/or lower noise levels. There is also significant effort in forming multi-sensor arrays for magnetic imaging. Commercial prototypes at TRL 8 on the basis of assemblies of individual commercial sensors exist, but fully scalable solutions are still at TRL 2–3, with a higher TRL at the constituent component level.
 2.  **Nitrogen vacancy centres in diamond:** The use of nitrogen vacancy (NV) centres in diamond for magnetometry has shown promising results, particularly in nanoscale magnetic field sensing. This is the key advantage of the technology, because the position of a colour (NV) centre is very precisely known and leads to the method having a high spatial resolution, especially in near-field microscopy devices. While some NV-based magnetometers have been used in research and laboratory settings, they may still be in the mid-to-high TRL range (TRL 4–7). Further advancements in fabrication techniques, scalability and integration into practical devices are needed to increase their TRL, but fully operational commercial devices could be in use within a few years [\[21\]](#sect721).
 3.  **Superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs):** SQUIDs have been extensively developed and commercialised for magnetic field measurement. They have achieved high TRLs and are widely used in scientific research, medical diagnostics and industrial applications. SQUID magnetometers have reached TRL 9, which indicates their successful deployment and integration into various measurement systems. Concurrently, there are still substantial developments leading to improvements in the technology at lower TRLs, for example, reducing noise levels into the $100~\text{aT}/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ range, which will increase spatial resolution in multi-SQUID systems [\[2\]](#sect8).
 4.  **Quantum dots:** Quantum dot-based magnetometers are still in the early stages of development, and their TRLs may vary depending on specific implementations and applications. While significant progress has been made in understanding the fundamental principles and experimental demonstrations, practical deployment and commercialisation are still ongoing. Quantum dot magnetometers tend to be in the lower to mid-range TRLs (TRL 2–5) and require further research and engineering efforts to increase their TRLs [\[3\]](#sect9).

It is important to note that the TRLs mentioned above are approximate and can vary based on the specific implementation, performance criteria and targeted application. Quantum magnetometry, as a whole, is a dynamic and evolving field with ongoing research and development efforts aimed at pushing the boundaries of sensitivity, precision and practicality. Advancements in materials, device fabrication and system integration are likely to contribute to the advancement of quantum magnetometry and increase the TRLs of various techniques in the future.

**Quantum communication**

Quantum communication has made significant progress since the first laboratory demonstrations a few decades ago, but is still primarily in the research and development phase. Here are some key aspects of the state of the art and current technical development in quantum communication.

1.  **Quantum key distribution (QKD):** Quantum key distribution allows secure key establishment between two parties by exploiting the principles of quantum mechanics. It provides a means to share cryptographic keys with unconditional security based on the laws of physics. QKD systems have achieved key distribution rates ranging from a few kilobits per second (Kbps) to over 1 gigabit per second (Gbps) over various communication channels, including fibre-optic cables and free-space links [\[22\]](#sect722).
 2.  **Quantum cryptography protocols:** In addition to QKD, new quantum cryptography protocols have been devised such as quantum coin flipping, quantum oblivious transfer and quantum secret sharing. These protocols aim to address different aspects of secure communication and have demonstrated successful implementations with high fidelity and security.
 3.  **Quantum repeaters:** Quantum repeaters are essential for long-distance quantum communication as they extend the range over which quantum states can be transmitted without significant degradation. Various approaches to building quantum repeaters exist, and recent experiments have achieved entanglement distribution over distances exceeding 1,000 kilometres.
 4.  **Quantum satellite communication:** Quantum satellite communication involves the use of satellites to transmit quantum information between ground stations. Satellites, such as the Chinese satellite Micius [\[23\]](#sect723), have demonstrated successful transmission of entangled photons over distances of more than 1,200 kilometres. These experiments have achieved high-fidelity entanglement distribution and hold promise for secure global communication networks.
 5.  **Quantum teleportation:** Quantum teleportation allows the transfer of quantum states from one location to another using entanglement. While practical applications of quantum teleportation are still in their infancy, recent experiments have achieved teleportation of quantum states with high fidelity and long-distance teleportation over tens of kilometres [\[24\]](#sect724).
 6.  **Quantum network architectures:** Developments of scalable quantum network architectures are underway with the goal of integrating quantum devices, quantum repeaters and quantum memories into a unified framework. These architectures aim to enable efficient and reliable quantum communication networks capable of supporting a wide range of quantum applications. Recent advancements include the development of hybrid quantum networks and the integration of quantum communication with classical communication infrastructure.
 7.  **Quantum communication and computing integration:** There is a growing focus on integrating quantum communication with quantum computing systems. This integration aims to facilitate secure communication between quantum computers and to harness the power of distributed quantum computing. Recent developments include the demonstration of quantum communication protocols for quantum cloud computing and the exploration of quantum networking techniques to enable distributed quantum computing architectures.

It is important to note that quantum communication technologies are still in the early stages of development (TRL <7), and several challenges need to be overcome before widespread deployment occurs. These challenges include improving the efficiency and reliability of quantum systems, mitigating noise and errors, developing practical quantum memories and scaling up the systems to support large-scale networks. However, the progress made so far in quantum communication research provides a strong foundation for future advancements and the realisation of secure and efficient quantum communication networks.

**Quantum simulation**

In recent years, quantum simulators have made significant advancements in terms of technical development and a better understanding of applications and potential use cases. However, in terms of progress towards devices that are usable in a practical industrial and/or commercial context, the state of the art is still that of scientific tools with applicability in the academic context. The TRL at the systems level thus remains low (1–2), even though components have matured to much higher levels. Here are some key highlights from the current state of the art in quantum simulators [\[4\]](#sect10).

1.  **Platforms:** Various physical platforms have been used for quantum simulation, such as trapped ions, superconducting qubits, cold atoms and photonic systems. Each platform offers unique advantages and challenges, and researchers have been working on optimising their respective technologies and expanding capabilities.
 2.  **Scalability:** Quantum simulators have seen improvements in scalability, which enables larger and more complex simulations. This includes increasing the number of qubits or particles that can be controlled and manipulated within the simulator. For example, trapped-ion systems have demonstrated the ability to control and simulate a relatively large number of ions in a single system [\[25\]](#sect725).
 3.  **Control and manipulation:** Quantum simulators have seen advancements in the control and manipulation of quantum states. This includes improvements in the fidelity and coherence of qubits or particles, as well as the ability to perform precise operations and measurements on the simulated system.
 4.  **Error correction and noise mitigation:** Noise and errors are inherent challenges in quantum systems. Researchers have been exploring error correction techniques and noise mitigation strategies to improve the accuracy and reliability of quantum simulations. This includes the development of error-correcting codes, quantum error correction protocols and error mitigation algorithms.
 5.  **Applications:** Quantum simulators have been used to tackle a wide range of scientific problems across various disciplines, including condensed matter physics, chemistry, material science and optimisation. They have been employed to simulate the behaviour of quantum systems that are difficult to study using classical computers, thereby providing insights into fundamental physics and aiding in the development of new materials and drugs.

A comprehensive summary not only of the state of the art, but also of the barriers and challenges for the various platforms and the theoretical framework, with a long-term outlook over the coming decades, has been provided in Fraxanet, Salamon and Lewenstein, 2022 [\[4\]](#sect10).

**Quantum computing**

There is some overlap between the current state of development in quantum computing and that of quantum simulation. The main difference is, however, that quantum simulators are built to handle specific problems or, at least, a specific class of problems, whereas quantum computers are intended for universal use, just like classical computers are universal and are not necessarily built for specific tasks only. Although, in principle, the concept of a universal quantum computer is not too complex and can, in fact, rely on a combination of single and two-qubit quantum gates, the actual implementation with a true gain over conventional computing is still a bold vision rather than an imminent reality. With the intensity of reporting on quantum computing, often falsely equated with quantum technologies as a whole, it is often difficult to distinguish, on the one hand, between the actual state of the technology and its current capability and, on the other hand, promises, hopes and future opportunities.

A criterion that is often used as a milestone of success, proper technology advance and, ultimately, market viability is the ability of a quantum technology to perform a **relevant** task that cannot be fulfilled (with reasonable effort) by any (known) conventional technology. This ability has been referred to as **quantum advantage** [\[5\]](#sect11). Although, in the areas of quantum clocks and sensors, the quantum advantage has been clearly and unambiguously demonstrated in numerous and impressive ways by large margins, this has not been clearly shown in quantum computing to date. There has, however, been significant progress towards that goal, for example, by calculating the properties of complex many-body quantum systems with relevance in physics and chemistry research [\[6\] \[6\]](#sect12) in ways that today's classical computers could not achieve. It is not by coincidence that this achievement could be classed as a quantum simulation rather than a quantum computing solution, as specific quantum simulators are more advanced than today's quantum computers. It should be noted, though, that the mechanism here is reported as quantum computing rather than a problem-specific simulation. In general, such results are obtained through large-scale efforts in academic laboratories and the research departments of some of the world's largest and most powerful technology companies, including Google, IBM and Microsoft. There are also specific quantum computing start-ups and more established companies, such as the Canadian enterprise D-Wave Systems, which have reported to have built quantum computers with several thousand qubits . These numbers, while still much smaller than the number of classical bits in any basic classical computer, are significant for several reasons.

1.  A classical computer emulating the operation of a quantum computer requires a substantial increase in memory and processing power even for a small number of qubits. More importantly, the requirements on these resources grow exponentially with the number of qubits, so that any operation at a level of only tens of qubits stops being feasible.
 2.  The number of qubits available on multiple different quantum computing platforms has grown significantly in recent years, so that an extrapolation into the future looks positive under the assumption of a continued rate of increase in available qubit numbers.
 3.  The number of qubits needed for error correction or error mitigation protocols is decreasing with new algorithms and higher available qubit preparation and processing fidelities.

Nevertheless, it is important to note that the quoted qubit numbers typically refer to the **total** number of **physical** qubits in a system, while the number of **logical** qubits available for use in quantum algorithms is substantially smaller due to the still large qubit overhead required for inevitable error-correction procedures.

Overall, the key messages regarding the state of development of quantum computing, which is arguably the most visible of quantum technologies, are the following.

-   Unprecedented large investments (financially and through human resources) have allowed academic centres and corporate players to increase the capacity of functional quantum processors to the point where, in niche science problems, conventional computing can be outperformed.
-   Clear evidence of existing quantum computers providing a quantum advantage in an industrially relevant problem is still missing.
-   Implementing quantum computers continues to be pursued on multiple different physical platforms with different advantages and disadvantages without a clear 'winner' to date.

## 7.2 Current challenges and barriers

Some of the key barriers of each of the quantum technologies described in this module have been covered by the content of the previous weeks and by the text above. The following summary outlines the types of challenges that are or will need to be addressed in quantum technologies on their path from initial idea and general demonstration of relevant phenomena to devices of practical usability and exploitation of the economic potential.

**Technological developments**

The quantum technologies under current consideration, including those presented in this module, have all been proved to be feasible in principle. As the TRL is increased, in each case, numerous technological improvements are required. This concerns usability of the technology under a set of conditions defined by each application. For example, for magnetometry of biomedical signal, the sensors must be compatible with a biological environment in terms of temperature, geometry and other specifics. A quantum computer may require platform-specific infrastructure in terms of cryogenic cooling, which may be amenable to a remote subscription cloud-based service, but not to local on-site operation in an office environment. In addition to this type of technological application-adaptation need, the performance of quantum devices is continuously increasing in order to realise, and at later stages also maximise, the full potential of each technology. As the TRL of the devices is increased, the technological development demands a shift from performance improvements typical of academic laboratory research to more industrial needs such as robustness, and reduced size, weight, power and cost (SWAP-C). Among the technologies that we have discussed, you can see that the still low-TRL quantum computing at one end of the scale is very much concerned with making the technology work to realise and demonstrate a quantum advantage. On the other end of the scale, for example, in clocks or magnetometers, the TRL is already high, commercial devices are sold at a profit and the focus of technology development is on specific applications to meet sector needs.

**Proof of relevance and practicality**

When new technologies become relevant for ‘real-world’ application, it is natural to face a combination of market or application pull and technology push. Traditionally, quantum technologies are dominated by the latter, where, for decades, a curiosity-driven desire to prepare, control and measure quantum systems with an ever increasing precision has been an important driver in the laboratories. As a result, the opportunities we see today across multiple quantum technologies have emerged with their new capabilities in measuring, communicating, simulating and computing. However, it is not always obvious how to turn these capabilities into a quantum advantage, that is, an improvement with sufficient relevance in the application context to economically justify a disruptive technology change (or the introduction of a new technology altogether). To this end, it is important to demonstrate such an advantage in a relevant context to the point where a pathway to near-term viability, and clear benefit despite the disruption, becomes credible and can be clearly cast into a robust technology development and deployment road map. An example from quantum computing is the fierce effort on all investigated platforms to devise new algorithms and to increase the number of usable logical qubits and their processing to the point where a true quantum advantage can be demonstrated. IBM's 100×100 challenge [\[7\]](#sect13) illustrates this quest and its importance in a playful way. Here, a user community is challenged to provide a relevant problem that can be solved with quantum computing resources that are believed to be available in the not-too-distant future.

**Use cases**

One of the most critical parts on the pathway to maturing a new technology to market is to find early-stage **use cases**. These are specific, ideally contained-scope, but relevant, aspects in an application sector. They play a pioneering role and enable the characteristics of the new technology to be tested and, in particular, allow its benefits to become directly visible. Such use cases serve the following purposes.

-   They promote the case for the disruptive technology by practically demonstrating the main advantages.
-   They provide early iterative feedback between technology development and application needs.
-   They find (initial) measures of performance potential and limitations in an application context.
-   They set the groundwork for standardisation and calibration in objectifiable ways for comparability of different versions (platforms) of the new technology or between the incoming quantum technology and its incumbent conventional counterpart.

An example of an important use case in quantum technologies is magnetoencephalography (MEG) conducted with optically pumped magnetometers (MEGs) [\[8\]](#sect14), where both performance increase (signal-to-noise improvement and better source localisation of neurological activity inside the human brain) and application-specific practicality (the ability of participants to move during measurements) have been demonstrated [\[9\]](#sect15). The corresponding experiments and demonstrations have led to increased efforts towards devising whole-head MEG systems based on optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs), which represents a shift of emphasis due to the success of the use case investigations and also to the fact that they have exposed the shortcomings of the current technology.

**Validation of usability**

Once the quantum advantage and wider relevance of a quantum technology has been demonstrated in general and through specific use cases, a next key step is to validate the technology in a wider context. This is necessary to gain a clear understanding of whether the technology is robust in routine operation and when used with expanded scope relative to the early-stage demonstration use cases. Also at this stage, iterative feedback between technological and application aspects remains important. Additionally, there are no directly technological aspects to consider now, such as:

-   regulatory and legal concerns of wider implementation
-   standardisation, calibration and bench marking for the technology
-   documentation and user interface definitions.

There are several different methods that can be used for validation of quantum technologies. Examples that are currently underway include multi-patient studies in schizophrenia using MEG with OPMs and through a quantum simulation subscription service [\[10\]](#sect16).

**Scalable and robust production**

As discussed above, the TRL is often much higher for the constituent components of a (complex) quantum device than for the device itself. This has led to a significant emphasis on **enabling technologies** in the quantum technologies community and a modular approach to designing and assembling the full device systems. One advantage of a modular approach is flexibility in design, because improved component technology enables the replacement of first-prototype components without affecting the entire system. Another important advantage is that, from an industrial point of view, investing in component technology with a high TRL has a lower risk than investing in low-TRL technology as there will be a higher demand for the improved devices that result from it. From the research and technology point of view, at the systems level, the process is de-risked because it provides reliability, robustness and warranties that are only possible in commercialised products. The modularity of component and systems technology is, furthermore, the basis of creating the robust supply chains that are necessary to scale up any new technology to a wider use rollout.
