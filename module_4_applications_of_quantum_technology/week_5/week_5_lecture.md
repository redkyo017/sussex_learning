This week you will learn about quantum simulators. Simulations are widespread tools in science, engineering and technology. This is a physical process, often of considerable complexity, and is mapped onto a controllable situation. The most common are numerical simulations that run on powerful computers or computer clusters. The benefit is that the physical representation of the process of interest can be implemented on a very well-known system based on the large degree of control of classical information processing in electronic computing devices. Underlying physical phenomena and potential occurrences can be switched on and off to analyse their impact on the ‘real’ process in detail. The cost in resources and time is much reduced in a computer test-run over the actual system. In some ways, functional quantum simulators can be viewed as versions of quantum computers, with the distinct difference being that, in contrast with universal quantum computers, quantum simulators are designed to solve specific scientific or technological problems.

## 5.1 What is quantum simulation and why do we need it?

[BACK TO TOP ![](https://canvas.sussex.ac.uk/courses/42093/files/6842212/preview)](#sect1.1) 

Depending on the system being investigated, we use the appropriate laws of physics to provide our description. For example, in classical mechanics, the dynamics of the system evolves according to Newton’s laws of motion, whereas an electromagnetic field is well described using Maxwell’s equations [\[4\]](#sect9). Similarly, a system of microscopic subatomic particles and molecules must be described by quantum mechanics, with the dynamics governed by the famous Schrödinger equation [\[5\]](#sect10). The state of a quantum system, such as an electron or a collection of atoms, can be described by a wavefunction ∣ψ⟩. In quantum mechanics, we do not have certainty about the state of the system, and so the wavefunction represents a probability amplitude, which tells us the probability that we would find the system in a particular state if we were to make a measurement. The wavefunction evolves in time according to the Schrodinger equation (Eq. (5.1))

$$
i\hbar \frac{\textrm{d}\psi}{\textrm{d}t} = H \psi,
$$
(5.1) 

where H is called the Hamiltonian of the system, and is the quantity that controls how the dynamics will proceed. For example, the Hamiltonian might describe how the particles in the system interact with each other. If the Hamiltonian does not depend on time, but is a constant, then the solution is simply

$$
\mid\psi(t)\rangle = e^{ -i Ht/\hbar } \mid\psi(0)\rangle,
$$
(5.2) 

where $\mid\psi(0)\rangle$ is the initial state. This exponential factor is called the unitary operator. While this may seem a simple enough equation to simulate, it can actually become very complex very quickly. Let’s take a simple example to illustrate this.

Think about a system of three particles which have the fundamental property of spin, which can be in one of two states – either ‘up’ or ‘down’ – and could represent the alignment of the magnetic dipole moments of the particles. To simulate this system quantum mechanically, we must be able to write down the state of the system. The possible states at any time are shown in Table [5.1](#tab5.1).

Table 5.1: The possible states at any time in quantum simulation.
| Particle 1 Spin | Particle 2 Spin | Particle 3 Spin | Probability amplitude |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Up | Up | Up | $\psi_1$ |
| Up | Up | Down | $\psi_2$ |

| Up | Down | Down |  $\psi_4$ |
| Down | Up | Up | $\psi_5$ |
| Down | Up | Down | $\psi_6$ |
| Down | Down | Up | $\psi_7$ |
| Down | Down | Down | $\psi_8$ |

 

In contrast with a classical system where only one definite configuration exists at each time, in a quantum system we have a superposition of all possible states. In this example, we can see that we need eight numbers (which are complex amplitudes) to describe the entire quantum state at each moment in time. Also, take note here that we are only considering that the particles have the spin property, but, in a more complex system, they could have additional properties, such as different possible positions or momenta. To store the state of the system in a regular computer’s RAM throughout our simulation, we need eight complex numbers. A single precision value occupies 32 bits of memory, and so a single precision complex value takes 64 bits = 8 bytes (considering each of the real and imaginary parts). So our three-particle quantum system requires 8 bytes × 8 states = 64 bytes to represent its state at each time. This is no problem, but let’s examine the memory requirements as the number of particles that we are studying increases, as shown in Table [5.2](#tab5.2).

We see that simulating a full quantum system quickly becomes intractable, and conventional computers are able to simulate quantum systems with up to a few tens of particles only. Indeed, this is only to **store** the state in memory – the task of calculating the time evolution must be factored in as well. This is not very useful for studying a material whose properties depend, for example, on its many electrons, or perhaps a chemical reaction with many molecules in quantum chemistry. Some problems are poorly understood because they are governed by complex quantum dynamics, and if the collective behaviour depends on hundreds or more quantum particles, then a different way to simulate it is needed. This is the reason why quantum simulators are pursued for more complex systems.

In a classical computer, the information for the calculation is held in the computer's RAM, and is stored on millions of tiny capacitors – a capacitor that is fully charged represents a ‘1’, whereas one that is empty represents a ‘0’. However, in 1982, Richard Feynman discussed the idea of quantum simulators and quantum computers, when he wrote ‘Let the computer itself be built of quantum mechanical elements which obey quantum mechanical laws’ [\[6\]](#sect11). The idea of the quantum computer and quantum simulator is to construct a machine that employs real quantum mechanical particles instead, such as atoms, ions or molecules, and to encode the stored states in the individual spins of these particles. Because in quantum mechanics particles can be in multiple states simultaneously (a phenomenon known as superposition), they are already inherently capable of storing all possible states of the system naturally. This means that our 50-particle system, which would require 9000 TB of disk space in a classical computer, could be simulated with 50 particles – known as qubits.

The goal is to find the full quantum state $\mid{\psi}\rangle$ of the system of interest, as well as its time dependence. The challenge of a quantum simulator is to prepare the system in some known initial state $\mid{\psi(0)}\rangle$, and then evolve it dynamically under the action of the appropriately engineered Hamiltonian, and finally to _‘_read out_’_ the state at time t by some form of measurement. The Hamiltonian describing the quantum simulator should implement a model by capturing all the important elements of the original system of interest – which may be too complex to work with directly. The measured observables from the simulator can finally be compared with measured data from the system. By engineering the Hamiltonian, properties of the system can be controlled that otherwise could not be with a real material, for example, lattice spacing, geometry, dimensionality.  A quantum simulator may be regarded as a type of quantum computer that is designed for a specific purpose – to solve the particular equations that govern another physical system in an idealised, controllable way.

## 5.2 Simulating electrons using whole atoms

There are many materials that have such a complex structure that they are difficult to investigate experimentally or even to study theoretically, as we have seen in the last section. One example is cuprate superconductors, which are made up of alternating layers of copper oxide with other oxides in between. These materials, which were first discovered in the 1980s, are still not fully understood. However, they have the potential to have a major technological impact. For this reason, researchers try to create model systems that exhibit the same important features, but are made from more easily controllable particles, such as ions, atoms or molecules. It is then possible for these particles to play the role of electrons in a crystalline lattice, or another complex solid material. For example, they can be used to study the phenomenon of ferromagnetism, whereby all the unpaired electrons in a material such as iron can align their spins in the same direction [\[7\]](#sect12).

Alkali metal atoms are commonly used for such a task. These atoms (such as lithium, sodium or rubidium) all have a single valence electron in their outer shell, with the inner shells being completely filled. As a result, the behaviour of these atoms is particularly simple to analyse, and is determined to a large extent by only this single electron. By using electromagnetic fields, this electron can be made to align or anti-align with an externally applied magnetic field, in the same way as a single electron in a solid material might behave.

A single electron is a type of particle known as a fermion, which leads to it having specific properties (such as restricting how many electrons can be in the same state, for instance). In order to simulate an electron with a whole atom, we need the atom itself to behave as a fermion. Depending on the number of neutrons in the atom's nucleus, its constituent particles (that is, the protons, neutrons and electrons) can add up to make the atom itself either a composite boson or a composite fermion. For example, the isotope lithium-6 has three protons, three neutrons and three electrons, which means that it behaves as a fermion and therefore exhibits some of the same properties as an electron in a solid.

So now we have a whole atom that exhibits many of the same features as a single electron, such as the ability to be in different spin alignment states and following the rules of fermions. The advantages are that these atoms are much easier to control, by using electromagnetic fields such as lasers and magnets, and they can be observed much more easily with cameras and microscopes. They can be cooled down efficiently to make the system cleaner, and by using laser beams to create standing wave patterns researchers can create periodic structures that are able to confine atoms to individual potential wells. This is known as an _‘_optical lattice_’_, and it provides a way to simulate electrons in a solid crystal structure using atoms in a much more tuneable, defect-free environment. The lattice structure is highly controllable, and, by changing the depth of the potential wells, particles can be allowed to move to varying degrees throughout the structure (or allowed to tunnel between neighbouring sites) to study mobility effects (as depicted in Figure [5.1](#fig5.1)).

## 5.3 Ultracold atoms as quantum simulators

Research with ultracold atoms has grown into one of the most active and rich fields in physics over the last several decades [\[8\]](#sect13). The advent of laser cooling [\[9](#sect14), [10\]](#sect15) and the magneto-optical trap [\[11\]](#sect16) in the 1980s, resulting from the availability of narrow-band tuneable lasers, provided researchers with convenient and widespread access to samples of sub-millikelvin gases. A further breakthrough came with the creation of Bose–Einstein condensates (BECs) of the dilute atomic gases rubidium, sodium and lithium in 1995 [\[12](#sect17), [13](#sect18), [14\]](#sect19). A BEC is the phenomenon whereby a macroscopic number of particles are able to occupy the same lowest quantum state, and occurs when the system is cooled below a critical temperature such that the de Broglie wavelengths of particles become of the order of their separation [\[15\]](#sect20). The entire gas can then be described by a single macroscopic wavefunction. To maintain the metastable gaseous state, and to prevent the vapour from solidifying, extremely low densities are required (around a million times less dense than air), which necessitates reaching very low temperatures of the order of hundreds of nanokelvin. In fact, a BEC actually obeys the same underlying collective equations as a superfluid, so we can already say that a BEC is a type of quantum simulator, for example, one which simulates the behaviour of superfluid liquid helium; indeed, many of the features (such as vortices) that had been observed in liquid helium were also then later replicated in BEC experiments.

Ultracold atom systems provide a remarkable degree of control over the internal and external degrees of freedom of particles using optical, magnetic and electric fields, as well as adjustment of the interaction strengths through either Feshbach resonances or the particle density. In contrast to condensed matter systems, the interactions between atoms can be described in a simple way due to the diluteness of the gas, which allows direct comparison with calculations and predictions. The versatile and precise nature of ultracold atom systems allows engineering of specific Hamiltonians in a controlled, isolated and defect-controlled environment, and provides an excellent testing ground for theoretical models over a wide parameter space. In addition, the relatively large length scales involved mean that quantum objects can be investigated directly with optical imaging. The low temperatures and weak interactions also give rise to what are often relatively slow dynamics, which can be probed on experimentally accessible timescales using rapid imaging methods.

Many early experiments with BECs focused on understanding macroscopic collective phenomena, perhaps most dramatically with the initial observation of matter-wave interference of two overlapping condensates [\[16\]](#sect21), followed by the so-called ‘atom laser’ [\[17\]](#sect22). The inherent superfluid properties of BECs were demonstrated, most notably by the appearance of vortices [\[18](#sect23), [19\]](#sect24) and vortex lattices [\[20\]](#sect25), along with dynamical effects of the macroscopic wavefunction, such as the generation of solitons [\[21](#sect26), [22\]](#sect27).

As well as the original magnetically trapped incarnations, the realisation of optically confined BECs [\[23](#sect28), [24](#sect29), [25\]](#sect30) opened the possibility of widely tuning the strength of interactions between atoms using an external magnetic field via Feshbach resonances [\[26](#sect31), [27\]](#sect32), if the atomic species is chosen appropriately. This added yet another degree of controllability for experimentalists, and eventually paved the way for studies of the BEC–BCS crossover using fermionic molecules [\[28](#sect33), [29](#sect34), [30\]](#sect35). With increasingly novel trapping geometries being explored, the potentials created by the standing waves in optical lattices [\[31](#sect36), [32\]](#sect37) allow production of periodic arrays of multiple degenerate quantum gases. Optical potentials have since become popular for use as quantum simulators for condensed matter and solid state systems, where they can be used to mimic the behaviour of electrons in the lattice potential of a solid. Work along these lines has resulted in the experimental realisation of systems described by the Bose–Hubbard Hamiltonian, accompanied by observation of the transition between the superfluid and Mott insulating phases [\[33](#sect38), [34\]](#sect39). Many condensed matter systems have been imitated using cold atoms, including studies of Anderson localisation [\[35](#sect40), [36](#sect41), [37\]](#sect42) and Josephson junctions \[[38](#sect43), [39\]](#sect44), to name a few. As well as condensed matter and solid state systems, the high coherence, low speed of sound and ultralow temperatures of BECs make them candidates for modelling more exotic phenomena, such as early-universe physics [\[40\]](#sect45), acoustic black holes and Hawking radiation [\[41](#sect46), [42](#sect47), [43\]](#sect48), as well as the dynamical Casimir effect [\[44\]](#sect49).

Traps for ultracold atoms can be created with extreme aspect ratios, and so are naturally well suited to studies of quantum many-body physics in one-dimensional and two-dimensional gases, where the behaviour can be fundamentally different. In addition to investigation of the equilibrium state of gases, the dynamics of non-equilibrium quantum many-body systems is currently an active field of study. Ultracold atoms are ideal for these types of studies, partly because their isolation from the environment effectively renders the system closed, and also because the dynamics occur on experimentally accessible timescales, essentially dictated by collision times. In fact, the absence of any sign of thermalisation was observed after perturbing a one-dimensional Tonks gas even after many thousands of collisions, due to the system's near-integrability [\[45\]](#sect50).

**Example: Superfluid to Mott-insulator transition in cold atoms**

A Mott insulator is a type of material that has been investigated since the 1930s [\[46\]](#sect51). It is a material that would be expected to be a conducting metal if you considered conventional band theory, but turns out to be an insulator due to strong interactions between its electrons. This type of system can be modelled in its simplest form by the Bose–Hubbard model. Although not yet fully understood, the Mott insulator transition is interesting because it could be applied to build, for example, transistors or switches, if the transition could be induced using some external parameter such as voltage, magnetic field, strain, etc. For example, several metal-oxide materials can be Mott insulators, such as VO $_{2}$ (vanadium oxide), which has been discussed as a potential alternative to silicon in field effect transistors (FETs) [\[47\]](#sect52).

It is difficult to study such materials directly, but in 2002 Greiner et al [\[34\]](#sect39), in Munich, demonstrated the phase transition of this material in a controlled way using a gas of ultracold atoms trapped in an optical lattice. This is a quantum phase transition, in the sense that it is driven by quantum fluctuations in the system. By making the atoms very cold, the complication of thermal fluctuations in the system is removed. The behaviour in the macroscopic phase transition is actually driven by the underlying microscopic details of the system.

Around 200,000 rubidium atoms at a temperature of 100 nK are held in an optical lattice, which is a regular lattice generated by counterpropagating laser beams that is able to trap the atoms. The lattice depth can be controlled with the power of the laser beams. Depending on the lattice depth, the particles on different sites are connected by quantum mechanical tunnelling. For low lattice depths, everything can be coupled, and the wavefunction spreads out (because of the large de Broglie wavelength). The system forms a Bose–Einstein condensate – a state of matter in which the wavefunctions of all the atoms overlap and have the same phase. Therefore, if you release the atoms from their lattice, you will see sharp interference patterns. However, as the lattice depth is slowly ramped up, the atoms distribute so that there is an equal number per well (around 2.5 on average), because they have repulsive interactions between them for this type of atom. When this happens, they cannot share the same phase anymore, and when released from the trap there is no interference. In addition, the fluctuation in the number of atoms that you find in each site is also greatly suppressed.

When dealing with normal systems that exist in three spatial dimensions, a phase transition is possible below which some long-range order can arise, such as in a ferromagnet. Another good example of this is the transition to a Bose–Einstein condensate, which exhibits long-range order and can be described by a single wavefunction. However, it has been well known for some time that this long-range order is actually prevented if the system is constrained to exist in either one or two dimensions instead. This is because thermal and quantum fluctuations now become significant enough to destroy any long-range order.

However, in the late 1970s, experiments with thin films of liquid helium showed that even though these systems were effectively two-dimensional, they were still able to exhibit a transition to an ordered state, turning into a superfluid. This behaviour is explained by the Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless (BKT) theory, which describes how long-range order can arise from the spontaneous vortices that occur naturally in two-dimensional systems. At high temperatures, there is enough energy for all the vortices to be separated, but below a certain threshold (known as the BKT transition temperature) it is more energetically favourable for the vortices to bind together into pairs through their interactions. This reduces defects in the phase and allows long-range order to prevail, with the material becoming superfluid.

Although the liquid helium experiments gave some evidence for the BKT transition by looking at macroscopic quantities, such as the superfluid flow, they were not able to actually reveal the underlying microscopic mechanism that was thought to be responsible – namely, the binding and unbinding of these vortex pairs. A spectactular demonstration finally came in 2006, by making use of ultracold rubidium atoms confined in a two-dimensional optical trap [\[49\]](#sect54). By splitting the gas into two parts, and then letting them expand and overlap, the experiment was able to detect whether the system had transitioned to a state with long-range order, because, in that case, the clouds would show interference. The temperature could be controlled precisely, allowing the BKT transition to be systematically crossed. In addition to this, sharp dislocations were seen in these interference patterns, which arose due to some free vortices, which confirms the BKT theory of vortex binding.

A more refined version of this experiment was carried out in 2021 [\[50\]](#sect55), in which the added ability to take sliced images through the trapped gas allowed the team to measure correlation functions and compare them with predictions of the theory. The 2016 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Thouless, Haldane and Kosterlitz for ‘theoretical discoveries of topological phase transitions and topological phases of matter’.

## 5.4 Trapped ions as quantum simulators

One of the most well-developed quantum simulator platforms is trapped ions, and research and technology in this field is becoming increasingly complex. Trapped ions are atoms that have been given a net electrical charge, with the number of protons not being equal to the number of electrons, and this means that they can be very efficiently trapped using a combination of electric or magnetic fields (using a Paul- or Penning-style trap). These ions interact strongly with each other via the Coulomb repulsion force (in contrast to ultracold neutral atoms, which are weakly interacting). The advent of laser cooling has meant that these trapped ions can be cooled to very low temperatures and can be, essentially, confined to fixed positions in space – remaining trapped for hours or days. The internal (electronic) and external (motional) states of the individual ions can then be manipulated by applying additional laser beams or microwave pulses. The separation of the particles can be of the order of micrometres, which allows them to be individually observed using standard charged-coupled device (CCD) cameras and optics. This type of system is illustrated schematically in Figure [5.7](#fig:PaulTrap).

Such a system can be engineered to model the behaviour of a different physical system – such as a collection of magnetic spins where the internal states of the ions map to the spin states in the real system – realising a quantum simulator platform. It could represent a simplified magnetic lattice that you might find in a solid material. For example, Porras and Zoller [\[51\]](#sect56) showed theoretically almost 20 years ago how the Hamiltonian for a one-dimensional chain of ions interacting with laser beams can map directly to be of the form of a spin–spin system (such as the Ising XY and XYZ interactions) where the spin–spin interaction is transmitted through the chain by means of collective vibrational modes from the forces due to the lasers. By changing the properties of the lasers (intensity and polarisation), quantum phase transitions can be induced.

**Example: Simulating a quantum magnet with trapped ions**

In 2008, a proof-of-principle experiment realised a trapped-ion analogue quantum simulator of a spin ½ system – a so-called quantum magnet – using two magnesium ions, and it controlled how strongly the spins interact with each other using additional laser beams [\[52\]](#sect57). The experiment first prepared the ions in a state with both individual spins aligned with the external magnetic field, with the spin–spin interaction turned off. This represents a paramagnetic state – this is when a material has unpaired electrons, and so the individual spins will align with any external field that is applied. This is illustrated in Figure [5.8](#fig:QuantumMagnet) (diagram A on the left of the figure). The spin–spin interaction is then gradually ramped up until it is much greater than the external field, at which point the interaction acts to keep the spins aligned with each other, which effectively maintains their ‘magnetisation’ regardless of the value of the field. This corresponds to a ferromagnetic state. Figure [5.8](#fig:QuantumMagnet) shows that the probability of finding the system in the ferromagnetic state increases as the interaction is ramped up.

At the bottom (labelled B), the figure represents the result of an actual simulation experiment of this phenomenon with two ions represented in a graph, and where the probability of finding the ion pair in a ferromagnetic state is represented as a function of the turnable spin interaction parameter. The probabilities for both possible ferromagnetic states gradually increase from the left (low interactions) to the right (high interactions), where it saturates at 50% for each state, as expected.

 

Although this experiment demonstrates the building blocks of the quantum phase transition from paramagnetism to ferromagnetism, in order to truly study phase transitions and the effect of magnetic order on system properties, quantum simulators need to be able to operate in the regime of large numbers of particles, so that average quantities such as material magnetisation can be studied. Over the years, various experiments have been performed with increasing numbers of ions. The experiment of Monroe in Maryland [\[53\]](#sect58) used three ions, which allowed both nearest and next-nearest neighbour interactions. In 2011, the same group varied the number of ions from N\=2 to N\=9 and observed the same transition from paramagnetic to ferromagnetic order, with the transition becoming sharper as the number of particles in the simulation increased [\[54\]](#sect59), as shown in Figure [5.9](#fig:MultipleIons), as expected when the system gets closer to the thermodynamic limit. In 2013, Islam et al [\[55\]](#sect60) observed frustration of the magnetic structure in an experiment employing 16 ions.

## 5.5 The future for quantum simulators

In the context of quantum technology, quantum simulators are still very much in the research phase, and are not yet being applied widely commercially – in contrast, for example, to quantum sensors which are already being commercialised and moved out of the lab. Nevertheless, the examples we have discussed make it clear that a variety of different systems have been successfully simulated so far in research laboratory environments, and have the potential for other applications in the future. In particular, two of the most interesting areas are in materials physics and quantum chemistry.

In materials physics, there is much interest in superconductors. Unlike regular metals, as a result of their quantum mechanical properties, superconductors have zero resistance, which means that an electrical current can flow indefinitely without power dissipation. Such a material was first discovered more than 100 years ago, with the superconducting properties appearing below a very low critical temperature of -269 $^{\circ}$ C, which required submersing the material in liquid helium. A variety of materials have been found to be superconductors since then, but cooling to low temperatures has always been required. One of the most cheaply available ways of cooling to such temperatures is using liquid nitrogen, and, as this boils at -196 $^\circ$ C, it is of interest to make materials that can turn into superconductors at temperatures greater than or equal to this (these are usually named high-temperature superconductors). In the 1980s, a type of ceramic material was found to be a high-temperature superconductor at -183 $^\circ$ C, and led to its discoverers being awarded the Nobel Prize. This was significant, because liquid nitrogen is much cheaper to produce commercially than liquid helium, in addition to having some other practical advantages. More and more superconducting materials have been discovered at gradually increasing critical temperatures (but these require being under high pressure as well). They are used today, for example, in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) machines, where the superconducting magnets are used to generate strong magnetic fields. However, the ultimate goal would be to create superconductors that could operate in everyday conditions, that is, at close to room temperature and under atmospheric pressure. This would have huge implications for technology, for example, in power-line energy distribution or in maglev train tracks. Such materials are not fully understood and are still the subject of intense research, so quantum simulators that are able to model particular systems and then predict their bulk properties could become a powerful tool towards discovering new candidate materials.

Another interesting potential future application is in the field of quantum chemistry, where full quantum simulations of a large system of particles is intractable. Experimental research so far has focused on engineering the interactions between atoms, studying phenomena such as molecule formation and collisions between particles. In contrast to atoms, molecules also have rotational and vibrational degrees of freedom, which leads to a more complex level structure. These new degrees of freedom can be manipulated, for example, using microwave fields, and a variety of spin states can then be encoded into these degrees of freedom for simulating models of quantum magnetism.

The early experiments were able to create ultra-cold diatomic homonuclear molecules (that is, having only two atoms, both being of the same species) of sodium atoms and to study the molecular energy spectrum [\[56\]](#sect61). It is more difficult to laser cool molecules, so the way in which the initial studies were carried out was to first laser cool a gas of the individual atoms down to ultra-cold temperatures, and then to use an additional laser pulse to cause the atoms to form molecules. A few years later, it was possible to create heteronuclear molecules (that is, two atoms of different species, such as potassium and rubidium [\[57\]](#sect62)). This was interesting, because these molecules can have an electric dipole moment (they are known as polar molecules) because of the imbalance of charge, which leads to the possibility of controlling long-range interactions between molecules using an electric field for simulating different types of materials. A new level of control over engineering molecule formation was demonstrated in 2018, when two individual atoms (one sodium and one caesium) were brought together using tweezers made of light (known as optical tweezers) and were made to form a single molecule by using a laser pulse [\[58\]](#sect63).

## Summary

Chemical reactions are ultimately the result of a quantum mechanical process of bonds being exchanged, and the experiments over the last 30 years have guided our understanding of how chemical processes proceed at the molecular level. Although an incredible amount of progress has been made, and the level of control is unprecedented, the field is really in its relative infancy in terms of commercial or industrial applications. The future direction is likely to be towards studying more and more complex systems, to gain a better understanding of the underlying physics and with the possibility of designing or discovering new materials, or even engineering new substances, for medical or pharmaceutical applications.
